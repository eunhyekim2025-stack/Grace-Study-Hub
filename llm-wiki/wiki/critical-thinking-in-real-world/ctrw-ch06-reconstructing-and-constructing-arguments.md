# Ch6 — Reconstructing & Constructing Arguments, Writing Evaluative Essays

> [[index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · Textbook Ch.6. Turns the diagramming + forms skills into writing. Exercises **6.1–6.2** (private answer key held for feedback).

---

## Reconstructing (understanding others)

To evaluate an argument you must first see how it's *meant* to work. Real passages hide premises, intermediate conclusions and indicators, and pad with description or asides. **Strip it to essentials and rebuild in SAF.**

- **Simple arguments:** basic premises first, conclusion last; interpret ambiguous statements charitably (pick the reading that makes the argument **deductively sound** without twisting the words).
- **Complex arguments:** treat as a **main argument + supporting sub-arguments**. Reconstruct the **main** line first, then a sub-argument for each **intermediate conclusion**. (The book works Augustine on astrology, Aquinas's efficient-cause proof, and — usefully for this course — [[case-drug-legalisation-grayling|Grayling's drug-legalisation op-ed]] itself.)
- **Guard rail:** reconstruction risks *misrepresentation* — stay sensitive to the arguer's intention; don't build a different argument than the one intended.

### The Principle of Charity — and its limits

Charity means **resolving any unclarity so the other person's argument comes out as strong as it sensibly can** — because the point is to test whether the *conclusion* is worth accepting, not to score a cheap win. More generally, it commits you to reading someone's words in the most sensible way available.

In practice, charity is what lets you **redeem** an argument that suffers any of these common **sources of "greyness"** (the list is not exhaustive — supply the missing piece the arguer plainly intended):

| Greyness | Charitable fix |
|---|---|
| inference indicator not explicit | insert the `[so]` / `[because]` the reasoning implies |
| a **premise** left unstated (implicit premise) | add the unstated premise the conclusion needs (e.g. "you'll be lonely if unmarried" behind "don't want to be lonely → should marry") |
| an **intermediate conclusion** left unstated | make the hidden middle step explicit |
| the **conclusion** itself left unstated | state the point the reasons are driving at ("bigger burgers are better + BK's are biggest → BK's are best") |
| unclear whether reasoning is **linked or convergent** | pick the structure that best fits the intent |
| unclear whether reasoning is **deductive or inductive** | read it as the stronger of the two |

**But be charitable with caution:**
1. **Don't expect charity for your *own* argument.** It is *your* job to make yours as clear as it can be.
2. **Use charity only when you can't just ask** the arguer to clarify — clarification beats guessing.
3. **Don't let your "fix" make the argument worse.** Rescuing "this cat has a bloated belly, so it's pregnant" by supplying "*all* cats with bloated bellies are pregnant" hands them an obviously false premise — that's not charity.

## Constructing (building your own)

To persuade, your reasons must be **(a) acceptable to your interlocutor** *and* **(b) actually support the conclusion**.

1. **Clarify the conclusion.** Pin its meaning and watch ambiguous words — "**should**" has a *moral* sense (ethically required) and a *prudential* sense (in your best interests); you may need to argue *both* lines.
2. **Use valid forms as scaffolding.** Reach the conclusion with **Modus Ponens**, and back its conditional premise with **Hypothetical Syllogism**, tease out an option with **Disjunctive Syllogism**, etc. ([[ctrw-ch05-forms-of-argument|Ch5]] forms.)
3. **Annotate as you build** — line references + form abbreviations. This makes the logic auditable *and* shows at a glance which claims are taken for granted (basic premises) vs derived.

### Thinking about premises (which to assume)
- **Acceptability is audience-relative** — a premise obvious to one audience needs defending to another. Start from what your interlocutor **already accepts** (plain-obvious truths, shared commitments) — but note: if the conclusion followed *immediately* from agreed premises, there'd be nothing to argue.
- You may ask them to grant a premise **"for the sake of argument"** to explore what follows.
- **Dismiss frivolous/eristic objections** — a real sceptic is open to the objection being true or false; someone firing off doubts they don't themselves believe (and don't live by) can be set aside to keep the debate moving.

## Deciding how much to claim (calibration)

Deductive **certainty is usually unavailable** on matters that matter — and a single **counterexample** makes a deductive argument *unsound*. But that needn't sink your point. **Retreat to induction:**

- **Strategy 1 — admit exceptions:** keep the thrust but concede the conclusion holds *generally, not in every case* ("retraining reduces unemployment *for many*, not all").
- **Strategy 2 — drop the certainty claim:** keep the conclusion but state it as **very likely, not 100% certain** (how science and courts actually talk — "**beyond reasonable doubt**," not "beyond *any* doubt"; "the evidence *strongly suggests*").
- **But don't over-weaken.** An argument watered down until *no one could disagree* says nothing. Qualify only as much as honesty requires, then **be ready to defend it.**

> The usual real-world goal is not "prove with 100% certainty" but "show this conclusion is the **most plausible**" — enough to act on.

## Writing the evaluative essay

An essay shouldn't read like a diagram. **Translate the technique into plain English:**

| Technical | Plain English |
|---|---|
| deductively valid / invalid | "this conclusion follows / does not follow…" |
| inductively strong / weak | "strongly / weakly supported…", "very likely / not likely given the evidence" |
| sound | "conclusively proven by…" |
| valid but unsound | "follows, but only from reasons that are false" |
| cogent / strong-but-uncogent | "the evidence makes this very likely" / "new information makes it less well supported" |
| objecting to an inference | "objecting that this doesn't follow / that the evidence is less conclusive because…" |

Then write the evaluation as prose: reconstruct the author charitably, state which inferences hold and which premises are doubtful, and reach a measured verdict. (The book models this on Grayling and on Peter Singer's *Solution to World Poverty*.)

## Key takeaways
- **SAF** = basic premises first, conclusion last, each derivation annotated with its source lines + form; **VSAF** = all-valid structure, so only truth remains to judge.
- Reconstruction is **charitable interpretation**, not rearranging — and only for unclear arguments.
- Construct with **valid forms as scaffolding**, from premises your **audience accepts**; watch the moral vs prudential "should."
- **Calibrate the claim**: soften a broken deductive argument into a strong inductive one (admit exceptions *or* drop certainty) — but never empty it of content.
