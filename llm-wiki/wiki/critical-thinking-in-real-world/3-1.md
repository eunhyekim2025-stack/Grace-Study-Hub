> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Definition disputes arise from (a) case‑fit uncertainty and (b) disagreement over the definition itself.  
> - A good argument needs **valid inference** *and* **acceptable premises**; the conclusion’s truth is irrelevant for justification.  
> - Inference strength is judged on a scale (maximal → very strong → moderate → weak) and reflects how much you’d be willing to bet on the conclusion.  

## 1. Types of definition‑related disagreements
- **Case‑fit uncertainty**: the definition is accepted, but it’s unclear if the facts satisfy it (e.g., does Gaza meet the UN genocide definition?).
- **Definition‑fit disagreement**: parties dispute the meaning of the term itself (e.g., who counts as “British”; is a hot‑dog a sandwich?).
- Disputes often hinge on **breadth vs. narrowness** of criteria—what counts as necessary or sufficient.
- Real‑world stakes: WBA’s definition of “woman” decides boxing eligibility; legal policy hinges on whether substances are treated consistently.

## 2. Charitable reconstruction & argument mapping
> [!info] **Charitable reconstruction** – adding implicit premises the author assumes, marked as not directly quoted, to present the argument in its strongest plausible form.  
- Identify **inference indicators** (because, therefore, so) to locate logical support.
- **Arrow rule**: draw an arrow only when a premise **explicitly** supports a conclusion.
- Do **not** insert unsupported intermediate statements; if a link is missing, no arrow is drawn.
- Structured texts (editorials) contain more indicators than casual conversation, making mapping easier.

> [!example] *Calvin & Hobbes*: Calvin claims “ignorance is bliss” with reasons (a) knowing isn’t fun, (b) not knowing is fun. Reconstruct to test whether the reasons actually support the conclusion.

## 3. Evaluating arguments
> [!info] **Good argument** – provides a reasonable justification: a valid (or sufficiently strong) inference plus premises that are true or plausibly true.  
- **Reasonable listener duties**: (1) accept the conclusion if the argument is good; (2) point out the flaw if rejecting the conclusion.
- **Failure points**  
  1. **Inference (arrow)** – invalid logical link.  
  2. **Premise** – false, unsupported, or implausible.  
  3. **Conclusion** – false; makes the argument unsound but not automatically “bad” if inference/premises are solid.
- **Four evaluation questions**  
  1. Is the inference acceptable?  
  2. Are the premises acceptable?  
  3. Is the overall argument acceptable? (fail if 1 or 2 fails)  
  4. What is the status of the conclusion? (true/false/unknown)

> [!example] Bad‑premise, valid inference: “Some men love whiskey; I love whiskey; therefore some men love whiskey.” (Premise true, inference valid → good).  
> [!example] Invalid inference, true premise: “If an expert says X, then X is true.” (Inference invalid → bad).

## 4. Inference strength
> [!info] **Inference strength** – a comparative measure of how strongly premises support a conclusion, assessed assuming premises are true.  
- **Maximal strength**: premises logically entail the conclusion (guaranteed).  
- **Very strong**: long‑standing regularities (e.g., Earth’s rotation).  
- **Moderate**: statistical/general knowledge (e.g., most dogs die before 20).  
- **Weak**: loose associations (e.g., basketball player’s shirt size).  
- Strength is **independent of premise truth**; we evaluate the link first, then consider premise verification separately.
- **Betting analogy**: the more you’d wager on a conclusion, the stronger the inference. Low confidence → don’t bet or demand compensation.
- Adding more evidence can raise strength but cannot exceed the logical limits of the underlying relation.

## 5. Consistency & policy argument example
- **Consistency principle**: similar items should be regulated alike unless a **significant difference** (e.g., health risk) justifies distinct treatment.
- Drug‑policy case: four statements about a substance (pleasure, stress relief, recreation, health danger).  
  - Either treat them as separate premises or link them; both are permissible.  
  - The argument’s force rests on showing current policies are **inconsistent**; if so, legalizing the substance may be the remedy.
- The claim “making currently legal drugs illegal is not the preferable option” illustrates the inconsistency argument.

## 6. Practical tips for argument diagrams
- Only draw arrows for explicit support.  
- Do not treat the diagram as a chronological sequence.  
- “So” can be an influence indicator, but in conditionals it may not signal causation—interpret in context.  
- Begin with the speaker’s intended meaning; avoid imposing your own conclusions.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Charitable reconstruction | Adding assumed premises to present the strongest plausible argument. |
| Good argument | One with a valid (or strong) inference and acceptable premises. |
| Inference strength | Comparative assessment of how well premises support a conclusion (maximal → weak). |
| Consistency principle | Treat similar cases alike unless a substantive difference exists. |
| Reasonable listener | Person obligated to accept a conclusion if the argument is good, or to point out flaws otherwise. |
