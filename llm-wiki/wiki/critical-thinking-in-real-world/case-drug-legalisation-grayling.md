# Real-World Case — "Why a High Society Is a Free Society" (Grayling)

> [[critical-thinking-in-real-world/index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · **Source:** A. C. Grayling, *"Why a high society is a free society"*, The Observer, 19 May 2002. Grayling is a philosopher; the piece is a civil-liberties op-ed, not reportage.

---

## The article in one breath

**Thesis (conclusion):** Currently-illegal recreational drugs — heroin, cocaine, marijuana, ecstasy — should be **legal and regulated the same way as alcohol and nicotine**.

Grayling is careful to note he takes none of them and thinks dependence on mind-altering substances is *inimical to a good life*. His case is therefore **not** "drugs are good" but "**a free society respects the autonomy** to use them." That separation — approving of *the freedom* while disapproving of *the act* — is itself a critical-thinking move worth naming: he refuses to let his personal distaste do the argumentative work.

---

## Step 1 — Reconstruct the argument

Buried in the prose is a clean structure. Pulled apart:

| # | Premise | Type |
|---|---------|------|
| P1 | A good society only polices behaviour that is *intrinsically damaging to the social fabric and harms unwilling third parties* (rape, murder, theft, riot, fraud). Purely self-regarding acts are the individual's own. | **Normative** (Mill's *harm principle*) |
| P2 | There is *no logical difference* between legal drugs (alcohol, nicotine) and illegal ones: both are used for pleasure / stress relief / "holidaying," both are dangerous to health in degrees. | Descriptive + conceptual |
| P3 | Consistency then forces **one of two options**: criminalise alcohol & nicotine too, *or* legalise the rest under the same regime. | Logical (consistency) |
| P4 | Prohibition (1920s USA) shows banning doesn't stop use — it *creates a criminal industry*; repeal didn't cause a drinking frenzy but left an enhanced crime problem. | **Descriptive** (empirical) |
| P5 | The illegal trade funds organised crime, laundering, terrorism, gun-running, trafficking, judicial corruption — which destabilise society *far more* than private use. Legal pharmacy supply removes that platform and much petty crime. | Descriptive (consequences) |
| P6 | Treating addiction as *medical, not criminal* (safe prescription supply) drops crime "dramatically" (cites police chiefs). | Descriptive (consequences) |
| P7 | "Paracetamol is more dangerous than heroin" — a double dose of paracetamol is dangerous; a double medical dose of diamorphine causes only sleepiness. | Descriptive (safety) |
| P8 | Until a century ago these substances were legal and freely used (opium as laudanum); society as a whole was not harmed and *benefited* from not burdening itself with paternalism. | Descriptive (historical) |
| **C** | **∴ Legalise and regulate recreational drugs like alcohol/nicotine.** | Normative conclusion |

Notice the shape: **P1 supplies the value standard**, P2–P3 apply it by consistency, and **P4–P8 argue that the consequences also favour legalisation** — so the piece is a hybrid of a *principled* and a *consequentialist* argument. That matters: an opponent must defeat *both* legs, not one.

---

## Step 2 — Classify the claims (attack them differently)

- **Normative claims** (P1, and the value in C) are contested by **counter-values or inconsistency**, not by data. You can't "disprove" the harm principle — you can reject its weighting (a paternalist thinks preventing self-harm *does* justify coercion) or show Grayling applies it inconsistently.
- **Descriptive claims** (P4–P8) are contested by **evidence**: are they true, well-sourced, representative? A 2002 op-ed with no citations gives you *hypotheses to verify*, not settled facts.

> Separating these is the single most useful habit: many "debates" are really one side arguing facts and the other arguing values, past each other.

---

## Step 3 — Where a critical thinker pushes back

| Target | The move Grayling makes | The probe / possible fallacy |
|--------|------------------------|------------------------------|
| **P1 — "principally concern only themselves"** | Classifies drug use as *self-regarding*. | This is the **load-bearing premise**. Is it true? Addiction reaches families, employers, healthcare cost, impaired driving (a real third-party harm). If use *isn't* purely self-regarding, P1 no longer licenses it. **Contestable classification.** |
| **P2 — alcohol/nicotine ↔ heroin/cocaine** | Argument from **analogy / parity** ("no logical difference"). | **Disanalogy objection:** are they alike in the *relevant* respect? Overdose lethality, addictiveness, speed of dependence differ. "Dangerous in degrees" may hide a morally-relevant difference *of* degree. |
| **P3 — "either ban alcohol *or* legalise all"** | Presents two options. | **False dilemma** — it erases the middle: decriminalisation, medical-only access, graded scheduling, harm-reduction. A spectrum is framed as a binary. |
| **P4 — Prohibition** | Historical precedent. | **Disanalogy + selection:** alcohol was culturally embedded pre-Prohibition in a way some drugs are not; one case is cited, chosen because it fits. |
| **P5–P6 — crime falls** | Appeal to consequences + authority. | Consequences **cut both ways** — an opponent argues legal access raises first-time use among the young. "Certain police chiefs," "poor research": **vague appeals to authority**; who, which studies? |
| **P7 — paracetamol vs heroin** | Striking comparison. | **One-dimensional:** true only for *acute overdose toxicity*. It ignores addiction, chronic harm, impurity, social cost — the dimensions that actually drive the policy. A rhetorical shock used to unsettle an intuition. |
| **P8 — "society was not harmed"** | Historical generalisation. | **Hasty generalisation / unfalsifiable:** "as a whole" is doing a lot of work; hard to check, easy to assert. |

**The descriptive→normative bridge.** Even if *every* fact (P4–P8) held, the jump to "therefore a good society *ought* to legalise" still rests on accepting P1's weighting of autonomy as overriding. Someone with a communitarian or paternalist value set reaches the opposite conclusion **from the same facts** — proof that the real disagreement lives in the normative premise.

## Seminar 4's version — the reconstruction the class actually diagrams

The reconstruction above follows the op-ed's own order. Seminar 4 uses a **tighter eight-statement
version**, and it is the one to know, because the exercise asks you to aim attacks at *numbered*
components:

| # | Statement | Role |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | Currently legal and currently illegal drugs (nicotine, ganja) are used for the same purposes and pose similar health risks | supports (2) |
| (2) | There is **no important difference** between currently legal and currently illegal drugs | premise |
| (3) | If there is no important difference, they should be treated identically in law | premise |
| (4) | **Either** legalise the currently illegal drugs **or** criminalise the currently legal ones | from (2) + (3) |
| (5) | In a liberal society we should not police private behaviour unless it damages the social fabric or harms unwilling third parties | premise (*the harm principle*) |
| (6) | Use of currently legal drugs does not damage the social fabric or harm unwilling third parties | premise |
| (7) | We should not (in a liberal society) criminalise the currently legal drugs | from (5) + (6) |
| **(8)** | **∴ We should legalise the currently illegal drugs** | conclusion, from (4) + (7) |

The shape is a **disjunctive syllogism**: (4) offers two options, (7) eliminates one, (8) is what is
left. That is why (4) is the load-bearing step — see the *false dilemma* probe in Step 3 above.

### The attack drill (slide 13) — what does each objection hit?

> [!example] "However, Grayling…"
> Each numbered objection below is aimed at a *specific* component. Test each with
> [[ctrw-ch04-diagramming-reasons-for-and-against#Which target is under attack? — the probability test|the probability test]]:
> does it lower the target's likelihood on its own, or only drain the support the premises give?

| Objection | Most charitable target | Why |
|---|---|---|
| (9) Parking on a double yellow line harms no one, yet is rightly criminalised | **premise (5)** | A counterexample to the harm principle as stated: here is lawful coercion with no social-fabric damage and no unwilling third party |
| (10) Second-hand smoke is a significant health hazard | **premise (6)** | Directly denies that a *currently legal* drug leaves third parties unharmed |
| (11) Unlike nicotine, ecstasy damages nerve cells | **premise (1)**, hence (2) | Denies the "similar health risks" parity that (2) rests on — a **disanalogy** objection |
| (12) Legalising ecstasy triggers a conservative backlash and loses the election **∴** (13) we should not legalise ecstasy | **the conclusion (8)** | A two-step attack: (12) *supports* (13), and (13) contradicts (8). Note it grants every premise and argues from a different value entirely |
| (14) Why must all societies be liberal? | **premise (5)** | Attacks the antecedent of the harm principle, not its content — if a society need not be liberal, (5) does not bind it |
| (15) Even liberal societies now criminalise not wearing a mask | **fails as an attack on (5)** | Not wearing a mask in a pandemic *does* harm unwilling third parties, so the example is consistent with (5). By [[ctrw-ch04-diagramming-reasons-for-and-against#Charity applies to attacks too|charity rule 3]], a would-be attack that fails on every reading is better treated as a separate observation than forced into the diagram |

> [!tip] Two lessons the drill is really teaching
> **(a)** An attack can be *supported* — (12)→(13) is an argument inside the attack, so the dashed
> arrow into (8) has a solid arrow feeding it. **(b)** Attacks on (5) and (6) are far more damaging
> than attacks on (1): (5) and (6) carry the whole right-hand branch, whereas (1) is one step of
> support for (2). **Where an objection lands matters more than how clever it is.**

> [!uncertain] These are worked answers, not the marked key
> The slide poses this as an open question. The targets above are the most charitable readings; (14)
> and (15) in particular are argued over in class. Check them against what was said before relying
> on them.

## Step 4 — Steelman both sides, then judge

- **Steelman Grayling (charity first):** apply *one* principle consistently; use real history rather than assertion; honestly separate his own distaste from the argument; target the genuinely large harm of a criminalised black market. This is a *good* argument, not a lazy one.
- **Steelman the opponent:** drug use is *not* purely self-regarding (third-party and public-goods harms); the alcohol analogy breaks on addictiveness/lethality; legal availability plausibly increases uptake; and the state may justly protect people from catastrophic, autonomy-destroying self-harm (addiction erodes the very autonomy P1 prizes — a self-undercutting move on P1).
- **Verdict is *your* job.** The exercise doesn't hand you a winner; it hands you a **map of exactly where the argument is strong (its consistency) and where it is exposed (the self-regarding classification, the parity analogy, the false dilemma).** Judge by weighing those, not by your prior on drugs.

## How to apply this to *this class* — the transferable toolkit

Every step above generalises to **any** real-world op-ed, ad, policy memo, or viral post. The playbook:

1. **Reconstruct** — find the *one* conclusion, list the premises, ignore the rhetoric. If you can't write it as premises → conclusion, you don't yet understand it.
2. **Classify each claim** — descriptive (verify with evidence) vs normative (contest with counter-values / consistency). Most talking-past happens here.
3. **Find the load-bearing premise** — the one that, if it falls, collapses the whole thing (here: "drug use concerns only the user"). Spend your scrutiny there, not on the easy targets.
4. **Test every analogy** — ask the *disanalogy* question: alike in the **relevant** respect? (alcohol↔heroin, Prohibition↔drug war).
5. **Name the moves & fallacies** — false dilemma, weak analogy, hasty generalisation, appeal to vague authority, one-dimensional comparison. Naming ≠ dismissing: a named move still needs weighing.
6. **Apply the principle of charity** — build the *strongest* version of the argument *and* its opposite before you decide. Beating a strawman proves nothing.
7. **Interrogate the evidence** — dates, citations, cherry-picking, base rates. An uncited 2002 op-ed's facts are hypotheses.
8. **Separate rhetoric from logic** — the memorable lines ("Paracetamol is more dangerous than heroin," "a high society is a free society") are *persuasion*; isolate whether the underlying inference actually holds.

> Run any real-world text through steps 1→8 and you convert a *feeling* about it ("this sounds convincing / manipulative") into a *reasoned judgement* you can defend — which is the whole point of Critical Thinking in the Real World.

---

## Key takeaways

- A strong argument can still be **vulnerable at one hinge** — locate the load-bearing premise before critiquing anything else.
- **Descriptive vs normative** is the master distinction: you defeat facts with evidence and values with consistency/counter-values, never the reverse.
- **Analogy = invitation to disanalogy**; **"either/or" = suspect a false dilemma**; a striking comparison usually hides a *chosen dimension*.
- **Charity first, verdict last:** steelman both sides, then judge — the map matters more than the destination.
