> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- AI learns by detecting patterns (associative) and by human‑guided feedback (reinforcement).  
- Inference = inductive generalization → statistical syllogism; strength depends on sample size, representativeness, and feedback quality.  
- Biases arise from data, trainers, and the automatically chosen reference class.  
- Abductive reasoning selects the *best* explanation, judged by simplicity, coherence, and predictive power.  
- Test abductive claims by attacking the observation, the “best‑explanation” claim, or the influence of the chosen hypothesis.

## AI Learning & Inference

- **Learning stage**  
  - **Associative learning** – scans massive corpora, extracts co‑occurrence statistics (Pavlovian‑like).  
  - **Reinforcement learning** – human trainers reward/punish responses, steering the model toward desirable outputs.  

> [!info] **Associative learning** – pattern detection from raw text without explicit labels.  

> [!info] **Reinforcement learning** – supervised fine‑tuning using human feedback signals.

- **Inference stage**  
  - Model predicts the most probable continuation given the prompt.  
  - Operates as an **inductive generalization** followed by a **statistical syllogism**.  

> [!info] **Inductive generalization** – “most Xs have property Y” inferred from observed cases.  

> [!info] **Statistical syllogism** – premises: (1) “X usually → Y”; (2) “Current case matches X”; conclusion: “Y likely”.

- **Strength factors**  
  1. Training sample size  
  2. Representativeness of the sample  
  3. Quality & neutrality of reinforcement  

## Biases & Reference‑Class Problem

> [!warning] **Hasty/generalization** – niche topics with few examples yield unreliable answers.  

- **Data bias** – corpus may encode societal stereotypes (e.g., studies linking dialects to intelligence).  
- **Trainer bias** – evaluators may favor certain phrasing or viewpoints.  
- **Reference‑class problem** – model auto‑selects a set of texts as context; changing the implicit class (e.g., “sky at noon” vs. “sky at dusk”) changes the answer.  

> [!example]  
Prompt: “Why is the American Dream true?” → assumes truth, model answers affirmatively.  
Re‑phrase neutrally: “What arguments support or challenge the American Dream?” → yields balanced view.

## Abductive Reasoning (Inference to the Best Explanation)

- **Form**: Observation → claim that *E* is the best explanation.  
- **Steps**  
  1. Identify the observation.  
  2. Generate competing explanations.  
  3. Eliminate those inconsistent with data.  
  4. Argue why the remaining explanation best fits (simplicity, coherence, predictive power).  

> [!info] **Abduction** – reasoning that selects the hypothesis that best accounts for the facts, not a deductive certainty.  

- **Common attack**: challenge the “best‑explanation” claim by offering a stronger alternative.  

> [!example]  
Phosphine on Venus:  
- Observation: spectral detection of phosphine.  
- Best explanation (initial): microbial life.  
- Alternatives rejected: photochemical, geological processes.  
- Critique: “Life may be unlikely due to acid clouds” (questions influence, not observation).

## Evaluating Explanations

| Criterion | What it tests | Typical question |
|-----------|---------------|------------------|
| **Simplicity** | Fewest assumptions, minimal ad‑hoc terms | Does this hypothesis require extra, unsupported mechanisms? |
| **Coherence** | Compatibility with existing knowledge | Does it fit the broader scientific or everyday framework? |
| **Predictive power** | Ability to generate testable predictions | What new observations would this hypothesis correctly anticipate? |

- Use **simplicity as tie‑breaker** when coherence and predictive power are equal.  
- Discard any explanation that fails any one criterion.

## Practical Tips for Using AI

- Verify that your topic is well‑represented in the training data.  
- Look for hidden premises in the prompt; re‑phrase to stay neutral.  
- Anticipate bias sources (data, trainer, reference class) and adjust expectations.  
- When evaluating AI‑generated answers, apply abductive criteria: is the explanation simple, coherent, and predictive?  

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Associative learning | Pattern detection from large text corpora |
| Reinforcement learning | Human‑guided feedback fine‑tuning |
| Inductive generalization | General rule inferred from observed cases |
| Statistical syllogism | Probabilistic inference from known frequencies |
| Abduction | Inference to the best explanation |
| Reference‑class problem | Ambiguity in which set of past cases the model uses |
| Simplicity (abduction) | Preference for explanations with fewest assumptions |
| Coherence | Consistency with existing knowledge |
| Predictive power | Capacity to forecast new observations |
