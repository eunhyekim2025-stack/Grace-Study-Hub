# Ch3 — Evaluating Arguments

> [[index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · Textbook Ch.3. Takes the map from [[ctrw-ch02-diagramming-reasoning|Ch2]] and judges it. Exercises **3.1–3.4** (private answer key held for feedback).

## First, complete the argument

Before judging, supply what's left unsaid ([[ctrw-ch01-basic-concepts-of-reasoning|Ch1]] Principle of Charity):

- **Missing premises** — add when the arguer *needs* it and *would agree*. Mostly a **deductive** concern (in induction, *all* relevant background info would count as "missing," so the book restricts it to deduction). An **obvious** gap ("cocaine is a narcotic") can be filled silently; a **controversial** one ("abortion is murder") must be **stated openly** so it can be challenged.
- **Missing conclusion** — sometimes "left hanging" ("The Oracle said Athens will win; the Oracle said so" ⇒ *[Athens will win]*).
- **Deep assumptions** — not gaps in support, but the **unstated commitments/context** motivating the argument (is the arguer a Christian? a utilitarian?). Uncovering them lets you "see where they're coming from" and is the essence of thinking *outside the box* — and it opens a deeper line of evaluation: *should rejecting the background lead me to reject the argument?*

## Q1–Q2: Judging truth

There is **no single test for truth** — the test must fit the *kind* of claim:

| Kind of claim | Appropriate test |
|---|---|
| Empirical ("Mark's socks are orange") | observe / check the world |
| Definitional ("'freedom' ≠ 'responsibility'") | consult a dictionary / meaning |
| Mathematical ("every even > 2 is a sum of two primes") | produce a proof |
| Reflective ("honesty is the best policy") | reason through the implications |

- **Certainty comes in degrees and from many sources** — we accept claims because we've never questioned them, because they're plain obvious, because we trust the source, because we have good reasons, because they *couldn't be otherwise* ("no married spinsters"), etc.
- **Possibility ≠ probability.** Everything probable is possible, but not everything possible is probable. Don't slide from "it *could* happen" to "it's *likely*."
- Assess truth by **comparing the claim with general knowledge** and by inspecting the **meaning/structure** of its terms.

## Q3–Q4: The spectrum of support

Strength runs from **no support → valid**, judged *as if the reasons were true*:

| Degree | What it means | Rough % (heuristic only) |
|---|---|---|
| **None** | premises **irrelevant** — a *non sequitur* ("Pope wears red socks → he's Italian") | 0% |
| **Weak** | relevant but **defective/suspect**; not more likely true than false | 0–50% |
| **Moderately strong** | true premises make the conclusion **more likely than not** | ~50–80% |
| **Strong** | true premises make it **very likely** but not certain | ~80–99% |
| **Valid** | true premises give **100%, conclusive** support | 100% |

> ⚠ The percentages are **rough thumbnails, not measurements**. Real judgement is *contextual*: crossing a road safely demands ~100% even though 80% already counts as "strong." **Context, risk, and how much the outcome matters to you** all shift the bar (an 80%-chance-of-hard-skin operation vs a 40%-chance-of-dying one).

### Why the focus on validity pays off
- **Saves work** — you can attack *either* the truth of the reasons *or* the inference ("even if your reasons were true, they don't oblige me").
- **Double criticism** — show the reasons are false *and* that they wouldn't support the conclusion anyway.
- **Spots false reasons** — if the conclusion is false but the argument is valid, **at least one premise must be false** (how Einstein overturned a Newton premise). A broken machine with a correct design has a faulty part.
- Validity is also **required for proof** and lets you **establish what's possible/impossible**.

### Two clean inductive forms (measurable-ish)
- **Statistical syllogism:** *n% of Fs are Gs; X is an F; so X is a G.* Strength tracks *n* (n = 100 → valid; n = 0 → no support).
- **Inductive generalisation:** *n% of a representative sample of S is F; so ≈ n% of S is F.* Needs a **random, large-enough, unbiased** sample; carries a **margin of error** ("probably about"). Vaguer than a statistical syllogism — and *more precise conclusions are actually weaker* (see [[ctrw-ch07-fallacies|Ch7]] small/biased sample).

### Problems with the percentage heuristic
Vagueness of "strong"/"very probable"; **different evaluators hold different standards**; **new background information** changes an inference's reliability (learning the off-wine bottle came from a 1%-off shipment); and *significance* (how much the truth matters) differs from *probability*.

## A note on valid ≠ true
A **valid** argument can have **false premises and even a false conclusion** — validity only guarantees that *if* the premises were true the conclusion *must* be. "Pavarotti was a ballet dancer; all ballet dancers are fit; so Pavarotti was fit" is **valid** yet has false premises. Only a **sound** argument (valid + all premises actually true, e.g. "All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; so Socrates is mortal") delivers a guaranteed-true conclusion.

## Key takeaways
- **Complete the argument first** (missing premises/conclusion/deep assumptions), *then* evaluate.
- **Truth of reasons ⟂ strength of support** — separate checks, separate fixes; pick the right *truth test* for the claim's kind.
- Support is a **spectrum** (none → weak → moderately strong → strong → valid); percentages are heuristics, **context sets the real bar**.
- **Validity is leverage**: it saves work, enables double criticism, and lets a false conclusion prove a premise false.
