> - Political “free rider” = voter who backs opposition while preferring PAP rule (Lee); Singh argues opposition voters are not free riders.
> - Valid deductive arguments require correct logical **form** (e.g., modus ponens, modus tollens); truth of premises is a separate issue.

## 1. Political free‑rider debate

> [!info] **Definition – Political free rider (Lee)**
> A voter who supports the opposition party **but** still wants the People’s Action Party (PAP) to stay in power.

> [!info] **Definition – Opposition voter (Singh)**
> A voter who chooses the opposition for substantive, positive reasons, not merely to let the ruling party win.

- **Lee’s claim**: Workers Party encourages free‑riding; if many free‑ride, the electoral system collapses.
- **Singh’s rebuttal**:
  - Residents of Aljunied/Sengkang would reject the “free‑rider” label.
  - Opposition MPs run town councils (originally created by Lee Kuan Yew) → prove governance competence.
  - First‑term opposition teams face challenges, yet voters trust them; elected opposition MPs have real influence, unlike NCMPs.
  - Therefore, opposition voters **are not** free riders.

> [!warning] **Vague argumentation**
> Both sides present unclear inference markers, making it hard to pinpoint the exact point of attack.

> [!example] **Reconstruction of Lee’s (flawed) argument**
> 1. Voters support opposition because they see it as competent.  
> 2. An opposition presence benefits Singapore.  
> 3. Therefore, labeling those voters “free riders” is inaccurate.

> [!callout] **Core dispute** – Whether the Workers Party’s encouragement of free‑riding is justified hinges on the correct definition of “free rider” in a political context.

## 2. Deductive validity basics

> [!info] **Soundness vs. validity**
> - **Valid**: conclusion follows logically from premises (form‑only).  
> - **Sound**: valid **and** all premises are true.

### 2.1 Logical operators (truth‑value rules)

| Operator | Symbol | Truth‑value rule |
|----------|--------|------------------|
| Negation | ¬P | opposite of P |
| Conjunction | P ∧ Q | true **only** if both P and Q are true |
| Disjunction (inclusive) | P ∨ Q | true if **at least one** of P, Q is true (both true also true) |
| Conditional (material implication) | P → Q | false **only** when P true **and** Q false; otherwise true |
| Exclusive disjunction* | P ⊕ Q | true **only** when exactly one of P, Q is true |

*Use exclusive reading **only** when the disjuncts cannot both be true (mutually exclusive).

> [!warning] **Common mistake** – treating “or” as exclusive; leads to invalid inferences.

### 2.2 Key inference forms

| Form | Symbolic pattern | Valid? | Typical name |
|------|------------------|--------|--------------|
| Modus ponens | P → Q, P ⊢ Q | ✅ | affirming the antecedent |
| Modus tollens | P → Q, ¬Q ⊢ ¬P | ✅ | denying the consequent |
| Denying the antecedent | P → Q, ¬P ⊢ ¬Q | ❌ | formal fallacy |
| Affirimng the consequent | P → Q, Q ⊢ P | ❌ | formal fallacy (often mis‑labelled “valid”) |

> [!example] **Modus ponens**
> 1. If he lives in Bangkok, he lives in Thailand.  
> 2. He lives in Bangkok.  
> 3. ∴ He lives in Thailand. – **Valid** (soundness depends on premise truth).

> [!example] **Denying the antecedent (invalid)**
> 1. If he lives in Bangkok, he lives in Thailand.  
> 2. He does **not** live in Bangkok.  
> 3. ∴ He does **not** live in Thailand. – **Invalid**, regardless of factual accuracy.

> [!example] **Affirming the consequent (invalid)**
> 1. If it rains, the streets are wet.  
> 2. The streets are wet.  
> 3. ∴ It rains. – **Invalid** (the streets could be wet for other reasons).

> [!example] **Modus tollens**
> 1. If God exists, there would be no evil.  
> 2. Evil exists.  
> 3. ∴ God does not exist. – **Valid** (soundness depends on premise truth).

### 2.3 Testing validity without full truth tables

- Identify the **form** of the argument.  
- If the form matches a known valid pattern (modus ponens, modus tollens), the argument is valid.  
- If it matches a known fallacy (denying the antecedent, affirming the consequent), it is invalid **irrespective** of content.

> [!callout] **Rule** – *Validity is determined solely by logical form; content matters only for soundness.*

### 2.4 Disjunction interpretation principle

> [!info] **Principle of charity for “or”**
> Prefer the **inclusive** reading (makes the statement easier to be true) unless contextual evidence shows the disjuncts are mutually exclusive.

- Example where exclusive reading is forced: “Woman is married to either Emma or Gabby in Singapore” (law prohibits polygamy).  
- Example where inclusive reading is appropriate: “Woman is dating either Emma or Gabby” (both can be true).

## 3. Refuting arguments

1. **Attack the premises** – show a premise is false or unsupported.  
2. **Expose an invalid form** – demonstrate the argument follows a known fallacy.

> [!example] **God‑hiddenness argument (invalid)**
> 1. If God exists, He shows Himself. (unsupported premise)  
> 2. God does not show Himself.  
> 3. ∴ God does not exist. – **Invalid** (denying the antecedent).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Free rider (political)** | Voter who backs opposition while preferring the ruling party to stay in power (Lee’s definition). |
| **Valid argument** | Logical form guarantees the conclusion if premises are true. |
| **Sound argument** | Valid + all premises are true. |
| **Modus ponens** | If P→Q and P, then Q (valid). |
| **Modus tollens** | If P→Q and ¬Q, then ¬P (valid). |
| **Denying the antecedent** | If P→Q and ¬P, then ¬Q (invalid). |
| **Affirming the consequent** | If P→Q and Q, then P (invalid). |
| **Inclusive disjunction** | “P or Q” true when at least one is true (both allowed). |
| **Exclusive disjunction** | “P or Q” true only when exactly one is true (mutually exclusive). |
