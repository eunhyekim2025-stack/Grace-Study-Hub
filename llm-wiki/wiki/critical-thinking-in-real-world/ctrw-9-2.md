> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Use authority only when expertise matches the factual claim.  
> - Distinguish primary (research‑producing) from secondary (interpretive) experts.  
> - Moral, normative, or personal judgments lie outside the realm of expertise.  
> - Evaluate authority by credentials, peer‑reviewed output, and conflicts of interest.  
> - Analogical arguments are stronger with many relevant similarities and no relevant differences.

## Appeal to Authority

### Roles
- **Acquisition** – lets us learn facts we cannot observe personally.  
- **Support** – supplies justification for premises lacking direct evidence.  
- **Challenge** – lets us refute an opponent’s unsupported claim by citing a more reliable source.

### Correct use criteria
> [!info] An appeal to authority is valid only if the authority:
> - Is an expert **in the relevant domain**.  
> - Is backed by **evidence or consensus** within that field.  
> - Provides **explainable reasons**, not just “I’m an expert.”

### What can be attacked
| Target | Typical attack |
|--------|----------------|
| Expert status | Lack of relevant training/experience |
| Relevance of field | Domain mismatch (e.g., psychiatry vs nutrition) |
| General “experts are usually correct” | Unreasonable; undermines concept of expertise |

### Domains where expertise does **not** apply
> [!warning] Authority cannot settle:
> - **Moral/Normative** claims (e.g., “adultery is wrong”).  
> - **Evaluative** judgments (e.g., “immigration is best for Singapore”).  
> - **Private personal matters** (only the individual or close confidant).

### Types of expertise
> [!info]  
> **Primary expert** – conducts original research, publishes peer‑reviewed work.  
> **Secondary expert** – synthesizes literature, lacks primary data (e.g., a psychiatrist who “read everything” on nutrition).  
> **AI (e.g., ChatGPT)** – secondary source; can summarize but not generate evidence.

### Indicators of genuine expertise
- Peer‑reviewed publications in recognized journals.  
- Invitations to review/edit or other peer recognition.  
- Ability to cite reputable sources on demand.

### Evaluating an appeal
> [!example]  
> 1. Verify credentials and primary contributions.  
> 2. Distinguish strong (primary, peer‑validated) from weak (secondary, no publications).  
> 3. Check for conflicts of interest or vested biases.

### Publication bias & consensus
- Journals favor statistically significant results → “pre‑bias” toward false positives.  
- Consensus is informative but not infallible (e.g., tobacco industry denial, climate‑change shift).  
- Lack of consensus may signal unresolved evidence.

## Analogical Reasoning

### Standard pattern
1. **Shared relevant properties** a, b, c between X and Y.  
2. X also has property d.  
3. **Conclusion**: Y likely has property d.

### Strength factors
> [!info] An analogy is stronger when:
> - Many **relevant** similarities are listed.  
> - No **relevant dissimilarities** exist.  
> - The similarity concerns the property under debate.

### Types of analogical inference
| Type | Logic | Typical use |
|------|-------|-------------|
| Deductive | Conclusion follows necessarily (rare) | Formal proofs |
| Inductive | Conclusion is probable | Moral or legal analogies |

### Attacking an analogy
> [!warning] Point out a **relevant dissimilarity** that undermines the premise that X and Y are alike. The logical form may be valid, but a false premise defeats the argument.

### Examples
- **Moral**: “If eating dogs is wrong and dogs share no morally relevant difference with other mammals, then eating any mammal is wrong.” (Inductive analogy).  
- **Legal**: Precedent applies when no legally relevant differences exist; introduce a legal distinction to refute.  
- **Peter Singer**: Climate‑action failure vs. bulldozer destroying crops; claims no relevant difference to equate moral wrongness.

## Practical Checklist for Evaluating Claims

1. **Identify the claim** – factual, moral, or evaluative.  
2. **Locate the authority** – primary vs secondary; check domain relevance.  
3. **Verify credentials** – education, primary publications, peer recognition.  
4. **Assess evidence** – does the authority cite peer‑reviewed data?  
5. **Check conflicts** – funding sources, ideological biases.  
6. **For analogies** – list shared properties, test for relevant differences.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Primary expert | Researcher who generates original, peer‑reviewed evidence in the field. |
| Secondary expert | Interpreter of existing literature without original contributions. |
| Appeal to authority | Accepting a claim solely because an authority says so, without evaluating the argument. |
| Analogical reasoning | Inference based on shared relevant properties between two cases. |
| Publication bias | Tendency of journals to publish significant results, skewing the literature. |
| Consensus | General agreement among experts; informative but not definitive proof. |
| Normative claim | Statement about what ought to be valued or done (outside empirical expertise). |
