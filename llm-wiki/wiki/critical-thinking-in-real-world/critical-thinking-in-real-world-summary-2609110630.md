> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Definition disputes arise from case‑fit uncertainty or disagreement over the definition itself.  
> - A good argument needs **acceptable premises** and a **valid or sufficiently strong inference**; conclusion truth is irrelevant for justification.  
> - Inference strength ranges from maximal (logical entailment) → very strong → moderate → weak.  
> - Evaluate: **Intent** → **Inference strength** → **Premise truth** → **Classification** (Sound, Cogent, Unsound).  
> - Consistency principle: treat similar cases alike unless a significant difference justifies distinction.

## Definition disputes
- **Case‑fit uncertainty**: definition is settled, but fact‑fit unclear (e.g., does Gaza meet the UN genocide definition?).  
- **Definition‑fit disagreement**: parties contest the meaning of the term itself (e.g., “hot‑dog” as a sandwich?).  
- **Breadth vs. narrowness**: broader criteria include more cases; narrower criteria exclude borderline cases, affecting real‑world policy (e.g., boxing eligibility).

> [!info] **Charitable reconstruction** – add implicit premises the author assumes to present the argument in its strongest plausible form.  

## Good argument checklist
- **Premises**: must be true or plausibly true.  
- **Inference**: must be valid (guaranteed) or at least relatively strong.  
- **Conclusion**: its truth is *not* a condition for the argument’s goodness.  

> [!example] *Calvin & Hobbes*: Calvin says “ignorance is bliss” with reasons (a) knowing isn’t fun, (b) not knowing is fun. Reconstruct to test whether the reasons actually support the conclusion.  

> [!warning] A bad argument can have a true conclusion; it still fails to justify it.

## Inference strength
| Strength | Typical source | Betting intuition |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Maximal** | Logical entailment | You would bet **all** your chips. |
| **Very strong** | Long‑standing regularities (e.g., Earth rotates) | You’d bet **most** of them. |
| **Moderate** | Statistical/general knowledge (e.g., most dogs die before 20) | You’d bet **some** chips. |
| **Weak** | Loose associations (e.g., shirt size predicts basketball skill) | You’d bet **none**. |

- Strength is judged **assuming premises are true**; truth is checked later.  
- Adding evidence can raise strength but never exceeds the logical ceiling of the relation.

## Validity, Soundness & Cogency
> [!info] **Valid inference** – premises *guarantee* the conclusion; if premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false.  

- **Validity** = guarantee; **invalidity** = not guarantee (mutually exclusive).  
- **Sound argument** = *valid* + *all premises true*.  
- **Cogent argument** = *relatively strong* + *all premises true*.  

> [!warning] Do **not** use “valid” in its everyday sense; logic restricts it to the guarantee relation.

### Evaluation flow (re‑used from diagram)
1. Identify speaker’s **intent** (deductive vs inductive).  
2. Test **inference**: valid? strong?  
3. Verify **premise truth**.  
4. Classify: **Sound**, **Cogent**, or **Unsound/Uncogent**.

> [!example] Bad‑premise, valid inference: “Some men love whiskey; I love whiskey; therefore some men love whiskey.” Premises true, inference valid → good argument.  
> [!example] Invalid inference, true premise: “If an expert says X, then X is true.” Premise true, inference invalid → bad argument.

## Normative vs. Descriptive conclusions
- **Descriptive (‑is)** claims can be supported by sound arguments alone.  
- **Normative (‑ought)** claims need at least one **normative premise** linking facts to the ought‑statement.  

> [!example] “Smoking is harmful” (descriptive) does **not** by itself justify “You should not smoke” (normative) without a principle such as “If something is harmful, one ought not to do it.”  

- Disagreements over normative conclusions usually target the **normative principle**, not the logical form.

## Consistency principle
- Similar items should be regulated alike **unless** a **significant difference** (e.g., health risk) justifies distinct treatment.  
- Policy‑argument example: show that current drug laws treat comparable substances inconsistently; then argue for reform.

## Practical tips for argument diagrams
- Draw arrows **only** for explicit support.  
- Do not treat the diagram as a chronological narrative.  
- “So” may signal influence but not causation in conditionals; interpret contextually.  
- Begin with the speaker’s intended meaning; avoid imposing your own conclusions.
