> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Organised in **teaching order** by textbook chapter: **diagramming (Ch 2/4) → evaluating arguments (Ch 3) → forms of argument (Ch 5) → reconstructing (Ch 6) → definitions (Ch 10) → practical applications (Ch 11)**.
> - A **good argument** needs **acceptable premises** and a **valid or sufficiently strong inference** — the conclusion's truth is *not* a condition of goodness.
> - **Inference strength** runs maximal (entailment) → very strong → moderate → weak, judged *assuming* the premises are true.
> - Evaluate in order: **Intent → Inference strength → Premise truth → Classification** (Sound / Cogent / Unsound).
> - **Consistency principle**: treat similar cases alike unless a significant difference justifies different treatment.
> - *(Fallacies (Ch 7) and categorical/propositional logic (Ch 8–9) are covered in their own chapter notes.)*

## Chapters 2 & 4 — Diagramming Reasoning

**Theory & concepts**
- An argument diagram maps **explicit support** between claims — start from the **speaker's intended meaning**, not your own reading.
- It is a logical, not a chronological, map: draw arrows only where a reason actually supports a conclusion.

**Practical tips**
- Draw arrows **only** for explicit support; don't treat the diagram as a narrative sequence.
- "So" can signal influence rather than causation in conditionals — interpret in context.
- Begin with the author's intent; avoid imposing your own conclusions.

## Chapter 3 — Evaluating Arguments

**Theory & concepts**
- A good argument has two independent requirements: **premises that are true/plausible** and an **inference that is valid or sufficiently strong**. The conclusion being true does *not* make the argument good.
- **Inference strength** is a scale, judged on the assumption that the premises hold; the actual truth of premises is checked separately.

| Strength | Typical source | Betting intuition |
|----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Maximal | Logical entailment | Bet **all** your chips. |
| Very strong | Long-standing regularities (Earth rotates) | Bet **most**. |
| Moderate | Statistical / general knowledge (most dogs die before 20) | Bet **some**. |
| Weak | Loose associations (shirt size → basketball skill) | Bet **none**. |

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Good argument | Acceptable premises + valid or sufficiently strong inference; conclusion truth irrelevant. |
| Inference strength | How strongly premises support a conclusion (maximal → weak). |
| Reasonable listener | Must accept the conclusion if the argument is good, or identify the specific flaw. |

> [!warning] A true conclusion does **not** rescue a bad argument.
> [!example] *Calvin & Hobbes*: Calvin argues "ignorance is bliss" from (a) knowing isn't fun, (b) not knowing is fun — reconstruct to test whether the reasons actually support the conclusion.

## Chapter 5 — Forms of Argument (Validity, Soundness & Cogency)

**Theory & concepts**
- A **valid** inference *guarantees* the conclusion: if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. Validity/invalidity is binary; strength is a matter of degree only for inductive inferences.
- **Deductive** intent aims for a guarantee (valid/invalid); **inductive** intent aims for likelihood (strong/weak).
- **Sound** = valid + all premises true. **Cogent** = relatively strong + all premises true.

> [!warning] Don't use "valid" in its everyday sense — in logic it means *only* the guarantee relation.

**Evaluation flow**
1. Identify the speaker's **intent** (deductive vs inductive).
2. Test the **inference** (valid? strong?).
3. Verify **premise truth**.
4. **Classify**: Sound, Cogent, or Unsound/Uncogent.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Valid inference | Premises guarantee the conclusion; a false conclusion is impossible if premises are true. |
| Sound argument | Valid inference **and** all premises true. |
| Cogent argument | Relatively strong inference **and** all premises true. |

> [!example] Valid inference, bad case: "Some men love whiskey; I am a man who loves whiskey; therefore some men love whiskey" — premises true, inference valid → good.
> [!example] Invalid inference, true premise: "If an expert says X then X is true" — premise plausible, inference invalid → bad argument.

## Chapter 6 — Reconstructing Arguments

**Theory & concepts**
- **Charitable reconstruction**: add the **implicit premises** an author assumes so the argument is presented in its **strongest plausible form** before you evaluate it.
- This distinguishes a genuinely weak argument from one that merely left its assumptions unstated.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Charitable reconstruction | Adding plausible implicit premises to present the strongest version of an argument. |
| Implicit premise | An unstated assumption the argument needs to work. |

## Chapter 10 — Definitions

**Theory & concepts**
- **Definition disputes** take two forms: **case-fit uncertainty** (the definition is settled, but whether a fact meets it is unclear) and **definition-fit disagreement** (the parties contest the meaning of the term itself).
- **Breadth vs narrowness** of a definition changes the stakes — broader criteria sweep in more cases, narrower ones exclude borderline cases.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Case-fit uncertainty | Settled definition, unclear whether a specific fact meets it (does Gaza meet the UN genocide definition?). |
| Definition-fit disagreement | Parties contest the term's meaning itself (is a hot-dog a sandwich?). |
| Breadth vs narrowness | How inclusive the criteria are; shifts which cases count and the real-world stakes (e.g. boxing eligibility). |

## Chapter 11 — Practical Applications (Normative claims & Consistency)

**Theory & concepts**
- **Descriptive ("is")** conclusions can be supported by factual arguments alone; **normative ("ought")** conclusions require at least one **normative premise** linking facts to the "ought". Disagreements usually target that principle, not the logic.
- The **consistency principle**: similar cases should be treated alike **unless** a **significant difference** justifies different treatment — a core engine of policy argument.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Normative principle | A premise linking factual claims to an "ought" conclusion. |
| Descriptive vs normative | "Is" claims (fact) vs "ought" claims (value/policy). |
| Consistency principle | Treat similar cases alike unless a substantive difference justifies a distinction. |

> [!example] "Smoking is harmful" (descriptive) does not by itself justify "you should not smoke" (normative) without a principle like "if something is harmful, one ought not do it."
> [!example] *Drug-policy argument*: show current laws treat comparable substances inconsistently, then argue for reform on the consistency principle.
