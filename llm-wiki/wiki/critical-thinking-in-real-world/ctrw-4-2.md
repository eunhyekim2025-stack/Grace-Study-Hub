> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Attacks are arguments; they can target a premise, the inference, or the conclusion.  
> - Reductive attacks prove falsity; inductive attacks show high probability of falsity.  
> - A cogent argument requires true premises, a strong inference, and reliability (no omitted weakening info).  
> - Use a clear diagram (formal or deep) to identify structure before critiquing.  
> - Apply the charity principle: assume the weakest plausible target (usually the inference) unless context forces a conclusion‑attack reading.

## 1. What an attack is
> [!info] **Attack definition** – An argument whose conclusion is that the attacked statement is false.  
- **Premise attack**: “Albert is blind” → attacks premise “Albert drives a taxi”.  
- **Conclusion attack**: “Albert receives welfare → Albert is not rich”.  
- **Inference attack**: Shows the logical link is unacceptable (e.g., “For every rich taxi driver there are three poor ones” refutes “Albert is rich because he drives a taxi”).  

> [!warning] **Bad attack** – Replies that do not target any component (e.g., “you’re always late”) are weak and unproductive.

## 2. Types of attacks
| Type | Goal | Success → |
|------|------|-----------|
| Reductive | Prove the attacked statement **must** be false | **Sound** attack |
| Inductive (aka “gorgeous”) | Show it is **very likely** false | **Strong** attack (unsound if fails) |

> [!info] **Intention rule** – Reductive: “Because the attacking statement is true, the attacked statement must be false.”  
Inductive: “Because the attacking statement is true, it is very probable the attacked statement is false.”

## 3. Locating the target
1. Identify **premises**, **inference**, **conclusion**.  
2. If ambiguous, apply **charity**: assume the attack is on the inference (lower burden) unless context forces a conclusion‑attack.  
3. Example of ambiguity: “Mona didn’t finish the AC” could attack the conclusion (Mona will go to university) or the inference (from Emma’s attendance).  

## 4. Evaluating the original argument (cogency)
> [!info] **Cogent argument** – (1) true premises, (2) relatively strong inference, (3) **reliability** (no omitted weakening info).  

- **Reliability**: No relevant, accessible information that would lower the conclusion’s likelihood is hidden.  
- **Available information** = facts the arguer or audience could reasonably know at the time.  

### Example: Air‑conditioner sales pitch
- Premises: cheap, energy‑efficient, “100 % environmentally friendly”.  
- Omitted fact: costly maintenance → argument becomes **unreliable** → not cogent.

### Example: Voting claim
- Premises true; inference strong.  
- Hidden facts (party whip, personal feud) would make it unreliable **if** the audience could know them.

## 5. Step‑by‑step assessment checklist
| Step | Question |
|------|----------|
| 1. Faithfulness | Does my reconstruction match the author’s statements? |
| 2. Identify structure | Premises → Inference → Conclusion (draw arrows). |
| 3. Strength | Are premises true? Does the inference provide sufficient support? |
| 4. Reliability | Is any relevant, weakening info omitted that the audience could know? |

> [!example] **Dress‑code debate** – Reena attacks the conclusion (discipline), Kevin attacks a premise (attire influences decorum), Steven attacks the underlying assumption (norms are problematic).  

## 6. Diagramming attacks
- **Formal diagram**: solid arrows = support, dotted arrows = attacks.  
- **Deep diagram**: includes both solid and dotted arrows; each arrow counts as a separate argument.  

> [!info] **Arrow count** – A deep diagram with 10 arrows represents 10 distinct argumentative moves.  

### Attack identification tip
Isolate the statement; ask whether it **weakens belief in the conclusion** (conclusion attack) or **undermines the reasoning** (inference attack).  
- Statement 7 “you could always hire a car” attacks the inference that buying a car is necessary, not the conclusion that buying a car is a good decision.

## 7. Common pitfalls
- Responding with new reasons **before** diagnosing the flaw.  
- Ignoring the need for a narrow (formal) diagram, leading to vague attacks.  
- Treating public‑platform comments as “small talk” when they merit substantive analysis.

## 8. Practical advice for responders
1. **Draw a narrow diagram** of the original argument.  
2. **State the target** (premise, inference, conclusion).  
3. **Choose attack type** (reductive/inductive) and present supporting premises.  
4. **Check reliability** – disclose any relevant omitted info.  

> [!warning] **Cognitive cost** – Meaningful engagement can take ≈1 hour per argument; be prepared to invest the effort.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Attack | Argument whose conclusion is that a statement is false |
| Reductive attack | Proves the attacked statement must be false |
| Inductive attack | Shows it is highly probable the attacked statement is false |
| Cogent argument | True premises + strong inference + reliability |
| Reliability | No relevant, accessible weakening information is omitted |
| Charity principle | Assume the weakest plausible target for an ambiguous attack |
| Deep diagram | Visual model with solid (support) and dotted (attack) arrows; each arrow = an argument |
