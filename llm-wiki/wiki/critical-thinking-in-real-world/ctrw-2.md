# CTRW 2 — Definitions

> [[critical-thinking-in-real-world/index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · Seminar 2 (recording). The textbook backing is [[ctrw-ch10-definitions|Ch10]]; the opening minutes finish off [[ctrw-ch02-diagramming-reasoning|Ch2]]'s linked-vs-convergent drill from Seminar 1.

> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - A definition is owed when the **disagreement is about the word itself** — argue about the argument only after that is settled, or you talk past each other.
> - A good definition serves **two masters: accuracy and informativeness.** Every named fault is a failure of one of them.
> - **Circularity is not "the word reappears"** — it is *presuming the hearer already understands the term you are defining*.
> - **Only necessary conditions → too broad. Only sufficient conditions → too narrow.** You need both.
> - A **stipulative** definition is judged by its **purpose**, not by whether it captures ordinary usage.

---

## 1. Closing out Seminar 1 — linked or convergent?

The first audible stretch is still the [[ctrw-ch02-diagramming-reasoning|Ch2]] drill, and the test used
is worth keeping:

> [!info] The question to ask
> **Can this reason, *on its own*, relatively adequately support the conclusion?**
> If yes for each reason separately → **convergent**. If each needs the other to do its work →
> **linked**.

- *Flight demand*: "Singaporeans go on holiday" supports "demand is higher" on its own; "foreigners visit family" does too. Each is independently adequate → **convergent**.
- But a claim about **supply** cannot on its own support a conclusion about **supply and demand coming together** — it needs the demand claim beside it → **linked**.

> [!important] When you genuinely cannot tell, that is the answer
> Asked whether melting ice caps plus extreme weather are *linked* or *convergent* support for
> "climate change is happening", the lecturer refused to rule: *"I do not know at what point an
> evidence becomes clear evidence, because I am not a climate scientist."* Charity here requires
> **asking someone who knows** — and the same holds for "she is rich and she is hot, so I should
> date her", which cannot be settled without asking the arguer whether they need both.
> Being unable to decide from the armchair is a real and reportable result, not a failure.

## 2. Why a definition is owed at all

For your opinion to be taken seriously you must first give an **argument** and the other side must
**hear it out**. But before quality of argument can even be discussed, one hurdle remains: making
sure the disagreement is actually *about the argument*.

> [!example] Emma and Gabby on the problem of evil
> Emma: *if God exists he is all-powerful, all-knowing and all-good; then there would be no evil in
> the world; but there is evil; so there is no God.* Gabby replies: **"Who says there is evil in the
> world?"**
> The disagreement is not about the quality of Emma's argument at all — it is about what **"evil"**
> means. Until that is settled there is no point continuing.

So a definition is called for in three situations:

| Occasion | What a definition does |
|---|---|
| **Disagreement over a term** | Lets you find out whether the disagreement can be reconciled at all — you need one *both* sides find acceptable |
| **You are advancing an opinion** using a term that is unclear or has several senses (*meritocracy*) | Gives the hearer the platform they need to judge whether your opinion is reasonable |
| **The hearer simply doesn't know the term** (a prospective student asking what *interdisciplinary* means) | A good-faith request for information |

## 3. Definiendum and definiens

- **Definiendum** — the term being defined.
- **Definiens** — the expression doing the defining.

A good definition must be **accurate** *and* **informative**. (On accuracy the lecturer's caveat: we
can argue about whether you *achieved* accuracy, but the **intention** to be accurate is
non-negotiable.) Everything that follows is a way of failing one of these two.

## 4. Failing to be informative

### Metaphor
*"Residents' Committees are the crowning jewels among grassroots political organisations in
Singapore."* A foreigner learns that RCs are *like* jewels, not what they *are*. Same with defining a
"big question" as **"a question with many layers"** — cakes have layers too.

### Obscurity
You are saying nothing false, but saying it in a way that blocks understanding.

> [!example] The tyre
> A child asks what a tyre is; you answer *"a rim-mountable pneumatic…"*.
> **Obscurity is contextual** — that definition is fine for a materials engineer and useless for
> everyone else. Audience decides.

> [!note] No tension between accuracy and informativeness
> Asked whether being clear forces you to be less accurate, the lecturer said no: lack of clarity
> interferes with **informativeness**; broadness and narrowness interfere with **accuracy**. They are
> separate dials and you want both.

### Ambiguity
*"Adultery is having sex with someone with a spouse."* Taken at face value it forbids sleeping with
your **own** wife, who is also "someone with a spouse". This is **structural** ambiguity — the
sentence can be parsed two ways ([[ctrw-ch10-definitions|Ch10]] distinguishes it from lexical
ambiguity and from vagueness).

*"A democracy is a form of government where **people** are in control of state policy"* — North Korea
qualifies, since Kim Jong-un and his circle are people. "People" and "control" are both doing
undefined work.

### Circularity

> [!important] The textbook's rule is itself a bad definition
> Textbook: *a definition is circular iff the definiendum reappears in the definiens.* The lecturer
> attacked it from both sides — the same objection [[ctrw-ch10-definitions|Ch10]] records:
> - **Too narrow:** *"to be a good man you have to avoid being a bad man"* contains no repeat of
>   "good", yet is plainly circular — to know what *bad* is you must already know *good*.
> - **Too broad:** an Australian-Cattle-Dog-style definition repeats words while genuinely adding
>   information, so it is not circular at all.
>
> **The real criterion:** a definition is circular iff the definiens **presumes you already
> understand the term being defined.**

Examples that fail it: *"time is what is measured by a clock"* (if you knew what a clock was, you
would already know what time is) and *"a free rider is someone who free rides on others."*

## 5. Failing to be accurate

| Fault | Test | Example from class |
|---|---|---|
| **Too broad** | the definiens admits things that are **not** examples of the definiendum | *"a typewriter is a means of writing"* — so is a pen |
| **Too narrow** | the definiens excludes things that **are** examples | *"a bird is a feathered animal that flies"* — penguins are out |

### Precision is not free

Two definitions of a *foetus* were compared, one more precise than the other. The more precise one is
**not** the better one:

> [!warning] Greater precision can make a definition worse
> Pinning the definition to a developmental threshold buys clarity by asserting something
> **controversial** — there is no scientific consensus on when a foetus becomes a human being. You
> gained precision and gave up accuracy. *"You can't follow the rule blindly that more precise means
> a better definition"* — you have to exercise judgement about what the context actually needs.

## 6. Methods of defining

### Ostensive — point at an example
Maximum clarity about *this* case, at the cost of leaving the boundary unstated.

### Descriptive — genus and difference
Reserve the descriptive approach for terms whose meaning is **intersubjectively well understood** —
where we already consensually agree, and your job is only to *describe* that agreement.

Method: **start deliberately too broad, then narrow in stages** until nothing that fits the definiens
is a non-example.

| Step | Cat | Whisky |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "a mammal" — far too broad | "an alcoholic beverage" — too broad |
| 2 | "a **feline** mammal" — still admits tigers | "…made from **barley**" — still admits beer |
| 3 | add the difference that separates it from the other felines | "…with at least **40% alcohol**" |

### Necessary and sufficient conditions

> [!important] You need both — and the failure modes are not symmetric
> - **Only a *necessary* condition → too broad.** *"A shape is a rhombus only if it has four sides."*
>   True, but it lets in squares, rectangles and trapezoids.
> - **Only a *sufficient* condition → too narrow.** *"A definition is unclear if it is vague."*
>   True, but it shuts out unclear definitions that are ambiguous or obscure instead.
>
> Done properly: *"a person is a bachelor **if and only if** the person is an unmarried man."*

> [!note] This corrects [[ctrw-ch10-definitions]]
> That note had the two the wrong way round ("only sufficient → too broad; only necessary → too
> narrow"). The seminar's version above is the correct one, and Ch10 has been fixed to match.

### Stipulative — defined by purpose
A stipulative definition is not reporting usage, so it cannot be "wrong"; it is judged by whether it
**serves the purpose you set it**.

> [!example] Defining "slippers" for a dress code
> The purpose is **to guide behaviour**. A commonly-understood, slightly-too-narrow definition is the
> right choice: people may contrive ways around it, but nothing more effective exists for that
> purpose. *"The purpose gives you the ability to choose"* what to give up — here, catching every
> example of a slipper.

## 7. The *Yes Minister* clip — every fault at once

A plant is rumoured to produce a dangerous chemical; the MP for the constituency wants to know the
difference between **dioxin** and **metadioxin**. The exercise: *where is a definition being asked
for, where is one given, and what is wrong with it?*

| Move | The fault |
|---|---|
| *"All chemicals are dangerous, it just depends"* | dodges the question by making the term useless |
| Humphrey's Greek etymology of *meta-* — "with or after, sometimes beyond, accusative or genitive…" | **accurate and entirely uninformative**; answers a different question than the one asked |
| *"Metadioxin is an inert compound of dioxin"* | **obscurity** — "inert" and "compound" are exactly the words the asker doesn't have |
| *"You know what compound interest is — that's the sort of thing a compound is"* | the definiens exploits an **ambiguous** term, and picks the wrong sense |
| *"Nobody suggests you're Hitler just because the names are alike"* | changes the subject entirely |

> [!tip] The transferable lesson
> When you are asked to define an **ambiguous** term, your job is to say **which sense you are
> using**. Keep that separate from ambiguity *inside* the definiens (the adultery case above) — one
> is about the definiendum, the other about the definition you gave.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Definiendum** | the term being defined |
| **Definiens** | the expression that does the defining |
| **Informative** | the hearer can actually use it — no metaphor, obscurity, ambiguity or circularity |
| **Accurate** | catches all and only the examples — neither too broad nor too narrow |
| **Circular** | the definiens presumes you already understand the definiendum (*not* merely repeating the word) |
| **Obscurity** | true but stated in terms this audience cannot use — contextual, judged by audience |
| **Too broad / too narrow** | the definiens admits non-examples / excludes genuine examples |
| **Genus and difference** | place the term in a larger class, then state what separates it from the rest of that class |
| **Stipulative definition** | a meaning laid down for a purpose rather than reported from usage — judged by fitness for that purpose |
