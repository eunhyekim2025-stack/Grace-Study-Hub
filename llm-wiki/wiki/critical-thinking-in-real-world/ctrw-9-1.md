> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- Conspiracy theories claim a hidden, powerful network; evaluate them by simplicity, coherence, and predictive power.  
- Common biases (confirmation, rationalization, appeal to ignorance) make complex explanations feel plausible.  
- Turning correlation into causation requires eliminating noise, confounders, and reverse causation, and supplying a mechanism.  
- Randomized Controlled Trials are the experimental gold standard for causal claims, but may be infeasible.  

## Conspiracy Theory Basics
- **Definition**: A claim that a secret, closed network of powerful actors covertly pursues a specific goal using a particular method.  
- Can be **global** (e.g., “Jews rule the world”) or **local** (e.g., a single death covered up).  
- Usually framed as **malevolent**, though some are presented as **benevolent** (e.g., divine conspiracies).  

> [!info] **Conspiracy Theory** – secret network + goal + method; global vs local; malevolent vs benevolent.

## Evaluating Conspiracy Claims
| Criterion | What it demands |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Simplicity | Fewer assumptions |
| Coherence | Fits established knowledge |
| Predictive power | Generates testable predictions |

- **Red‑flag questions**:  
  1. Is there a simpler explanation covering the same facts?  
  2. How many documented instances support the complex claim?  
  3. Does it rely on “free‑rider” reasoning (“they hide it, so we can’t see it”)?

> [!example] **COVID‑19 lab‑leak** – natural zoonotic spillover is simpler, better‑supported; the secret‑bioweapon story lacks comparable evidence.

## Cognitive Biases that Fuel Conspiracy Thinking
- **Confirmation bias** – seek only supporting evidence.  
- **Rationalization of disconfirming evidence** – attribute failures to conspirators’ secrecy.  
- **Appeal to ignorance** – “cannot be disproved → must be true.”  
- **Authority bias** – accept a claim because an expert says so, without independent verification.  

> [!warning] Authority bias can masquerade as sound reasoning; always check the underlying evidence.

## From Correlation to Causation
- **Two‑step inference**:  
  1. **Generalization** – show the sample correlation reflects a true population correlation (eliminate *noise*).  
  2. **Explanation** – show the population correlation reflects a causal effect (eliminate *bias*).  

- **Noise**: random variation that disappears at the population level.  
- **Bias**: systematic distortion; main types:

| Bias type | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Confounder (common cause) | Third variable influences both X and Y (e.g., genetics affecting nicotine use and cancer). |
| Reverse causation | Y actually causes X (e.g., higher prices causing demand, not vice‑versa). |

- **Spurious correlation example**: Sociology PhDs vs. worldwide non‑commercial space launches (r = 0.789) – no plausible mechanism; likely noise or selective sampling.  

> [!example] **Ice‑cream sales & shark attacks** – both driven by beach attendance (common cause), not a direct causal link.

## Experimental Gold Standard: Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)
- **Core elements**:  
  1. Random assignment of participants.  
  2. Distinct treatment and control groups.  
  3. Outcome measurement.  
- **Randomization** reduces noise; **blinding** (single or double) reduces bias.  
- **Limitations**:  
  - Some interventions (e.g., yoga) cannot be double‑blinded.  
  - Ethical/practical constraints may forbid random allocation (e.g., assigning people to smoke).  

> [!warning] When RCTs are infeasible, causal claims based on single‑case observations are weak and prone to spurious inference.

## Applying Causal Reasoning to Real Data
- **Marriage‑obesity correlation** (sample = 2,405): possible explanations  
  1. Reverse causation – obesity leads to marriage.  
  2. Common cause – age or wealth increase both marriage likelihood and obesity.  
  3. Direct causal mechanism – shared meals raise caloric intake.  

  *Testing*: control for age & wealth; if the link persists, the direct mechanism gains support.  

- **NAFTA lemons vs. highway fatalities**:  
  - Correlation observed (1996‑2000) is not causal.  
  - Plausible common causes: improved vehicle safety, fuel‑price changes.  
  - Limited data set prevents generalization.  

> [!example] **Marriage‑obesity** – researchers must rule out confounders and propose a mechanism (shared meals) before claiming causation.

## Key Terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Conspiracy theory | Claim of a secret, powerful network pursuing a hidden goal. |
| Simplicity (Occam’s razor) | Preference for explanations with fewer assumptions. |
| Confirmation bias | Tendency to seek evidence that supports one’s existing belief. |
| Noise | Random variation that obscures true population relationships. |
| Bias (confounder, reverse causation) | Systematic distortion that can create false causal appearances. |
| Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) | Experimental design with random assignment, control group, and blinding to infer causality. |
| Authority bias | Accepting a claim solely because an expert endorses it. |
