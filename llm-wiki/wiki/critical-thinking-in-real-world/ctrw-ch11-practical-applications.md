## Socratic questioning — the deepest tool
Drawing out truth and testing beliefs by questioning, in three overlapping modes:

| Mode | Greek sense | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Maieusis** | "midwifery" | *draw out* ideas someone already implicitly has, by good questions (Plato's *Meno*: a slave boy reaches a geometry truth through questions alone) |
| **Elenchos** | "cross-examination" | *test* a claim by teasing out its implications until it hits a contradiction (*aporia*) — the way a definition of "lying" gets refined step by step into "telling someone something with the intention of making them believe what you know is false" |
| **Dialectic** | "discussing" | a *shared* conversational inquiry — talk ideas through, test theory against practice, stay open to being surprised; there is "no bad or stupid question" |

> The point of Socratic questioning is not just to gather facts but to **evaluate implications and assumptions**, move toward greater clarity, and stay willing to uncover the unexpected.

## Writing an argumentative essay — four stages
An argumentative essay argues a position on a **contested** issue (if it weren't contested, no essay would be needed).

1. **Stage 1 — Think (no writing yet; the most important stage).** Pick a topic that is neither too broad nor too narrow; see it from all sides; examine motivating assumptions; make a definite **claim**; gather evidence (stats, expert opinion, analogies); **anticipate objections and alternatives** — and *steelman* them (never [[ctrw-ch07-fallacies|straw-man]]).
2. **Stage 2 — Outline / diagram.** [[ctrw-ch04-diagramming-reasons-for-and-against|Diagram]] your reasons, objections and conclusion; the map reveals hidden assumptions and where to focus objections, and becomes your plan.
3. **Stage 3 — Draft.** Clear **beginning** (state the problem + your stance), **body** (one point per paragraph, with **signposts** — "Having dealt with X, I now turn to Y"), and **end** (conclusion — don't introduce new lines here).
4. **Stage 4 — Revise (don't underestimate it).** Let it sit; read with an audience's eyes; spell out what only *you* know; check your premises really *do* support your conclusion; hunt your own fallacies; show it to a friend.

## Moral reasoning
- **Ethical principles are the hidden premises.** A moral conclusion needs at least one **moral** premise — "my parents are elderly" (fact) only reaches "I ought to care for them" once you add "adult children should care for their elderly parents" (principle). Surfacing that principle makes the argument valid *and* exposes it to challenge.
- **The consistency principle — "treat like cases alike."** You can't judge one case one way and a similar case differently *unless* there's a **morally relevant difference**. This is [[ctrw-ch07-fallacies|argument from analogy]] applied to ethics, and the core of justice (objecting to being punished when you did nothing to deserve it).
- **Dilemmas via disjunction.** Frame a hard choice as "either A or B," but note that casting it as a tidy [[ctrw-ch05-forms-of-argument|disjunctive syllogism]] can *hide* the moral weight — the real work is **justifying which horn**, using the details of the circumstances, not the deductive form.
- **Argument from Tradition is a fallacy** ("we've always done it, so it's right"). It also arises with **cultural relativism** — but distinguish two things: *cultural* relativism is **descriptive** (moralities vary across cultures — true and compatible with universal principles); *ethical* relativism is the further **normative** claim that no framework is better than another. Ethical relativism is **self-defeating**: "all moral claims are relative" is itself an *absolute* claim, and "we must tolerate every culture" is an absolute value smuggled in.
- **Moral definitions matter** ([[ctrw-ch10-definitions|Ch10]]): whether "euthanasia" is defined as "murder," or an activist as "terrorist" vs "freedom fighter," can settle a debate before it starts — agree on neutral terms first.

## Legal reasoning
- Naive picture: law is pure deduction — "Law says if Y then penalty Z; Y happened; so Z." But every defence says **"my case is different."**
- So real legal reasoning is **largely inductive**: deductive rule-application only *categorises* a case and sets expectations; the disputed details are argued by analogy to precedent, weighing relevant similarities/differences — the same **"treat like cases alike"** consistency principle as ethics.
- Law and morality overlap but aren't identical (the **legal positivist** denies any necessary connection); a good legal system protects you even where you don't share the society's morality, as long as you harm no one.

## Scientific & management reasoning
- **Scientific reasoning** leans on **[[ctrw-ch01-basic-concepts-of-reasoning|induction]]** and the **[[ctrw-ch03-evaluating-arguments|argument from best explanation]]**: form hypotheses, test them against evidence, and guard against the causal fallacies of [[ctrw-ch07-fallacies|Ch7]] (post hoc, cum hoc, biased/small sample).
- **Management reasoning** applies the *same* argument tools to decisions under uncertainty — clarify the claim/goal, weigh evidence and stakeholder objections, calibrate how much to claim, and decide on the most **plausible** option rather than a falsely "certain" one.

## Key takeaways
- **Match the question to the knowledge** (know-that / how / why); use **Socratic** questioning (maieusis · elenchos · dialectic) to test assumptions, not just collect facts.
- Write essays in **four stages** — the thinking/steelmanning (Stage 1) and the revision (Stage 4) are where quality is won.
- Moral & legal reasoning both run on **hidden principles** + the **"treat like cases alike"** consistency rule; watch for **appeal to tradition** and **self-defeating relativism**.
- Across science and management, it's the same engine: **evidence → best explanation → a calibrated, defensible decision.**
