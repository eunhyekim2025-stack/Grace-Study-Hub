# CTRW 6-2 — Abduction: coherence, predictive power, and appeal to ignorance

> [[critical-thinking-in-real-world/index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · Lecture 6, part 2 — continues [[ctrw-6-inductive-forms|CTRW 6 — Inductive Forms]] (abduction). Reference chapter: [[ctrw-ch07-fallacies|Ch7]] (appeal to ignorance).

> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - We choose the "best" explanation mainly by **coherence** — the explanation that clashes *least* with the background stories we already hold about the world. That makes abduction **subjective**: what counts as "best" depends on upbringing, culture and personal belief.
> - Coherence is corrupted by **confirmation bias**. The fix (Wason's 2-4-6 task): actively look for evidence *against* your hypothesis, not for it.
> - **Predictive power** is the more objective criterion: derive testable predictions from each explanation and keep the one whose predictions come true. An explanation that predicts **nothing** (a ghost) is thereby the weaker one.
> - **Appeal to ignorance** ("no evidence for X, therefore X is false") is weak as stated but is charitably an **abduction** — its cogency turns on one premise: *is "X is false" really the best explanation of the missing evidence?*
> - When appeal to ignorance runs both ways (God exists / doesn't), the question becomes **burden of proof** — and that is often not clean.

---

## Criterion 1 — Coherence

Abduction picks the explanation that **least violates our existing beliefs about the world**. We each carry a *worldview* — a web of background stories about what is true and false — and, often without noticing, we accept the explanation that keeps that web most intact.

> [!info] "Give up the belief you hold least strongly"
> When an observation forces a change (the **phosphine on Venus** case: we do *not* start out believing there is microbial life there), the belief we surrender is the one we were **least committed to**. Trace almost any dispute between two experts back far enough and it bottoms out in a disagreement about **which picture of the world** is more believable.

Because coherence runs on personal background stories, the *same* observation gets *different* best explanations from different people:

| Situation | Depends on your background story |
|---|---|
| A strange nightly experience | Raised in a tradition that accepts the supernatural → a **ghost** is coherent; raised in a scientific tradition → a **hallucination** is coherent |
| A partner spotted at dinner with someone else | A suspicious / low-trust worldview → **cheating** is coherent; a trusting worldview → an innocent explanation is coherent (and it also depends on whether he *usually* tells you his plans) |
| Two judges, one case | Different background stories → **different verdicts** — the subjectivity built into how we make meaning of the world |

> [!warning] Where do the background stories come from?
> Two sources make people's stories differ: **experience / upbringing** (a pre-modern culture explains disease by an angry god; the modern one by pathogens) and **personal belief**. So an explanation that is "coherent" in one time or group can be incoherent in another.

## The danger in coherence — confirmation bias

Because we pick by coherence, we are prone to **confirmation bias**: once we hold a view we hunt for evidence *for* it and quietly ignore evidence *against* it. It is natural — "we are a little in love with our own beliefs" — which is exactly why it is dangerous.

> [!example] Wason's 2-4-6 task
> You are shown **2, 4, 6** and told a hidden rule generated it; you may propose triples and ask whether each fits. Most people guess "add 2" and then test **8, 10, 12** — a case that *confirms*. But a confirming test adds nothing: the informative move is to try a triple that would **break** your rule (e.g. **1, 2, 3** or **1, 4, 9**). Looking only for confirmation is how the natural bias shows itself.

> [!tip] Three safeguards for any abduction
> 1. **List all the alternative explanations first.** Skip this and you will pick the one you were already biased toward.
> 2. **Audit your background stories.** If they do not track reality, your "best" explanation won't be best — however right it feels.
> 3. **Watch the stories you *love*.** The beliefs you are most attached to are the ones you will least want to give up (this is the engine of confirmation bias — and of one-sided "research" written only to justify a conclusion already held).

## Criterion 2 — Predictive power

Coherence is subjective and **simplicity** is an aesthetic preference, so where possible use the more objective test: **predictive power**. From each candidate explanation, derive **testable predictions**, then check which predictions come true. The explanation that yields **more true predictions** is the better one.

> [!example] The dog that won't eat
> Explanations: (a) it is **sick** → predict other symptoms; (b) it is **full / ate something** → predict it eats again in a few hours; (c) it "wants to lose weight" → we laugh, because that explanation is **incoherent** with what we believe a dog can want. Between the coherent ones, we let the **predictions** decide.

- **Science runs on this.** Einstein did not stop at "here is how the world is structured" — relativity **predicts** specific observations, and we accept it to the extent those come true. The Venus case is the same shape: *if* the clouds contain microbial life, *then* a sample should detect it — a testable prediction.
- **No predictions → not the better explanation.** "A ghost crossed the road" explains the swerve but predicts **nothing** further; among rivals, the one that can be tested wins.
- **False predictions have value too** — they let you *eliminate* an explanation.

> [!important] Bad abduction can be tragic
> Two literary warnings. **Romeo** sees Juliet lying motionless and abducts "she is dead" — without weighing the better explanation (a sleeping potion), in a world where such potions exist. **Othello** finds the gifts he gave Desdemona in other men's hands and abducts "she is unfaithful" — never considering the **conspiracy** that planted them. Both skip the alternatives; both end in a killing.

## Worked case — the discrimination TV debate

A journalist and the economist **Thomas Sowell** argue over whether census income gaps show workplace discrimination against Black Americans. Reconstructed as an argument diagram, it is a clean specimen of the week's forms:

- **Her argument.** Census data (population level) show Black workers earn less on average than White workers → an **inductive generalisation / statistical syllogism** carries that down to individuals → then an **abduction**: *the best explanation of the gap is racial discrimination.*
- **His attack.** Sowell does not deny the data; he attacks the **abduction** — disputing that discrimination is the *best* explanation and offering a rival: higher-paying jobs require **college education**, in which Black workers were then under-represented (compare like-for-like — e.g. both spouses college-educated — and the gap narrows or flips).

> [!note] The general move
> Interpreting data is itself an abduction ("why is the data this way?"). The most common way to challenge an abductive argument is exactly Sowell's: **grant the observation, dispute that the named explanation is the best one, and supply a better one.**

## Appeal to ignorance — as an abduction

**Appeal to ignorance** uses a *lack* of evidence as a reason:

> [!info] The two forms
> - We have **no evidence that X** ∴ X is **false**.
> - We have **no evidence that not-X** ∴ X is **true**.

As stated the inference is **weak**. The charitable reading turns it into an **abduction**:

> **P1.** There is no evidence for X.  **P2.** The *best explanation* of that absence is that X is false.  ∴ **C.** X is false.

So its quality turns on **P2** — *is "X is false" really the best explanation of why there is no evidence?* The inference and the conclusion can rarely be attacked; the whole fight is over that second premise.

| Case | Is "it's false" the best explanation of the missing evidence? |
|---|---|
| **HIV** — a reliable test comes back negative | **Yes** — evidence *would* show up if it were there, so absence is good evidence of absence. **Cogent.** |
| **North Korea, COVID community spread** — no reported evidence | **No** — a better explanation is that the evidence was **suppressed or never collected**. Uncogent. |
| **Iraq WMD (2003)** — UN inspector Hans Blix reports no stockpiles found | Blix infers "no WMDs"; Bush **rejects P2**, claiming a better explanation (they were hidden). History: no stockpiles were found — **rejecting the best explanation had catastrophic consequences.** |
| **God exists** — no evidence | Contested: perhaps God is **not the kind of thing evidence could bear on** — a better explanation of the absence than "God is false." |

> [!tip] The habit
> When you meet an appeal to ignorance, rewrite it as an abduction and ask only: **can I give a better explanation of the missing evidence than "the claim is false"?** If yes, it fails on P2; if no, it may be cogent.

## Burden of proof

Sometimes there is no evidence **either way** (does God exist? are the ministers systematically corrupt?), so you can run the appeal to ignorance in **both directions** at once. Then the real question is **who bears the burden of proof** — who must supply the evidence?

> [!info] Russell's rule (and its complication)
> **Bertrand Russell**: the person making the **existence claim** carries the burden. His illustration is an *undetectable* tiny elephant held between two fingers: because it is defined to be undetectable, "there is no evidence it *doesn't* exist" cannot license "so it exists" — the claimant must give a positive reason.
> **The complication:** the person who says "God does **not** exist" is arguably *also* making a claim (that billions of believers are deluded), so the burden is not always cleanly on one side. In the elephant case it is obvious; in others it genuinely is not.

## Conspiracy theories — abduction gone wrong

Conspiracy theories are abduction with the safeguards removed: fix the conclusion first, then hunt for "clues" that confirm it (confirmation bias) while simpler, better-predicting explanations are ignored. The lecture runs through familiar examples — staged massacres, hidden vaccine harms, the "Paul McCartney is dead" hoax — where believers assemble a wall of coincidental clues yet the theory predicts nothing and overlooks the ordinary explanation. As a rule, the bigger the name, the weirder the theory.

> [!note] The point
> The fault is not the *form* — abduction is legitimate and everywhere (doctors, judges, scientists all use it). The fault is the *misuse*: no list of alternatives, background stories that do not track reality, and an active refusal to seek disconfirming evidence.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Abduction / inference to the best explanation** | Concluding the explanation that best accounts for an observation |
| **Coherence** | The criterion we actually use most — least violation of our background beliefs; subjective |
| **Predictive power** | The more objective criterion — prefer the explanation whose testable predictions come true |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking evidence for a held belief while ignoring evidence against it |
| **Wason 2-4-6 task** | The classic demonstration that people test to confirm rather than to disconfirm |
| **Appeal to ignorance** | Using a lack of evidence to conclude something is true or false; charitably an abduction whose weight rests on its second premise |
| **Burden of proof** | Who must supply the evidence when none exists either way — on the claimant, per Russell |
