> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- Large samples shrink random error; bias can remain regardless of size.  
- Bias types: environmental, self‑selection, survivorship, questionnaire (omission, wording, social desirability, recall, etc.).  
- Distinguish **noise** (random sampling variability) from **bias** (systematic error).  
- Evaluate any generalization by checking population definition, sample size, bias sources, and effect size.  
- Unjustified generalizations → stereotypes → prejudice.

## 1. Sample size & estimator precision
- **Small sample** → high standard error → noisy, imprecise estimator.  
- **Large sample** → estimates cluster → smaller standard error → precise estimator.  
- Rule of thumb: sample ≈ 10 % of a modest population (e.g., 10 beans from 100) for reasonable precision.  
- Sample size adequacy depends on population size, not a fixed proportion.

> [!info] **Estimator** – a statistic computed from the sample that serves as a proxy for the population property (e.g., sample mean for population mean).

## 2. Bias – systematic distortion
### 2.1 Sample‑composition bias
| Bias | Cause | Example |
|------|-------|---------|
| **Environmental** | Sampling only from a specific setting/demographic | Only first‑generation Singaporeans |
| **Self‑selection** | Respondents volunteer because they align with sponsor | PAP‑commissioned poll answered mainly by supporters |
| **Livelihood/repression** | Certain groups unavailable (e.g., imprisoned) | Political dissenters excluded |
| **Survivorship** | Only “survivors” observed | WWII aircraft study; K2 climbers |

> [!warning] **Survivorship trap** – ignoring non‑survivors yields a distorted picture (e.g., gym “before‑after” stories).

### 2.2 Questionnaire bias
| Type | Mechanism | Illustration |
|------|-----------|--------------|
| **Omission** | Missing answer options | Only “Grab” or “MRT” for travel |
| **Wording** | Loaded/emotive language | Calling traffic “waste” |
| **Vague/ambiguous** | Multiple interpretations | “How often do you exercise?” without scale |
| **Central‑tendency** | Preference for middle Likert option | “Neutral” chosen to avoid thinking |
| **Social‑desirability** | Under‑reporting undesirable behavior | Skipping lectures |
| **Recall** | Memory errors | Reporting first sexual experience |

> [!example] **National Service poll** – leading question “Do you think NS is a waste of time?” steers respondents, creating bias.

## 3. Noise vs. Bias
- **Noise** = random sampling variability; reduced by larger samples.  
- **Bias** = systematic deviation; persists unless sampling or estimation method is corrected.

## 4. Evaluating a Generalization (step‑by‑step)
1. **Define the population** (who you want to infer about).  
2. **Identify the sample** actually measured.  
3. **Check sample size** relative to population (large enough?).  
4. **Spot bias sources** (environmental, self‑selection, questionnaire).  
5. **Measure effect magnitude** (is the observed difference practically important?).  
6. **Decide**: inference is reasonable **or** a hasty/generalization fallacy.

> [!info] **Hasty generalization** – conclusion drawn from a small or biased sample, regardless of effect size.

## 5. Illustrative examples
- **Diet study**: evaluating only people who lost weight ignores failures → over‑optimistic claim.  
- **Personal dating claim**: “All women I date are insensitive” → self‑selection + possible environmental bias.  
- **TripAdvisor ratings**: reviewers self‑select; satisfied customers over‑represented → self‑selection bias.  
- **Guardian article**: 13 000 US women (18‑44) report 1 in 16 forced first sexual experience. Large sample supports inference, but self‑selection & social desirability may **under‑estimate** true rate.  

## 6. Stereotyping, prejudice & valid inductive reasoning
- Stereotypes become **prejudice** when based on unjustified or uncogent generalizations.  
- Valid inductive generalizations require sound evidence (adequate, unbiased sample).  
- Example of proper inductive reasoning: “Most philosophers drink; Steven is a philosopher; therefore Steven likely drinks.” – relies on a justified statistical claim about the whole class.

> [!warning] **Unjustified stereotype** → prejudice (“R‑word”) → logical and ethical error.

## 7. Practical checklist for researchers
- Randomly select participants whenever possible.  
- Aim for a sample size that yields a low standard error (use power analysis or rule of thumb).  
- Pre‑test questionnaire for omission, wording, and response‑style biases.  
- Report both **sample size** and **potential bias sources** in findings.  
- When interpreting results, separate **statistical significance** (noise) from **practical relevance** (effect size).

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Population** | The full set of entities you wish to infer about. |
| **Sample** | Subset actually measured; should be representative. |
| **Estimator** | Statistic computed from the sample to approximate a population parameter. |
| **Bias** | Systematic error causing consistent over‑ or under‑estimation. |
| **Noise (sampling variability)** | Random error due to different possible samples. |
| **Survivorship bias** | Distortion from analyzing only those that “survived” a selection process. |
| **Self‑selection bias** | Distortion when participants choose to take part, often aligning with the study’s premise. |
| **Hasty generalization** | Faulty inference from an insufficient or biased sample. |
| **Social desirability bias** | Tendency to answer in a way that will be viewed favorably by others. |
