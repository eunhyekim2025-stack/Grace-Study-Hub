# Seminar 1 — What Critical Thinking Is

> [[index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · Seminar 1 (Sovan Patra) · Pairs with textbook **Introduction + Ch.1** → [[ctrw-ch01-basic-concepts-of-reasoning]]

---

> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - A critical thinker is **a reasonable person** — someone who actively asks whether they have reasons, whether those reasons are good, and who knows they are asking.
> - The course gives you a **toolkit** (good reasons vs bad) plus an **attitude** that makes you use it by instinct. Neither works alone.
> - Critical thinking is **domain neutral**: what makes a reason good in economics makes it good in accounting or computer science.
> - It is **not** common sense. Our intuitions are systematically biased, and three classroom tests show them failing.
> - Only **statements** (truth-bearing sentences) enter the machinery. Sorting statements from non-statements, and clear language from vague/ambiguous language, is the first real skill.
> - An **argument** = premises + inference + conclusion. Contradiction, repetition, abuse, description and bare conditionals are not arguments.

## 1 · What critical thinking is

> [!quote] The formal definition (Scriven & Paul, 2008)
> "Critical thinking is the intellectually disciplined process of actively and skillfully **conceptualizing, applying, analyzing, synthesizing, and/or evaluating** information gathered from, or generated by, observation, experience, reflection, reasoning, or communication, **as a guide to belief and action**. In its exemplary form, it is based on **universal intellectual values that transcend subject matter divisions**: clarity, accuracy, precision, consistency, relevance, sound evidence, good reasons, depth, breadth, and fairness."

Two phrases in that mouthful are doing the real work:

- **"as a guide to belief and action"** — the target is not tidy thinking for its own sake. It is what you end up *believing* and *doing*.
- **"universal intellectual values that transcend subject matter divisions"** — this is domain neutrality (§3) stated as a checklist. The ten values: **clarity · accuracy · precision · consistency · relevance · sound evidence · good reasons · depth · breadth · fairness**.

### The three active moves

When you are thinking critically — deciding what you know and what else you can believe:

| # | Move | What it rules out |
|---|------|-------------------|
| 1 | You actively consider **whether you have reasons** for the beliefs you hold | belief by default, inheritance, habit |
| 2 | You actively ask **whether those reasons are good** | reasons that merely feel satisfying |
| 3 | You are **aware that you are doing 1 and 2** | doing it accidentally, unable to repeat it |

> [!info] Why move 1 is unavoidable
> Can we go through life *without* beliefs? No — impossible. Since you cannot opt out of having beliefs, the only live question is whether you have reasons for them. That is where critical thinking starts.

Move 3 means critical thinking is not merely cognitive but **metacognitive** — you watch yourself thinking while you think.

## 2 · Toolkit + attitude

- The **toolkit** puts you in a position to distinguish good reasons from bad ones. *Why* is one reason good and another bad — that is the 13 weeks.
- The **attitude** makes you go to the toolkit by instinct: an inclination to head for the truth without being told to.

> [!warning] The seminar's own framing
> A toolkit you have no inclination to use has no value. The course can hand you the tools; generating the instinct is the harder half, and it is what the labs are for.

## 3 · Domain neutrality

The rules that make a reason good in economics are the same rules that make it good in computer science, accounting or business. Good-vs-bad reasoning **does not depend on the discipline** — which is why critical thinking sits in the general-education basket rather than inside one major.

## 4 · What critical thinking is *not*

| Confusion | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **Accumulating information** | Someone excellent at memorising may be terrible at reasoning |
| **Being argumentative** | Wanting to win at any cost is not critical thinking |
| **Being harshly critical of others** | Critical thinking also *strengthens* arguments, finds best solutions, and cooperates toward shared goals |

## 5 · "…in the Real World"

The qualifier does real work. The course:

- focuses on beliefs about **the world we actually live in**, and on navigating everyday life;
- takes seriously that the real world is **messy**, largely because ordinary language is messy;
- therefore teaches **judgement** alongside logic — not logic alone.

> [!info] Art as much as science
> Real-world critical thinking is rule-bound (science) *and* interpretive (art). When context decides whether a question is rhetorical, or how vague a word is being used, only judgement and charity settle it.

## 6 · Why bother — the payoffs

Three layers, from the slides:

- **Extrinsic** — employers rank communication, problem-solving and critical thinking above technical skills.
- **Intrinsic** — the value of holding beliefs you can actually defend.
- **Social** — a population of reasonable people argues differently. The slides make the point bluntly with electoral examples.

> [!quote] The extrinsic case, as the slide puts it
> "Year after year, the skills employers value most in the new graduates they hire are **not technical, job specific skills**, but written and oral communication, problem-solving, and critical thinking."
> — Wilson Peden, *Fortune*, 2015

## 7 · Common sense is not critical thinking

Three tests were run on the room. Each one is a place where untrained intuition reliably fails.

> [!example] Test 1 — Emma (conjunction)
> Emma: single, 30, bright, outspoken; philosophy degree, dissertation on a just society; wrote on discrimination, social justice, global warming, nuclear disarmament. Which is **more probable**?
> **A**: Emma works at a bank. **B**: Emma works at a bank *and* is active in the feminist movement.
>
> **A.** B is a *subset* of A — every world where B holds is a world where A holds, so P(A) ≥ P(B) always. Intuition picks B because B *resembles* the description. That resemblance is the **representativeness heuristic**, and preferring the conjunction is the **conjunction fallacy**.

> [!example] Test 2 — the cards (conditional testing)
> Four cards are face up: **A · K · 2 · 7**. Each has a number on one side and a letter on the other. Claim: *"If a card has an A on one side, it has a 2 on the other."* Which cards must you turn to show the claim is **false**?
>
> **A and 7.**
>
> | Card | Turn it? | Why |
> |---|---|---|
> | **A** | ✅ | if the reverse is not a 2, the claim is dead |
> | **K** | ❌ | the claim says nothing about non-A cards |
> | **2** | ❌ | the claim never said *only* A's have 2s — an A or a K behind it are both fine |
> | **7** | ✅ | if the reverse is an A, the claim is dead |
>
> Most people turn **A and 2** — they go hunting for the case that *confirms* the rule instead of the case that could *break* it. (This is the Wason selection task; it is also why falsification, not confirmation, is the test that counts.)

> [!example] Test 3 — the disease (base rates)
> Prevalence 0.1%. No false negatives. False-positive rate 5%. A resident tests positive, no other information. Chance they have the disease?
>
> Take 1,000 people: **1** has it and tests positive; of the 999 who don't, 5% ≈ **50** test positive. So P(disease | positive) = 1 / 51 ≈ **2%**. Intuition says something near 95% because it drops the **base rate** entirely.

> [!warning] The moral
> Common sense is heuristic — rule-of-thumb thinking that was an efficient use of cognitive capacity for hunter-gatherer ancestors. Natural selection biased it toward **imitation**, **avoiding cognitive dissonance**, **deferring to successful others**, **neglecting baselines**, and **non-abstract situations**. Sometimes common sense and critical thinking reach the same place. You cannot tell which case you're in from the inside.

## 8 · Nuts and bolts: sentences vs statements

- A **sentence** ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark, and is meaningful/grammatical.
- A **statement** (= claim, proposition) is a special sentence: it has the capacity to be **either true or false, but not both**.
- **Test:** does *"Is it true that ___?"* come out as a meaningful sentence? If yes, it's a statement.

Non-statements: requests ("Please pass the salt"), orders ("Tie your shoelaces"), genuine questions ("How old are you?"), exhortations ("Let's get there on time!").

> [!info] Why this matters
> Critical thinking is interested only in statements, because it is interested in **truth**, and only statements can bear truth.

### Drill — statement or not?

The seminar's own list. Run the *"Is it true that ___?"* test on each; the last two rows are the ones people get wrong.

| Sentence | Statement? | Why |
|---|:---:|---|
| All men are mortal. | ✅ | plainly true-or-false |
| Socrates is a man. | ✅ | |
| The sky is overcast. | ✅ | empirical, checkable |
| Rich people must pay more taxes. | ✅ | **normative, but still truth-apt** — *"Is it true that rich people must pay more taxes?"* is a meaningful question |
| People who think Taylor Swift is better than Beethoven should be shot. | ✅ | ditto — being outrageous is not the same as being incapable of truth |
| Please pass me the salt. | ❌ | request |
| Tie your shoelaces. | ❌ | order |
| How old are you? | ❌ | genuine question — *unless* it is rhetorical (below) |
| Let's get there on time! | ❌ | exhortation |
| People are such fools! | ✅ | **an exclamation mark does not disqualify it** — the content is still true or false |

> [!warning] The two traps in that table
> 1. **"Ought" claims are statements.** Normative content does not make a sentence truth-less; if it did, moral reasoning would be impossible from the start (see §9).
> 2. **Punctuation does not decide.** *"People are such fools!"* is a statement; *"How old are you?"* may or may not be. Grammar is a hint, not the test.

### Rhetorical questions

A rhetorical question is a **statement in disguise** and should be treated as one — *"Should you be talking to me like that?"* asserts that you should not. But questions often *are* genuine, and some are genuinely unclear (*"How old are you?"*, *"Why did you cheat on me?"*). When it's unclear, fall back on context — tonal, situational — and on charity.

## 9 · Facts and opinions

**Opinion** — what I think the world is (or ought to be) like:

- its content takes the form of a statement;
- it can be meaningfully prefaced with *"I think that…"* / *"In my judgement…"*;
- it concerns something you cannot directly know;
- sincerely expressed, the speaker takes it to be true — so an opinion is a **subjectively true statement**, which may or may not also be a fact.

**Facts** — a working typology:

| Class | Sub-class | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Private** (about me) | affect | "I am feeling cold" |
| | perception | "I see a red wall" |
| | cognitive/epistemic commitment | "I believe immigrants are eating pets" |
| | preference | "I love watching Rafa play" |
| **Non-private** (about the world) | conceptual objects | "A bachelor is unmarried" |
| | empirical phenomena | "The sky is overcast" |

> [!info] The asymmetry to remember
> Any time we express an opinion we express a **fact about ourselves**. The converse does not hold — stating a fact is not thereby stating an opinion.

### Drill — private fact or opinion?

| Utterance | Private fact | Opinion |
|---|---|---|
| "I believe that immigrants are eating pet cats and dogs." | ✅ that I hold this belief — an epistemic commitment I have direct access to | the *embedded* claim (immigrants are eating pets) is an opinion, and a checkable one |
| "I love watching Rafa play." | ✅ preference | — |
| "I am feeling cold / in pain." | ✅ affect — nobody can correct me about it | — |
| "I love Emma." | ✅ affect | — |
| "Emma loves me." | — | ✅ someone **else's** inner state; I cannot directly know it |
| "It is morally wrong that girls don't go to school in Afghanistan." | — | ✅ normative — and this is where the open question bites |
| "I think that if you are a bachelor, you are not married." | ✅ that I think it | the embedded claim is a **necessarily true** non-private fact (conceptual) |

The sharp pair is **"I love Emma"** vs **"Emma loves me."** Same verb, same two people — but the first reports something I have direct access to and the second does not. Direct access is what separates a private fact from an opinion.

> [!question] Can there be moral / normative facts?
> The seminar leaves this open, deliberately. *"It is morally wrong that girls don't go to school in Afghanistan"* is clearly a statement (§8), and clearly not a private fact about me. So is it an opinion that happens to be very widely shared, or is it a **fact about the world** of a non-empirical kind? Your answer decides how much moral reasoning can accomplish. Picked up again in [[ctrw-ch11-practical-applications]].

### When is an opinion likely to be true?

- Some opinions are **necessarily true** (conceptual truths).
- Some we accept because there is **consensus among reasonable, well-informed people** — which immediately raises: how much agreement counts, who is reasonable, and how much information makes someone well-informed. (The course returns to "who is reasonable" in the final session.)
- Others we accept because we accept the **justification offered** for them.

> [!todo] Set reading — "You are not entitled to your own opinion"
> The slide sets this piece and one question to bring back: **when is a claim worth considering as a "maybe fact"?** The headline point is that having the *right to express* an opinion is not the same as having an opinion that *deserves a hearing* — the second has to be earned with reasons.

> [!example] Live case — Meta ends third-party fact-checking (Jan 2025)
> The deck drops in Meta's announcement ("More Speech, Fewer Mistakes", replacing third-party fact-checkers with Community Notes) and asks flatly: **what is the problem?**
>
> It lands exactly on the machinery above. If an opinion counts as likely true when there is **consensus among reasonable, well-informed people**, then handing the adjudication to whoever shows up dissolves both qualifiers — no filter for *reasonable*, none for *well-informed*. But the opposite arrangement has a cost too: a small panel of designated fact-checkers is exactly the "who counts as reasonable, and who appointed them?" problem in institutional form. The seminar does not settle it; it returns to "who is reasonable" in the final session.

## 10 · Clarity: vagueness vs ambiguity

| | **Vagueness** | **Ambiguity** |
|---|---|---|
| Defect | the word has **no precise meaning** | the word/phrase/statement has **multiple meanings** |
| Shape | the property sits on a **continuum**; unclear when there is *enough* of it | discrete rival readings |
| Examples | bald, tall, dark, fast, big | "stoned at a rally", "killed the man with an axe" |

Ambiguity comes in three types:

- **Lexical (semantic)** — one word, several senses: *"Lawrence Wong was stoned at a rally in Hougang."*
- **Structural (syntactic)** — the grammar permits two parses: *"The police killed the man with an axe."*
- **Situational (pragmatic)** — context decides the force: *"How old are you?"*

More of the deck's specimens, sorted:

| Specimen | Defect | Reading(s) |
|---|---|---|
| "NBA players are tall." | **vague** — but harmlessly so | nobody is in the borderline zone |
| "Stefan is bald." · "Boxers are big dogs." · "Shanthi Pereira is fast." | **vague**, and it bites | how much hair / how big / how fast is *enough*? |
| "Sovan drinks every evening." | **lexical** | drinks *what* — water, or alcohol? |
| "The dog is ready to eat." | **structural** | the dog is hungry, or the dog is dinner |
| "The woman left the dog in the rain to drink beer." | **structural** | who is drinking the beer — the woman or the dog? |
| "I love you too, Emma!" | **structural** | as well as loving someone else / as well as someone else loving you / in addition to other feelings |
| "How old are you?" | **situational (pragmatic)** | a genuine request for your age, or a reproach |

> [!warning] Why unclarity is a logic problem, not a style problem
> A vague or ambiguous statement has **no settled truth value** — so it cannot do honest work as a premise or a conclusion. Worse, an ambiguous sentence can be **true on one reading and false on another**, which is exactly the crack an *equivocation* is driven into (→ [[ctrw-ch07-fallacies]]).
>
> **When does the problem with vagueness disappear?** When the case is **not a borderline one**. "Stefan is bald" is useless if Stefan has thinning hair; it is perfectly serviceable if Stefan has no hair at all. Vagueness only bites at the edges — which is why "NBA players are tall" causes nobody any trouble.

Open questions the seminar leaves you with: can a word be both vague and ambiguous? Do the two exhaust unclarity? And *why* is ordinary language unclear — **cognitive efficiency** (precision is expensive and usually unnecessary), or **the social value of loose talk** (the "ummm", language as social grooming rather than information transfer)? The answer to "what should we do about it" is: **it depends** — on whether the borderline case is the one you are actually arguing about.

> [!note] In-class activity — *Descripto!*
> "Learning clarity through action" — a description exercise run in the seminar. The point of doing it as an activity rather than a lecture: you only feel how underspecified ordinary description is when someone else has to act on yours.

→ Developed fully in [[ctrw-ch10-definitions]].

## 11 · The backbone: the argument

Having or giving reasons for a claim is called **arguing**.

> [!info] Definition
> An **argument** is a collection of statements such that the truth of some statement(s) in the collection provides support (are reasons) for the truth — or likely truth — of other statement(s) in it.
> - the supporting statements are the **premises**;
> - the supported statements are the **conclusions**;
> - the support relation itself is the **inference**.

```
Premise(s)
   │  (inference)
   ▼
Conclusion(s)
```

### Inference indicators

The first skill is *identifying* an argument: does this passage contain one, and if so what are its premises, conclusions and inference structure? Well-articulated arguments signpost themselves:

| Premise indicators | Conclusion indicators |
|---|---|
| because, as, for, owing to the fact that, since | therefore, hence, thus, so, it follows that, as such, in conclusion |

### What is *not* an argument

- **Mere contradiction** — "No, you're wrong. This is a mistake. I disagree."
- **Repetition of the conclusion** — a politician staying on message.
- **Abuse of the opponent** — "You'd have to be an idiot to believe that."

> [!warning] Conditionals are not arguments
> *"If it rained, the streets will be wet"* asserts that the truth of one statement is a **condition** for the truth of another. You can ask whether *that* assertion is true — so it is a (conditional) **statement**, not an argument. Contrast *"**Since** it rained, the streets will be wet"*, which does argue.
>
> In *If A then B*: **A** is the **antecedent**, **B** the **consequent**.

**The slide's fill-in-the-blank, filled in:**

| Form | It claims | The condition is |
|---|---|---|
| **If A then B** | A's truth is **enough** to get you B — though B might also arrive by other routes | **A is sufficient for B** |
| **Only if A then B** | without A you never get B — though A on its own may not be enough | **A is necessary for B** |

The deck's own example: *"Only if I have my whisky every evening, I am truly happy"* — the whisky is **necessary** for the happiness, not sufficient. Plenty of whisky-filled evenings could still be miserable.

> [!warning] Write it the standard way
> *"Only if A then B"* is the slide's phrasing, but it is **non-standard and reads ambiguously**. The textbook form — and the one to use in an exam answer — is *"**B only if A**"*, i.e. **B → A**, i.e. A is necessary for B. Same content, but the standard form makes the direction of the arrow unmistakable. Worked out in full in [[ctrw-ch05-forms-of-argument]].

## 12 · Drill — "got argue or not?"

The single most-tested skill of the week: given a passage, decide **first** whether there is an argument at all, and only then what its premises and conclusion are. Note that identifying an argument is a separate job from judging whether it is a *good* one — Ex.2 below is a bad argument, but an argument all the same.

| # | Passage | Argument? | Why |
|:--:|---|:---:|---|
| 1 | "Thou shall not covet thy neighbour's husband." | ❌ | a **command** — not even a statement (§8), so it cannot be a premise or a conclusion |
| 2 | "Lying is wrong **since** the Bible says it is wrong." | ✅ | premise indicator *since*. Premise: the Bible says lying is wrong → Conclusion: lying is wrong. (Whether that reason is any **good** is a different question) |
| 3 | "Trump's tariffs **entail** that the Fed is not going to cut interest rates soon." | ❌ | one statement that **reports** an inference instead of **performing** one. *"Is it true that the tariffs entail that?"* is meaningful → it is a statement, like a conditional. Compare: *"Trump has imposed tariffs, **so** the Fed won't cut soon"* — that argues |
| 4 | "Emma loves me! She really really does! There is no way she doesn't!" | ❌ | **repetition** of the conclusion, three times, with rising volume |
| 5 | "What you are saying is downright silly and wrong. It does not matter how many times you say it." | ❌ | **contradiction + abuse** |
| 6 | "For the crises in the Middle East to end, either Trump or Netanyahu must go. If both go, then even better." | ❌ | two conditional/necessary-condition **statements**. Conditions are stated; no reason is offered for any conclusion |
| 7 | "No one can be a fan of Doja Cat if they buy sitting tickets for her concert. You can't enjoy Doja if you are not moving your body!" | ✅ | no indicator word, but the inference is there. Premise: enjoying Doja requires moving your body → Conclusion: sitting-ticket buyers are not fans. (Suppressed premise: being a fan requires enjoying her) |
| 8 | "I don't know whether to date Mona or Emma. Mona is beautiful but Emma is not; Emma is rich but Mona is not. I must date one of them, but I'd rather date someone both beautiful and rich." | ⚖️ | **the contested one.** Read literally, it is a description of a predicament with no conclusion drawn. Read charitably, sentence 1 **is** the conclusion (*I can't decide*) and the rest are the reasons. Charity says take the reading on which there is something to evaluate |
| 9 | Geoff Dyer's beach passage (2003) — "We hit the beach and fanned out… I was never able to lose myself in the music, which was really quite idiotic." | ❌ | **narration + evaluation**, not argument. It reports what happened and how he felt; the closest thing to a reason ("which was really quite idiotic") **explains** his reaction rather than offering evidence for a disputed claim |

> [!tip] The four things that masquerade as arguments
> **Commands** (no truth value) · **conditionals and entailment-reports** (one statement, no inference performed) · **repetition** (no new statement) · **description and narration** (nothing is being established). Add the seminar's own three — contradiction, repetition, abuse — and you have the whole rogues' gallery.

> [!example] The set-piece — Monty Python's *Argument Clinic*
> The "how many arguments?" exercise is run on the sketch: a man pays for an argument and is met with flat contradiction. It dramatises §11's definition better than any definition can — **"an argument is a connected series of statements intended to establish a proposition"**, and contradiction, however confidently delivered, is not one. Answer the question by counting **inferences**, not exchanges.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Critical thinking** | Actively asking whether you have reasons for a belief, whether they are good, while aware you are doing so |
| **Domain neutral** | The standards for a good reason do not vary by discipline |
| **Metacognitive awareness** | Watching yourself think while you think |
| **Statement** | A sentence capable of being true or false, but not both — the only input critical thinking accepts |
| **Opinion** | A subjectively true statement about something you cannot directly know; expressing one states a fact about yourself |
| **Vagueness** | A word has no precise meaning because its property lies on a continuum |
| **Ambiguity** | A word, phrase or statement carries multiple meanings — lexical, structural or situational |
| **Argument** | Premises + inference + conclusion; not contradiction, repetition, abuse, description or a bare conditional |
| **Private fact** | Something about me I have direct access to — affect, perception, belief, preference. "I love Emma" is one; "Emma loves me" is not |
| **Rhetorical question** | A statement in disguise; treat it as the statement it asserts |
| **Inference indicator** | A word flagging that an inference is happening — premise (*because, since, for, as*) or conclusion (*therefore, hence, thus, so*) |
| **Antecedent / consequent** | In *If A then B*: A is the antecedent, B the consequent. *If* makes A **sufficient**; *only if* makes A **necessary** |
