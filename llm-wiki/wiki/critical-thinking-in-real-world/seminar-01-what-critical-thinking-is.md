# Seminar 1 — What Critical Thinking Is

> [[index|← Critical Thinking in Real World]] · Seminar 1 (Sovan Patra) · Pairs with textbook **Introduction + Ch.1** → [[ctrw-ch01-basic-concepts-of-reasoning]]

---

> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - A critical thinker is **a reasonable person** — someone who actively asks whether they have reasons, whether those reasons are good, and who knows they are asking.
> - The course gives you a **toolkit** (good reasons vs bad) plus an **attitude** that makes you use it by instinct. Neither works alone.
> - Critical thinking is **domain neutral**: what makes a reason good in economics makes it good in accounting or computer science.
> - It is **not** common sense. Our intuitions are systematically biased, and three classroom tests show them failing.
> - Only **statements** (truth-bearing sentences) enter the machinery. Sorting statements from non-statements, and clear language from vague/ambiguous language, is the first real skill.
> - An **argument** = premises + inference + conclusion. Contradiction, repetition and abuse are not arguments.

## 1 · The three active moves

When you are thinking critically — deciding what you know and what else you can believe:

| # | Move | What it rules out |
|---|------|-------------------|
| 1 | You actively consider **whether you have reasons** for the beliefs you hold | belief by default, inheritance, habit |
| 2 | You actively ask **whether those reasons are good** | reasons that merely feel satisfying |
| 3 | You are **aware that you are doing 1 and 2** | doing it accidentally, unable to repeat it |

> [!info] Why move 1 is unavoidable
> Can we go through life *without* beliefs? No — impossible. Since you cannot opt out of having beliefs, the only live question is whether you have reasons for them. That is where critical thinking starts.

Move 3 means critical thinking is not merely cognitive but **metacognitive** — you watch yourself thinking while you think.

## 2 · Toolkit + attitude

- The **toolkit** puts you in a position to distinguish good reasons from bad ones. *Why* is one reason good and another bad — that is the 13 weeks.
- The **attitude** makes you go to the toolkit by instinct: an inclination to head for the truth without being told to.

> [!warning] The seminar's own framing
> A toolkit you have no inclination to use has no value. The course can hand you the tools; generating the instinct is the harder half, and it is what the labs are for.

## 3 · Domain neutrality

The rules that make a reason good in economics are the same rules that make it good in computer science, accounting or business. Good-vs-bad reasoning **does not depend on the discipline** — which is why critical thinking sits in the general-education basket rather than inside one major.

## 4 · What critical thinking is *not*

| Confusion | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| **Accumulating information** | Someone excellent at memorising may be terrible at reasoning |
| **Being argumentative** | Wanting to win at any cost is not critical thinking |
| **Being harshly critical of others** | Critical thinking also *strengthens* arguments, finds best solutions, and cooperates toward shared goals |

## 5 · "…in the Real World"

The qualifier does real work. The course:

- focuses on beliefs about **the world we actually live in**, and on navigating everyday life;
- takes seriously that the real world is **messy**, largely because ordinary language is messy;
- therefore teaches **judgement** alongside logic — not logic alone.

> [!info] Art as much as science
> Real-world critical thinking is rule-bound (science) *and* interpretive (art). When context decides whether a question is rhetorical, or how vague a word is being used, only judgement and charity settle it.

## 6 · Why bother — the payoffs

Three layers, from the slides:

- **Extrinsic** — employers consistently rank communication, problem-solving and critical thinking above technical skills; the WEF *Future of Jobs 2025* report puts "analytic thinking" at the top precisely because AI is expected to obsolete many domain-specific skills while higher-order thinking becomes the adaptive advantage.
- **Intrinsic** — the value of holding beliefs you can actually defend.
- **Social** — a population of reasonable people argues differently. The slides make the point bluntly with electoral examples.

## 7 · Common sense is not critical thinking

Three tests were run on the room. Each one is a place where untrained intuition reliably fails.

> [!example] Test 1 — Emma (conjunction)
> Emma: single, 30, bright, outspoken; philosophy degree, dissertation on a just society; wrote on discrimination, social justice, global warming, nuclear disarmament. Which is **more probable**?
> **A**: Emma works at a bank. **B**: Emma works at a bank *and* is active in the feminist movement.
>
> **A.** B is a *subset* of A — every world where B holds is a world where A holds, so P(A) ≥ P(B) always. Intuition picks B because B *resembles* the description. That resemblance is the **representativeness heuristic**, and preferring the conjunction is the **conjunction fallacy**.

> [!example] Test 2 — the cards (conditional testing)
> Cards have a number on one side, a letter on the other. Claim: *"If a card has an A on one side, it has a 2 on the other."* Which cards must you turn to show the claim is **false**?
>
> Turn the **A** card (if the reverse is not 2, the claim dies) and the card showing a **number that is not 2** (if the reverse is an A, the claim dies). Do **not** turn the 2 card — the claim never said only A's have 2s — nor a non-A letter. Most people turn A and 2, i.e. they hunt for confirmation instead of falsification.

> [!example] Test 3 — the disease (base rates)
> Prevalence 0.1%. No false negatives. False-positive rate 5%. A resident tests positive, no other information. Chance they have the disease?
>
> Take 1,000 people: **1** has it and tests positive; of the 999 who don't, 5% ≈ **50** test positive. So P(disease | positive) = 1 / 51 ≈ **2%**. Intuition says something near 95% because it drops the **base rate** entirely.

> [!warning] The moral
> Common sense is heuristic — rule-of-thumb thinking that was an efficient use of cognitive capacity for hunter-gatherer ancestors. Natural selection biased it toward **imitation**, **avoiding cognitive dissonance**, **deferring to successful others**, **neglecting baselines**, and **non-abstract situations**. Sometimes common sense and critical thinking reach the same place. You cannot tell which case you're in from the inside.

## 8 · Nuts and bolts: sentences vs statements

- A **sentence** ends with a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark, and is meaningful/grammatical.
- A **statement** (= claim, proposition) is a special sentence: it has the capacity to be **either true or false, but not both**.
- **Test:** does *"Is it true that ___?"* come out as a meaningful sentence? If yes, it's a statement.

Non-statements: requests ("Please pass the salt"), orders ("Tie your shoelaces"), genuine questions ("How old are you?"), exhortations ("Let's get there on time!").

> [!info] Why this matters
> Critical thinking is interested only in statements, because it is interested in **truth**, and only statements can bear truth.

### Rhetorical questions

A rhetorical question is a **statement in disguise** and should be treated as one — *"Should you be talking to me like that?"* asserts that you should not. But questions often *are* genuine, and some are genuinely unclear (*"How old are you?"*, *"Why did you cheat on me?"*). When it's unclear, fall back on context — tonal, situational — and on charity.

## 9 · Facts and opinions

**Opinion** — what I think the world is (or ought to be) like:

- its content takes the form of a statement;
- it can be meaningfully prefaced with *"I think that…"* / *"In my judgement…"*;
- it concerns something you cannot directly know;
- sincerely expressed, the speaker takes it to be true — so an opinion is a **subjectively true statement**, which may or may not also be a fact.

**Facts** — a working typology:

| Class | Sub-class | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Private** (about me) | affect | "I am feeling cold" |
| | perception | "I see a red wall" |
| | cognitive/epistemic commitment | "I believe immigrants are eating pets" |
| | preference | "I love watching Rafa play" |
| **Non-private** (about the world) | conceptual objects | "A bachelor is unmarried" |
| | empirical phenomena | "The sky is overcast" |

> [!info] The asymmetry to remember
> Any time we express an opinion we express a **fact about ourselves**. The converse does not hold — stating a fact is not thereby stating an opinion.

### When is an opinion likely to be true?

- Some opinions are **necessarily true** (conceptual truths).
- Some we accept because there is **consensus among reasonable, well-informed people** — which immediately raises: how much agreement counts, who is reasonable, and how much information makes someone well-informed. (The course returns to "who is reasonable" in the final session.)
- Others we accept because we accept the **justification offered** for them.

## 10 · Clarity: vagueness vs ambiguity

| | **Vagueness** | **Ambiguity** |
|---|---|---|
| Defect | the word has **no precise meaning** | the word/phrase/statement has **multiple meanings** |
| Shape | the property sits on a **continuum**; unclear when there is *enough* of it | discrete rival readings |
| Examples | bald, tall, dark, fast, big | "stoned at a rally", "killed the man with an axe" |

Ambiguity comes in three types:

- **Lexical (semantic)** — one word, several senses: *"Lawrence Wong was stoned at a rally in Hougang."*
- **Structural (syntactic)** — the grammar permits two parses: *"The police killed the man with an axe."*
- **Situational (pragmatic)** — context decides the force: *"How old are you?"*

Open questions the seminar leaves you with: can a word be both vague and ambiguous? Do the two exhaust unclarity? And *why* is ordinary language unclear — cognitive efficiency, or the social value of loose talk? The answer to "what should we do about it" is: **it depends**.

## 11 · The backbone: the argument

Having or giving reasons for a claim is called **arguing**.

> [!info] Definition
> An **argument** is a collection of statements such that the truth of some statement(s) in the collection provides support (are reasons) for the truth — or likely truth — of other statement(s) in it.
> - the supporting statements are the **premises**;
> - the supported statements are the **conclusions**;
> - the support relation itself is the **inference**.

```
Premise(s)
   │  (inference)
   ▼
Conclusion(s)
```

### Inference indicators

The first skill is *identifying* an argument: does this passage contain one, and if so what are its premises, conclusions and inference structure? Well-articulated arguments signpost themselves:

| Premise indicators | Conclusion indicators |
|---|---|
| because, as, for, owing to the fact that, since | therefore, hence, thus, so, it follows that, as such, in conclusion |

### What is *not* an argument

- **Mere contradiction** — "No, you're wrong. This is a mistake. I disagree."
- **Repetition of the conclusion** — a politician staying on message.
- **Abuse of the opponent** — "You'd have to be an idiot to believe that."

> [!warning] Conditionals are not arguments
> *"If it rained, the streets will be wet"* asserts that the truth of one statement is a **condition** for the truth of another. You can ask whether *that* assertion is true — so it is a (conditional) **statement**, not an argument. Contrast *"**Since** it rained, the streets will be wet"*, which does argue.
>
> In *If A then B*: **A** is the **antecedent**, **B** the **consequent**. *Only if A then B* claims something different from *If A then B* — worth working out before Ch.5. → [[ctrw-ch05-forms-of-argument]]

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Critical thinking** | Actively asking whether you have reasons for a belief, whether they are good, while aware you are doing so |
| **Domain neutral** | The standards for a good reason do not vary by discipline |
| **Metacognitive awareness** | Watching yourself think while you think |
| **Statement** | A sentence capable of being true or false, but not both — the only input critical thinking accepts |
| **Opinion** | A subjectively true statement about something you cannot directly know; expressing one states a fact about yourself |
| **Vagueness** | A word has no precise meaning because its property lies on a continuum |
| **Ambiguity** | A word, phrase or statement carries multiple meanings — lexical, structural or situational |
| **Argument** | Premises + inference + conclusion; not contradiction, repetition or abuse |
