> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Conditional chains (hypothetical syllogism) let you link “if‑then” statements: P→Q, Q→R ⇒ P→R.  
> - Contrapositive swaps and negates: P→Q ⇔ ¬Q→¬P, revealing sufficiency ↔ necessity.  
> - Disjunctive syllogism (P∨Q, ¬P ⟹ Q) is always valid; affirming a disjunct is a fallacy unless exclusivity is explicit.  
> - Constructive and destructive dilemmas combine two conditionals with a disjunction to yield a new disjunction.  
> - Validity depends only on logical form; soundness adds the requirement that every premise is true.

## 1. Conditional chaining & truth tables
- **Hypothetical syllogism**:  
  > [!info] *If P then Q* and *If Q then R* ⇒ *If P then R* (valid).  
- Example: rain → wet streets; wet streets → slippery → rain → slippery.  
- With three atomic statements (P, Q, R) a truth‑table needs **2³ = 8** rows to test validity.

## 2. Contrapositive & sufficiency/necessity
- **Contrapositive (transposition)**:  
  > [!info] From *If P then Q* infer *If not Q then not P* (always valid).  
- **Sufficient condition**: P sufficient for Q ⇔ *If P then Q*.  
- **Necessary condition**: Q necessary for P ⇔ *If P then Q* (or *If not Q then not P*).  
- **If and only if (↔)**: both directions hold; P ↔ Q = (P→Q) ∧ (Q→P).  

## 3. Converting “only if” & “unless”
| Natural language | Standard form |
|------------------|---------------|
| P **only if** Q | If P then Q |
| P **unless** Q  | If not Q then P |

> [!example]  
> *“Unless drugs damage society, we should legalize them.”* → *If drugs do **not** damage society → we should legalize them*.

## 4. Disjunctive reasoning
- **Disjunctive syllogism (DS)**:  
  > [!info] Premises: P ∨ Q, ¬P → conclude Q (valid).  
- **Inclusive “or”** (standard): both disjuncts may be true; DS works regardless.  
- **Exclusive “or”**: only one can be true; affects truth of premises, not DS validity.  
- **Fallacy – affirming the disjunct**:  
  > [!warning] From P ∨ Q and P infer ¬Q (invalid unless exclusivity is guaranteed).  

## 5. Dilemmas
- **Constructive dilemma**:  
  > [!info] (P→R) ∧ (Q→S) ∧ (P∨Q) ⇒ (R∨S).  
- **Destructive dilemma**:  
  > [!info] (P→R) ∧ (Q→S) ∧ (¬R∨¬S) ⇒ (¬P∨¬Q).  
- Both rely on **Modus Ponens** (P→R, P ⊢ R) and **Modus Tollens** (P→R, ¬R ⊢ ¬P).

## 6. Validity vs. Soundness
- **Validity**: conclusion follows from premises *solely* by form.  
- **Soundness**: validity **+** all premises are true → conclusion guaranteed true.  
- **Unsound**: any false premise makes the argument unsound, even if the form is valid.  

> [!warning] Do not confuse a true premise with a valid form; validity is assessed with truth‑tables or recognized patterns, independent of factual content.

## 7. Practical workflow for “unless” problems
1. Rewrite “unless” as an *if‑then* statement.  
2. Assign a distinct propositional symbol to each atomic claim.  
3. Identify the conditional structure.  
4. Apply a known rule (e.g., modus ponens) to derive the conclusion.  

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Hypothetical syllogism** | Chain of two conditionals yielding a new conditional (P→Q, Q→R ⟹ P→R). |
| **Contrapositive** | Logical equivalent: P→Q ⇔ ¬Q→¬P. |
| **Disjunctive syllogism** | From P∨Q and ¬P infer Q (or symmetrically). |
| **Constructive dilemma** | From two conditionals and a disjunction infer a disjunction of consequents. |
| **Destructive dilemma** | From two conditionals and a disjunction of negated consequents infer a disjunction of negated antecedents. |
| **Validity** | Truth of conclusion follows from form alone. |
| **Soundness** | Validity plus all premises actually true. |
