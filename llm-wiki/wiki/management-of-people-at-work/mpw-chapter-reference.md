Built from the textbook chapter notes + the lecture decks. Figures are reconstructed from the notes — verify before quoting in an exam.

## Ch 1 — What Is Organizational Behavior?

**Theory & concepts**
- **What OB is.** The *systematic, evidence-based* study of how **individuals, groups and structure** (the three levels of analysis) affect behaviour at work — in order to make organizations more effective. It is not "soft skills": its outcome variables (performance, citizenship, withdrawal, productivity, survival) are the ones the business cares about.
- **Systematic study vs intuition.** Intuition ("I just know people") is often wrong; OB attributes cause and effect from scientific evidence. **Evidence-based management (EBM)** = decide from the best available evidence, not fads — extended today by big data and AI.
- **What managers do**, through four complementary lenses: **functions** (plan, organize, lead, control), **roles** (Mintzberg), **skills** (technical / people / conceptual), and **activities**.
- **Few absolutes / contingency.** Human behaviour rarely obeys universal laws, so the honest form of any OB claim is *"x leads to y, under conditions z."*
- **The OB model** = **inputs → processes → outcomes**, run at the three levels — the architecture of every later chapter.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Organizational behaviour (OB) | Study of the impact individuals, groups and structure have on behaviour in organizations, applied to improve effectiveness |
| Systematic study | Attributing cause and effect from scientific evidence (vs intuition) |
| Evidence-based management (EBM) | Basing managerial decisions on the best available scientific evidence |
| Contingency variables | Situational factors that moderate the relationship between two variables |
| Organizational citizenship behaviour (OCB) | Discretionary behaviour that improves the social/psychological environment, beyond the job description |
| Withdrawal behaviour | Actions taken to separate oneself from the organization (lateness, absence, turnover) |
| Productivity | Effectiveness (meeting needs) **+** efficiency (at low cost) |

**Cases & examples**
- **Hawthorne studies** — behaviour changes when people are observed; the origin of OB's "it depends" humility.
- **Gallup** engagement data linking attitudes to business results; large-scale people analytics (Amazon).

**Beyond the textbook**
- **Evidence-Based Management** (Pfeffer & Sutton) — the dedicated reading: managers should use evidence like doctors, not chase management fads; big data & AI are EBM's modern extension.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — AI is framed as a *powerful but fallible* decision tool that must be validated; it does not replace systematic evidence.

## Ch 2 — Diversity, Equity & Inclusion

**Theory & concepts**
- **Two levels of diversity.** *Surface-level* = visible characteristics (age, race, sex) that activate stereotypes; *deep-level* = values, personality, work style — what actually matters once people know each other.
- **Prejudice vs discrimination.** Prejudice is the **attitude** (a hierarchy-maintaining bias); discrimination is the **behaviour** it predicts. Both are increasingly *subtle* (microaggressions) rather than overt.
- **Four mechanisms** that turn difference into disadvantage: **social categorization** (us/them sorting), **stereotyping / stereotype threat / stigma**, **system justification & social dominance** (people rationalize existing hierarchies), and **intersectionality** (identities compound).
- **Inside groups**, diversity is hard in the short run: surface diversity raises early conflict and **fault lines** split a team into subgroups. **Functional** diversity and **time** are what make it pay off.
- **Across countries**, **Hofstede's** dimensions and **GLOBE** map how values differ; **cultural intelligence (CQ)** is the skill of functioning across them.
- **Management = three goals, not one**: **Diversity** (representation) · **Equity** (fair access despite unequal starts) · **Inclusion** (belonging). Representation alone does nothing.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Surface- / deep-level diversity | Visible, stereotype-activating traits / differences in values, personality, work preferences |
| Prejudice / implicit bias | Hierarchy-maintaining attitude toward a group / that bias held outside conscious awareness |
| Discrimination (disparate impact / treatment) | Behaviour reinforcing one group's advantage; unintentional vs intentional |
| Stereotype threat | Fear of being judged by a stereotype, which then depresses performance |
| Fault lines | Divisions splitting a group into subgroups along aligned differences |
| Hofstede's dimensions | Power distance, individualism–collectivism, masculinity–femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long/short-term orientation |
| Cultural intelligence (CQ) | The ability to function effectively in culturally diverse settings |
| Equity / inclusion | Equal access despite unequal starting points / an environment where all feel valued |
| Diversity climate | Shared perception that supportive diversity practices are **real and enforced** (not just stated) |

**Cases & examples**
- **Hofstede / IBM** — the original national value-dimension data; the **GLOBE** cross-cultural study extends it.
- **Tokenism** — a perfunctory diversity gesture that signals values without delivering inclusion.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 2 slides** pair personality with diversity and stress "*why and when* diversity brings value" — groups have **more information but use it poorly** (they over-discuss what everyone already knows; unique information disappears). Diversity pays only when the unique information is actually surfaced.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — AI *homogenises collective output* (less idea diversity across people) and can embed bias in selection (→ Ch 17).

## Ch 3 — Job Attitudes

**Theory & concepts**
- **Attitudes have an ABC structure** — *cognitive* (belief), *affective* (feeling), *behavioural* (intention). OB cares because the behavioural component drives effort, citizenship and turnover.
- **Attitudes predict behaviour**, moderated by importance, specificity, accessibility, social pressure and direct experience. Sometimes the arrow reverses — **cognitive dissonance** (we change the attitude to fit the behaviour).
- **The main job attitudes** — organizational identification, job satisfaction, job involvement, psychological empowerment, **organizational commitment** (affective / normative / continuance), **POS**, **engagement** — are **highly correlated** with one another.
- **Job satisfaction** is caused mostly by the *work itself* and *personality*, **not pay** beyond a comfortable level; it produces performance, OCB, customer satisfaction and life satisfaction.
- **Dissatisfaction** shows up on the **EVLN** grid (constructive/destructive × active/passive) and as **CWB**, absence and turnover — and managers systematically **overestimate** how satisfied their people are.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cognitive dissonance | Any incompatibility between attitudes, or between behaviour and an attitude |
| Organizational commitment | Affective ("want to stay") / normative ("ought to") / continuance ("have to") |
| Perceived organizational support (POS) | Belief that the organization values your contribution and cares about your well-being |
| Employee engagement | The degree of enthusiasm/absorption an employee feels for the job |
| EVLN | Exit (leave) · Voice (constructively improve) · Loyalty (wait optimistically) · Neglect (let it worsen) |
| Counterproductive work behaviour (CWB) | Actions that actively damage the organization |
| Embeddedness | Links to job and community that lower the probability of leaving |

**Cases & examples**
- **Salesforce** — a leukemia-stricken engineer's medical costs covered by the CEO and 350 colleagues: POS in action.
- **Molson Coors / Caterpillar** — engaged units had fewer safety incidents and grievances; **Zappos** on culture-driven satisfaction.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **Seminar 3-1** (satisfaction drivers beyond pay, nudging/workspace design, value congruence).
- **AI lecture / Amazon RTO** — a live EVLN case: surveillance and ignored feedback collapse **POS** and **affective commitment**; exit (48% job-hunting) and neglect (quiet non-compliance) follow.

## Ch 4 — Emotions & Moods

**Theory & concepts**
- **The Affective Revolution** reversed the old "keep emotion out of work" view: emotions supply *information* and guide behaviour; cold rationality fails because people are, by nature, emotional.
- **Affect / emotions / moods.** *Affect* is the umbrella; *emotions* are intense, short, event-caused; *moods* are longer, milder, diffuse — mapped on the **affective circumplex** (positive/negative × high/low activation).
- **Emotional labor** — the gap between **felt** and **displayed** emotions (display rules), managed by **surface acting** (faking) or **deep acting** (genuinely re-feeling); the cost is **emotional dissonance**.
- **Affective Events Theory (AET)** is the causal model: workplace *events* → emotional reactions → attitudes and behaviour. **Emotional intelligence (EI)** is the capability to read/manage emotion; **emotion regulation** (reappraisal beats suppression) is the technique.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Affect | The broad range of feelings, covering both emotions and moods |
| Emotions vs moods | Intense, discrete, event-caused / longer-lived, milder, no clear stimulus |
| Emotional labor | Expressing organizationally desired emotions during work interactions |
| Surface vs deep acting | Hiding real feelings to meet display rules / actually modifying felt emotion to meet them |
| Emotional dissonance | Inconsistency between felt and projected emotions (a strain) |
| Affective events theory (AET) | Events cause emotional reactions that drive attitudes and behaviour |
| Emotional intelligence (EI) | The ability to detect and manage emotional cues in self and others |
| Cognitive reappraisal | Reframing a situation to change the emotion — more effective than suppression |

**Cases & examples**
- **Service roles (e.g. Starbucks)** — display rules and the toll of emotional labor; the "weather changes mood" belief is largely an **illusory correlation**.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **AI lecture tie-in** — AI is deliberately **anthropomorphised** and made *warm* to manage our emotions (comfort, trust); but training a model warmer traded against truth **raised its error rate ~10–13%**, and **sycophancy** inflates users' confidence (→ Ch 6).

## Ch 5 — Personality & Individual Differences

**Theory & concepts**
- **Fit comes first.** Organizations match people not just to jobs (**person–job fit**, Holland's six types) but to the organization, group and supervisor (**person–organization fit**): people are attracted to, selected by, and stay in places matching their values.
- **Two frameworks, unequal evidence.** The **MBTI** is the most *popular* and least *defensible*; the **Big Five** (OCEAN) is the scientific standard. The **Dark Triad** (Machiavellianism, narcissism, psychopathy) covers the toxic side; HEXACO adds honesty-humility.
- **Science vs conventional wisdom** — a usable construct must pass **reliability** (consistent) and **validity** (captures/predicts what it claims). MBTI fails both.
- **Three further OB traits**: core self-evaluations, self-monitoring, proactive personality.
- **Situation matters as much as trait** — **situation strength** (clarity/consistency/constraints/consequences) suppresses personality; **trait activation** draws it out. Nature *and* nurture.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Big Five (OCEAN) | Conscientiousness, agreeableness, emotional stability, extroversion, openness |
| Conscientiousness | The **best single personality predictor of job performance** |
| Reliability / validity | Gives a consistent answer / captures and predicts what it claims |
| Dark Triad | Machiavellianism (pragmatic, manipulative) · narcissism (grandiose, entitled) · psychopathy (callous, remorseless) |
| Core self-evaluation (CSE) | Bottom-line belief in one's worth and competence |
| Self-monitoring | The ability to adjust behaviour to external, situational cues |
| Proactive personality | Spotting opportunities, taking initiative, persevering until change happens |
| Situation strength theory | Strong situations suppress personality's effect on behaviour |
| Trait activation theory | Certain situations activate a trait more than others |
| Terminal vs instrumental values | Desirable end-states / preferred modes of behaviour |

**Cases & examples**
- Holland's vocational types; **Wells Fargo / Uber** as cautionary Dark-Triad leadership.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 2 slides** — students take the **16personalities (MBTI)** test in class, then see the critique: **88 of the Fortune 100 use MBTI** and it earns the publisher **~\$20M/yr**, yet **~50% get a different type after 5 weeks** (unreliable) and it shows few links to work outcomes (invalid). The deck's thesis: distinguish **scientific constructs** from constructs that merely *sound* true.
- **Seminar 2** folded the MBTI-vs-Big-Five argument into this chapter.

## Ch 6 — Perception & Individual Decision Making

**Theory & concepts**
- **Behaviour follows perception, not reality.** Perception is shaped by the perceiver, the target and the situation.
- **Judging others — attribution theory.** We read behaviour as internally vs externally caused using **distinctiveness, consensus, consistency**, and we make two systematic errors: the **fundamental attribution error** (over-weight the person) and **self-serving bias** (our wins are internal, losses external). Everyday shortcuts: selective perception, halo/horns, contrast, stereotyping, the **self-fulfilling (Pygmalion) prophecy**.
- **Deciding.** The **rational model** (6 steps, maximising) is an ideal; we actually run on **bounded rationality** (satisfice on a simplified model) and **intuition** (distilled experience). A catalogue of biases — overconfidence, anchoring, confirmation, availability, **escalation of commitment**, hindsight — distorts choices.
- **Ethics & creativity** — three criteria (utilitarian, rights, justice) + behavioral ethics; the three-stage model of creative behaviour (problem formulation → information gathering → idea generation → evaluation).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Attribution theory | Judging behaviour as internally vs externally caused (distinctiveness/consensus/consistency) |
| Fundamental attribution error | Over-weighting internal, under-weighting external causes in others |
| Self-serving bias | Crediting our successes internally, blaming failures externally |
| Halo / horns effect | A single good/bad trait colours the whole impression |
| Bounded rationality | Simplified "good enough" decision making within cognitive limits |
| Anchoring / confirmation / availability bias | Fixating on first info / seeking confirming evidence / judging by what springs to mind |
| Escalation of commitment | Staying with a losing course despite negative evidence |
| Hindsight / outcome bias | "I knew it all along" / judging a decision only by how it turned out |

**Cases & examples**
- **Wells Fargo** — escalation and ethical collapse under pressure.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 4 slides** — the **Carter Racing** exercise (a Challenger-analogue): the team must decide whether to race, and the lesson is that the *right data exists but you must ask the right question* — a drill in **confirmation bias** and missing information. The deck frames bad decisions at **three levels**: *individual* (overconfidence, sunk costs, selection bias), *group* (power distance, weak leadership, unfamiliarity), *system* (complex interactions + tight coupling). The brain is "trimmed for efficiency," so biases spike under complexity, uncertainty, changing information and time pressure. Fixes: incentives, nudges, training, **psychological safety**, contingency planning.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — **sycophantic AI** amplifies confirmation bias and overconfidence; **hallucination** = confidently wrong; **cognitive offloading** erodes the skill of thinking.

## Ch 7 — Motivation Concepts

**Theory & concepts**
- **Motivation = intensity · direction · persistence** of effort toward a goal.
- **Three 1950s classics** (Maslow's hierarchy, Herzberg's two-factor, McClelland's needs) — the shared *vocabulary*, but of **questionable validity**; know them to speak the language, not to decide.
- **Contemporary theories in three families** — *content* (what motivates: self-determination, engagement), *context* (reinforcement, social learning), *process* (how people choose and strive: goal-setting, self-efficacy, expectancy).
- **Self-determination theory** — motivation is **autonomous** (freely chosen) vs **controlled**; satisfying **autonomy, competence, relatedness** sustains it. **Cognitive evaluation theory**: controlling extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic interest.
- **Organizational justice** — fairness as a motivational input: distributive (outcomes), procedural (process + voice), informational (honest explanations), interpersonal (dignity). A fair *process* makes people accept unfavourable outcomes.
- **Expectancy theory** integrates the survivors: effort→performance (**expectancy**) × performance→reward (**instrumentality**) × reward→goals (**valence**).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Self-determination theory | Motivation shaped by autonomy, competence and relatedness; autonomous vs controlled |
| Expectancy theory | Act = expectancy × instrumentality × valence |
| Goal-setting theory | Specific, difficult, accepted goals + feedback raise performance (→ MBO) |
| Self-efficacy | Belief that you can perform a task (raised by mastery, modelling, persuasion, arousal) |
| Equity theory | People compare their outcome/input ratio to a referent and act to remove inequity |
| Organizational justice | Distributive · procedural · informational · interpersonal fairness |
| Two-factor theory | Hygiene factors prevent dissatisfaction; motivators create satisfaction |
| nAch / nPow / nAff | McClelland's needs for achievement / power / affiliation |

**Cases & examples**
- Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland as the "language"; **Seminar 3-2** dealership reward case (diagnosing a broken expectancy link).

**Beyond the textbook**
- **Seminar 3-2 / OBHR101 Week 4 recap** — motivation can be *diagnosed* link-by-link (expectancy/instrumentality/valence); different broken links need different fixes.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — **algorithmic management** and surveillance shift motivation from *autonomous* to *controlled*, crowding out intrinsic motivation (SDT).

## Ch 8 — Motivation: From Concepts to Applications

**Theory & concepts**
- The **toolkit** for Ch 7, in four families. Every lever works **conditionally** — ask "for whom, under what conditions, and does the expectancy chain hold?"
- **Job design — the Job Characteristics Model (JCM).** Five dimensions — **skill variety, task identity, task significance** (→ meaningfulness), **autonomy** (→ responsibility), **feedback** (→ knowledge of results) — combine in the **MPS**; autonomy and feedback are *multiplied*, so a job near-zero on either has near-zero motivating potential. Redesign via **rotation, enrichment, relational design, job crafting**.
- **Alternative work arrangements** — flextime, job sharing, telecommuting (raises satisfaction but risks isolation and "out of sight, out of mind").
- **Employee involvement (EIP)** — participative and representative participation (heavy cultural caveats).
- **Rewards** — build a pay structure, then **variable pay** (piece-rate, merit, bonus, profit-sharing, ESOP), **flexible benefits**, and intrinsic **recognition**.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Job Characteristics Model (JCM) | Any job on five core dimensions → three psychological states → outcomes |
| Motivating potential score (MPS) | (variety + identity + significance)/3 × autonomy × feedback |
| Job enrichment | Adding higher-level responsibility to raise intrinsic motivation (vs mere enlargement) |
| Job crafting | Employees reshaping their own tasks/relationships to use their strengths |
| Flextime / telecommuting | Flexible hours around a core / working outside the physical workplace |
| Employee involvement (EIP) | A participative process using employees' input to raise commitment |
| Variable-pay program | Part of pay tied to individual/organizational performance |
| Flexible benefits | Each employee tailors their own benefits package |

**Cases & examples**
- Flextime helps **absenteeism more than performance**; telecommuting raises satisfaction but creates isolation; pay-for-performance motivates **and can corrupt**.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **AI lecture / Amazon RTO** — mandating office work removes the JCM's **autonomy** dimension; AI is also a job-design force (which tasks stay human, which get offloaded).

## Ch 9 — Foundations of Group Behavior

**Theory & concepts**
- A **group** = two+ interacting, interdependent people pursuing objectives; **social identity theory** explains the emotional investment (we define ourselves by group membership).
- **Development** — the five-stage model, and the **punctuated-equilibrium** model for deadline-driven temporary groups (inertia, a midpoint burst, then a final push).
- **Five group properties** — **roles** (expectations, role conflict, the **psychological contract**), **norms** (+ **conformity**, Asch), **status** (status characteristics theory), **size** (bigger → **social loafing**), **cohesion**.
- **Group decisions** — sometimes better, but with two pathologies: **groupthink** (consensus crushes dissent) and **groupshift** (discussion pushes to extremes); contain with brainstorming, nominal group, electronic meeting techniques.
- **The reframing finding:** **cohesion multiplies existing performance norms** — a cohesive group with *low* norms reliably produces *low* output.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Social identity theory | Why/when people define themselves by group membership |
| Punctuated-equilibrium model | Temporary groups alternate inertia with a burst of change at the midpoint |
| Role conflict | Facing divergent role expectations at once |
| Psychological contract | The unwritten set of mutual employee–employer expectations |
| Norms / conformity | Shared standards of acceptable behaviour / adjusting to match them |
| Social loafing | Exerting less effort collectively than individually |
| Cohesion | The bond that makes members stay and work together — **multiplies norms** |
| Groupthink / groupshift | Consensus overrides realistic appraisal / discussion produces a more extreme position |

**Cases & examples**
- **Asch** conformity line-judgment experiments; **Hawthorne** norm enforcement (rate-busting).

**Beyond the textbook**
- **Seminars 5–6** — decision & team dynamics, jury deliberation, wisdom of crowds, persuasion tactics.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — an AI "co-worker" that removes the *need* to work in groups erodes team spirit and the reason the firm coordinates at all.

## Ch 10 — Understanding Work Teams

**Theory & concepts**
- **Calling a group a team does not make it one.** Synergy is only *potential*; a **work group** shares information (performance = sum of parts), a **work team** produces performance **greater than the sum** through *positive synergy, mutual accountability and complementary skills*.
- **Five team types** — problem-solving, self-managed, cross-functional, virtual, and multiteam systems.
- **The team effectiveness model** — three clusters: **context** (resources, leadership, trust, rewards), **composition** (abilities, personality, roles, size, diversity), **processes/states** (common plan, efficacy, **mental models**, conflict, minimal social loafing).
- **Turning individuals into team players** — select for it, train it, reward it — and know **when *not* to use a team** (a task with no interdependence is better done alone).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Work group vs work team | Shares information, sum of parts / complementary efforts, greater than the sum |
| Self-managed / cross-functional / virtual team | Runs its own work / same-level across functions / technology-linked, dispersed |
| Reflexivity | Reflecting on and adjusting the team's master plan |
| Mental model | Team's shared knowledge of the task and how to work it |
| Transactive memory system | "Who knows what" — how the team distributes and retrieves knowledge |
| Team efficacy | The team's collective belief it can succeed |

**Cases & examples**
- Cross-functional and virtual team design; Big-Five composition effects on team performance.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 5 slides** — the **Desert Survival** exercise (does the team beat its best individual?) and a clean **work group vs work team** table (goal · synergy · accountability · skills). Decision quality breaks down at **individual/group/system** levels, and **psychological safety** is the key enabler of speaking up.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — **expertise overreach**: using AI beyond your understanding and passing it to teammates *raises team conflict* (the lecturer's own research).

## Ch 11 — Communication

**Theory & concepts**
- Communication is **always imperfect**; the OB skill is diagnosing and fixing breakdowns. The process: sender → encode → **channel** → decode → receiver → **feedback**, with noise throughout.
- **Three interpersonal modes** — *oral* (synchronicity, active/reflective listening, meetings), *written* (email, IM), *nonverbal* (body language, space, time).
- **Choosing a method** = matching message to medium via **channel richness**; manage three barriers — **information overload, communication apprehension, crisis**.
- **Virtual & social** — channel richness, enterprise social software, the **shallowing hypothesis**, and cybersecurity/reputation.
- **Cross-cultural** — **high- vs low-context** cultures, five interaction approaches, and Casmir's **"third culture"** (an ad-hoc common ground).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Channel richness | How much information a medium can carry in one episode |
| Filtering | A sender manipulating information so the receiver sees it more favourably |
| Information overload | Inflow exceeding processing capacity |
| Communication apprehension | Undue tension/anxiety about communicating |
| High- vs low-context cultures | Meaning carried by subtle/nonverbal cues vs by explicit words |
| Grapevine | The organization's informal communication network |
| Third culture | Common-ground culture a diverse group builds by blending each member's preferences |

**Cases & examples**
- The **hospital robot** delivering end-of-life news to a 78-year-old surrounded by family — the wrong *medium* destroyed the message. **Google / Enron** on communication and culture.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **AI lecture tie-in** — using AI to draft tense emails and social replies; emojis/usernames/selfies as the new "currency"; the always-on **autonomy paradox** of smartphones.

## Ch 12 — Leadership

**Theory & concepts**
- **Leadership = influencing a group toward a vision**; organizations need both leadership (challenge the status quo, inspire) and management (plan, structure, control).
- **Five families, each fixing the last:** **traits** (who *emerges* — better than who's effective) → **behaviours** (**initiating structure** vs **consideration**; implies leadership can be trained) → **contingency** (**Fiedler**'s LPC + situational control; SLT; shared leadership; followership) → **positive styles** (**LMX** ingroups/outgroups; **charismatic**; the **full-range model** ending in **transformational**) → **(un)ethical** (authentic, ethical, servant; **abusive supervision**).
- **Trust** is the common substrate; leadership is partly an **attribution**, and **neutralizers/substitutes** can make it irrelevant.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Initiating structure vs consideration | Organizing roles/tasks for goals / trust and regard for feelings |
| Fiedler contingency model | Match leader style (LPC) to how much control the situation gives |
| Situational leadership (SLT) | Style depends on follower readiness (telling/selling/participating/delegating) |
| Leader–member exchange (LMX) | Leaders form higher/lower-quality ties → ingroups and outgroups |
| Transactional vs transformational | Guide via rewards and correction / inspire followers beyond self-interest |
| Charismatic leadership | Followers attribute heroic qualities from vision and emotional behaviour |
| Abusive supervision | Hostile verbal/nonverbal supervision |
| Neutralizers / substitutes | Attributes that make leader behaviour irrelevant / replace the need for it |

**Cases & examples**
- **Fiedler**'s model; **W. L. Gore** (shared leadership / lattice organization); Dark-Triad vs transformational leaders.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 10 recap** — leadership *emergence* ≠ *effectiveness*; effective leaders **adapt** to task, situation and followers.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — **AI "bosses" / algorithmic management** and AI weighing in on leadership decisions (a boardroom AI) shift influence and accountability.

## Ch 13 — Power & Politics

**Theory & concepts**
- **Power = the capacity to enforce one's will**; its essence is **dependence** — the more others rely on what you control, the more power you have, and power **evaporates** when they gain alternatives.
- **Power vs leadership** — power needs only dependence (not goal congruence), is more *downward*, and is about *tactics for compliance* rather than style.
- **Five bases** — coercive, reward, legitimate (position) + expert, referent (personal; the more durable).
- **Influence tactics** (9) translate bases into action; **political skill** is doing it effectively.
- **Politics** — its causes, the move from **voice to silence**, **impression management**, and the **ethics** of behaving politically; sexual harassment analysed as an abuse of power.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Power / dependence | Capacity to enforce one's will / the extent others rely on a powerful person |
| Coercive / reward / legitimate power | Fear of punishment / control of rewards / formal position |
| Expert / referent power | Knowledge and skill / identification with an admired person |
| Influence tactics | Ways to turn a power base into specific action |
| Political skill | Influencing others so your objectives are met |
| Impression management (IM) | Controlling the impression others form of you |
| Voice vs silence | Speaking up with suggestions/concerns / withholding them |

**Cases & examples**
- **Seminar 9-2** — power in organizations; political behaviour and impression-management tactics.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 10 recap** — power comes from position *and* personal traits *and* others' dependence; it shapes risk-taking, listening and perspective-taking; build it via coalitions, political skill, IM.
- **AI lecture tie-in** — unequal **access to frontier AI** is a new source of dependence/power.

## Ch 14 — Conflict & Negotiation

**Theory & concepts**
- **Conflict** = a process starting when one party perceives another has negatively affected something they care about. **Functional** conflict helps; **dysfunctional** hurts. Three types: **task** (usually functional), **relationship** (usually dysfunctional), **process**; and loci dyadic/intragroup/intergroup.
- **The five-stage process** — potential opposition → cognition & personalization → **intentions** (competing/collaborating/avoiding/accommodating/compromising) → behaviour → outcomes.
- **Negotiation** — **distributive** (fixed pie, win–lose) vs **integrative** (expand the pie, win–win) bargaining; the five-step process; and **BATNA** (your walk-away alternative — the source of your power).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Functional vs dysfunctional conflict | Supports and improves performance / hinders it |
| Task / relationship / process conflict | Over content / interpersonal / over how work gets done |
| Distributive bargaining | Dividing a fixed amount — win–lose |
| Integrative bargaining | Creating value for a win–win settlement |
| Fixed pie | The (often mistaken) belief that only a set amount can be divided |
| BATNA | Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — the least you should accept |
| Five conflict intentions | Competing, collaborating, avoiding, accommodating, compromising |

**Cases & examples**
- **Tesla's board** "asleep at the wheel" — with information filtered through Musk, there was too *little* conflict; the **absence of conflict is itself a warning signal**.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **Seminars 7-1 / 7-2** — negotiation fundamentals, traps and preparation; multi-issue tactics (trade across issues to find integrative gains).

## Ch 15 — Foundations of Organization Structure

**Theory & concepts**
- **Structure** = how tasks are formally **divided, grouped and coordinated** — "structure follows strategy, controls behaviour, supports execution." It also has an **equity** dimension (structural barriers can block advancement).
- **Seven elements** — work specialization, departmentalization, chain of command, **span of control**, **centralization**, **formalization**, boundary spanning.
- **Classic designs** — simple (centralized, little formalization), **bureaucracy** (specialization + rules), **matrix** (dual authority); **newer designs** — virtual, team, circular; plus **downsizing**.
- **Mechanistic vs organic** — rigid/standardized vs flat/flexible/participative. **Why structures differ** — strategy (innovation/cost/imitation), size, technology, environment, institutions.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Work specialization | How far tasks are subdivided into separate jobs |
| Span of control | Number of subordinates a manager can direct effectively |
| Centralization | How concentrated decision making is |
| Formalization | How standardized jobs and rules are |
| Bureaucracy | Routine via specialization, rules, functional departments, narrow spans |
| Matrix structure | Dual functional + product lines of authority |
| Mechanistic vs organic | Rigid, formalized, centralized / flat, cross-functional, participative |
| Innovation / cost-min / imitation strategy | New offerings / tight cost control / enter only proven markets |

**Cases & examples**
- **Netflix / Amazon / Microsoft / Toyota** on structure–strategy fit; bureaucracy vs organic; downsizing effects on survivors.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **AI lecture tie-in** — AI enabling flatter, more virtual structures; the **"organisation run entirely by AI"** thought experiment (the Spark scenario) and why the human layer still matters.

## Ch 16 — Organizational Culture & Change

**Theory & concepts**
- **Culture** = a system of **shared meaning** (values, beliefs, assumptions) that distinguishes the organization; it is **descriptive** (how members *perceive* the place), not evaluative like job satisfaction. It ① filters what people notice ② shows up in symbols/stories ③ is the basis of shared meaning.
- **The iceberg / three layers** — visible **artifacts** → espoused **values** → invisible **underlying assumptions**.
- **Learning & sustaining culture** — employees learn it through **stories, rituals, symbols, language**; it is created by founders and kept alive by selection, top management and **socialization** (prearrival → encounter → metamorphosis).
- **Strong culture** = intensely held, widely shared values → consistency, commitment, coordination — but risks **homogeneity / blind spots / slow adaptation**. **Climate** is the shared perception of policies/practices.
- **Change** — **resistance** (individual: habit, security, fear; organizational: structural/group inertia, threats to power/expertise), and **four approaches**: **Lewin** (unfreeze → move → refreeze, via driving/restraining forces), **Kotter**'s 8 steps, **action research** (data-driven), **OD**.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Organizational culture | Shared values, beliefs and assumptions that distinguish the organization |
| Strong culture | Core values intensely held and widely shared |
| Socialization (3 stages) | Prearrival → encounter → metamorphosis |
| Organizational climate | Shared perceptions of policies, practices and procedures |
| Driving vs restraining forces | Forces pushing away from / holding at the status quo (Lewin) |
| Lewin's three-step model | Unfreeze → movement → refreeze |
| Kotter's 8 steps | A detailed guide (urgency → coalition → vision → communicate → empower → wins → consolidate → anchor) |
| Action research | Change chosen from systematically collected data; lowers resistance via participation |

**Cases & examples**
- **Bridgewater Associates** (Ray Dalio) — radical transparency/truth that *fit* the goal (accurate financial decisions) but would misfit a service firm; **W. L. Gore / Southwest / Nike**; Gallup's millennial "purpose" finding (88% would stay 5+ years if satisfied with the mission).

**Beyond the textbook**
- **OBHR101 Week 10 slides** — opens with **Amazon (5 days in office) vs Spotify (work from anywhere)**: *what different assumptions about people and work explain the two choices?* Culture comes from **many levels** (national → industry → occupational → organizational → subcultures); the **iceberg** (artifacts/values/assumptions); "we're a team, not a family" (Netflix).
- **Culture & change seminar** — Bridgewater, the **S-curve of diffusion** (win early adopters for critical mass), "treat people like children" (respect predictable irrationality).
- **AI lecture tie-in** — AI **homogenises** ideas (the blind-spot risk); adoption is **bottlenecked by human behaviour** (Narayanan's "AI as normal technology") vs the singularity view (Kurzweil).

## Ch 17 — Human Resource Systems & Practices

**Theory & concepts**
- **HR practices shape behaviour and effectiveness** (HR ↔ OB, a never-ending cycle), walked along the **employee lifecycle**.
- **Recruitment** — attract the right applicants (referrals, **realistic job previews** to cut early turnover).
- **Selection — a three-stage funnel**: *initial* (rough cuts on basic qualifications), *substantive* (the best-qualified — **work sample tests, assessment centers, situational judgment tests, structured interviews**), *contingent* (final checks). Every method is judged on **reliability and validity**.
- **Training & development** — the goal is **transfer of training** (using it on the job); designed via ISD/ADDIE.
- **Performance management** — what to measure (results/behaviour/traits), who rates (360), how (BARS, forced ranking) and how to improve it.
- **Accessible workplaces** (reasonable accommodations) and **high-performance work systems (HPWS)** — bundles of HR practices that reinforce one another.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Realistic job previews | An honest preview of the actual job to reduce early turnover |
| Selection funnel | Initial → substantive → contingent stages |
| Work sample / assessment center | Hands-on simulation of the job / off-site battery of performance simulations |
| Situational judgment test | "What would you do?" scored against high performers |
| Structured vs unstructured interview | Planned, job-related, comparable / casual, improvised (weaker) |
| Transfer of training | Applying trained knowledge/skills on the job |
| BARS | Behaviourally Anchored Rating Scales (critical incidents + rating scale) |
| High-performance work system (HPWS) | Mutually reinforcing HR practices that lift organizational outcomes |

**Cases & examples**
- **Greyston Bakery** — **open hiring** since 1982 (no résumé, interview, background or drug test; next on the list is hired); near-zero hiring cost, **~\$1,900/hire in training**, ~**\$7M/yr** local economic impact; standards still strictly enforced.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **AI lecture tie-in** — **AI screens job candidates** at the first stage (prompting the CV "ignore previous instructions" arms race), and rigid policies (Amazon RTO) raise offer-decline rates — selection, validity and fairness in one.

## Ch 18 — Stress & Health in Organizations

**Theory & concepts**
- Mismanaged workplace stress is framed as a **public-health crisis**. The single model: **stressors → stress → strain**.
- **Stressors** — **challenge** (workload, time pressure — can energise) vs **hindrance** (red tape, politics, unclear roles — purely draining); a healthy appraisal is **eustress**.
- **Physical health** — sleep, and **presenteeism** (working while ill). **Mental health** — job insecurity, **workaholism**, **burnout** (exhaustion + depersonalization + reduced accomplishment).
- **Four theories** — **COR** (conserve resources), **ERI** (effort–reward imbalance), **JDCS** (demand–control–support: high demand + low control + low support = strain), **JDR** (demands vs resources).
- **Work–life balance** — boundary management, **spillover/crossover**; managed **individually** (time/exercise/mindfulness/resilience) and **organizationally** (job redesign, support, **wellness programs**).

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Stressor / stress / strain | Challenging/threatening condition / its unpleasant appraisal / the resulting consequences |
| Challenge vs hindrance stressors | Workload/time pressure / red tape, politics, role ambiguity |
| Presenteeism | Working while ill or injured |
| Burnout | Emotional exhaustion + depersonalization + reduced personal accomplishment |
| Job insecurity | Perception that one's job/employment is at risk |
| JDCS model | Strain rises with high demands + low control + low support |
| Work–life spillover | One domain's psychological residue carried into another |
| Resilience / mindfulness | Resistance to stress effects / present-moment attention and awareness |

**Cases & examples**
- **Massachusetts General Hospital ED** beat burnout during the pandemic via **emotional (not financial) rewards, meaningful interaction, transparent communication, reasonable workloads, and community** — two-thirds reported *more* unity than before.

**Beyond the textbook**
- **AI lecture tie-in** — heavier **AI interaction** correlated with **worse sleep, higher alcohol use and isolation** (four-country study) — a modern stressor seen through **JDCS** (↑demands, ↓control).
