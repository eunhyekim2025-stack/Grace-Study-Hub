# Chapter 9 — Foundations of Group Behavior

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch08-motivation-applications|Ch 8]] · Next: [[ch10-understanding-work-teams|Ch 10 — Understanding Work Teams]]

---

## Core summary

A **group** is two or more individuals, interacting and interdependent, who have come together to achieve particular objectives. The chapter answers four questions:

1. **What kinds of groups exist, and why do people identify with them?** Formal versus informal; **social identity theory** explains the emotional investment.
2. **How do groups develop over time?** The **punctuated-equilibrium model** for temporary deadline-bound groups.
3. **What properties define a group?** **Roles, norms, status, size, cohesion** — the analytic core of the chapter.
4. **Are group decisions better?** Sometimes — with two characteristic pathologies (**groupthink**, **groupshift**) and three techniques for containing them.

> [!important] The finding that reframes everything else
> **Cohesion is not good on its own.** Cohesion multiplies whatever the group's performance norms already are. A cohesive group with low performance norms produces **low** productivity — reliably, and with enforcement.

## Defining and classifying groups

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Formal group** | Defined by the organization's structure, with designated work assignments and established tasks; behaviours are stipulated and directed toward organizational goals | An airline flight crew |
| **Informal group** | Neither formally structured nor organizationally determined; forms to meet social needs or bind people with common interests | Three employees from different departments who lunch together |

Informal interactions, though unofficial, **deeply affect behaviour and performance**.

### Social identity theory

**Social identity theory** considers **when and why individuals consider themselves members of groups**. It pairs with social categorization from [[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]]:

- **Social categorization** explains why people sort *others* into groups
- **Social identity** explains why people *identify with* particular groups

The mechanism: people have emotional reactions to their group's failure or success because **their self-esteem gets tied to what happens to the group**. Hence pride at company success, threat when the company is threatened, and even **schadenfreude** when competitors suffer — which Wendy's social media team monetizes through Twitter "roasting wars."

**Two identification routes inside organizations:**

- **Relational identification** — connecting with others because of our **roles**
- **Collective identification** — connecting with the **aggregate characteristics** of our groups

> [!note] Group identification beats organizational identification
> In the workplace, identification with the **group** is stronger than with the **organization** — though both drive positive attitudes and behaviours. Low group identification means lower satisfaction and fewer OCBs. Immigrant workers often experience strained, threatened identities without inclusive supervisors or community belonging, which drives high turnover.
>
> Even language matters: across sixteen years, CEOs using **"we"/"us"** rather than **"I"** language correlated with increased sales and return on investment.

Identities are **plural, clustered and situational**. A US expatriate in Rome is acutely aware of national identity; the same person moving Tulsa → Tucson never thinks about it. Identities can also conflict, and acquiring, maintaining or losing them affects health and well-being.

> [!warning] Favouritism, not hostility, may be the main engine of discrimination
> Social identity leads to ingroup/outgroup thinking — believing outgroup members are "all the same," and **playing favourites** with one's own. Some research suggests favouritism rather than hostility causes most discriminatory behaviour: *"Hostility isn't integral to the definition of discrimination; you can treat people differently without being hostile to anyone."* Which means employees and managers can be **complicit in discrimination without any hostile intent**.

## Stages of group development

The **punctuated-equilibrium model** describes how **temporary groups with finite deadlines** actually behave:

1. **The first meeting sets the group's direction** — general purpose plus a framework of assumptions, sometimes formed implicitly in the first few seconds
2. **Phase 1 is inertia** — the direction is solidified and rarely re-examined, even when new insights challenge it
3. **A transition occurs at the halfway point** — whether the project is an hour or six months long
4. **The transition initiates major changes** — a concentrated burst, dropping old patterns, adopting new perspectives, setting a revised direction
5. **Phase 2 is a second period of inertia** — executing the plans made at the transition
6. **The final meeting is a burst of accelerated activity**

> [!tip] The midpoint is an alarm clock
> The transition works because the midpoint heightens awareness that **time is limited**. It is the practical reason to schedule a deliberate mid-project review — the group will otherwise not revisit its founding assumptions at all.

The model does not apply to all groups — only to **temporary task groups under a deadline**. The alternative **forming–storming–norming–performing** sequence can be read as occurring inside Phase 1, with a second performing-and-conforming stage in Phase 2 after brief renorming.

## Group property 1 · Roles

A **role** is a set of expected behaviour patterns attributed to someone occupying a given position in a social unit. Everyone plays several, at work and in life.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Role perception** | An individual's view of how to act in a given situation — drawn from family, coworkers, media |
| **Role expectations** | How **others** believe a person should act in a given situation |
| **Psychological contract** | An **unwritten agreement** between employees and employers establishing mutual expectations |
| **Role conflict** | Being confronted by divergent role expectations |
| **Interrole conflict** | The expectations of an individual's different, separate groups being in opposition |

**Expectations shape perceptions.** A supervisor who expects innovation makes the employee believe creativity is part of the role, **even when it is not formally defined that way**. Leaders' voice and action on ethical issues likewise shape followers' ethical role perceptions. Expectations are also emotionally charged and can diverge: supervisors often perceive employees' roles **more optimistically** than employees do, and the pessimistic employees then show diminished engagement and performance.

### The psychological contract

Management is expected to treat employees fairly, provide acceptable conditions, communicate what a fair day's work is, and give feedback. Employees are expected to show a good attitude, follow directions and demonstrate loyalty.

| If management breaches | If management fulfils |
|---|---|
| Negative effects on performance, **CWB**, turnover and dissatisfaction | Employees feel **grateful**, identify with the organization, and perform more OCBs |

Employees may fail their side through **loss of resources, workload, stress, family interfering with work**, or wanting more than the contract offers — with performance suffering and promotions passed over. A subtle failure mode: employers who are **"overly supportive"** can make employees nervous, feeling obliged to do more than the role requires. And employees are sometimes expected by employers to be **unethical**, with sanctions (insults, pressure) and being perceived as less warm if they refuse.

Repair is possible: **forgiveness** relieves emotional exhaustion after a breach, and if you caused it, **do not deny the breach — take responsibility** if you want to rebuild trust.

### Role conflict

A significant source of stress for most employees. It appears when two roles clash (professors expected to excel at both teaching and research with time for only one) and as **interrole conflict** across groups — a second job interfering with the primary one, or family and work roles colliding. A vivid case: nurses in a disaster required to calmly treat someone close to them, regulating emotions arising from the relational role while performing the professional one.

Coping: some employees adjust creatively, especially adopting a **mindfulness mindset**; managers help by providing support, especially for newer employees juggling doing the job with learning the ropes.

## Group property 2 · Norms

**Norms** are acceptable standards of behaviour within a group, shared by its members — what they *ought* and *ought not* to do. Every group, community and society has them. Crucially, they are **not just leader-set policy**: to be adopted rather than abandoned in three days, **they must be accepted by all**.

### Norms and conformity

**Conformity** — adjusting your behaviour to align with group norms — happens because you want acceptance and stability, and because you perceive that others are conforming.

> [!important] Asch's line experiment
> Groups of seven or eight compared a single line to three lines of varying length. The difference was obvious — people were wrong **less than 1% of the time** alone. But when confederates unanimously gave an obviously wrong answer, **75% of subjects conformed at least once**, and the average conformer gave wrong answers **37% of the time**.
>
> Two qualifications. Asch himself noted that participants "agreed nearly without exception that **independence was preferable to conformity**" — we do not enjoy the pressure. And we conform mainly to **reference groups**: groups we belong to or want to belong to, whose members we consider significant. Conformity is not always bad — people conform to **prosocial norms** such as generosity in donating, and feel more empathy as a result.

### Norms and behaviour — the Hawthorne Studies

Conducted 1924–1932 at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works, in three phases:

1. **Illumination.** Raising light levels raised output for both experimental and control groups — and *lowering* the light **kept raising** productivity, until light reached moonlight intensity. Conclusion: **group dynamics, not environment**, drove behaviour.
2. **The relay assembly group.** A small group of women assembling telephones showed steadily increasing output and about **one-third** the absence rate of the regular department. They believed they were an **elite group** and that management cared about their interests. Workers in both experiments were reacting to the **increased attention**.
3. **The bank wiring observation room.** With a wage incentive plan, employees did **not** individually maximize output. Performance was controlled by a **group norm of "fair output"** — neither too much nor too little — because members feared the rate would be cut, expected output raised, layoffs triggered, or slower workers reprimanded. The enforced "don'ts": *don't be a **rate-buster*** (too much work) · *don't be a **chiseler*** (too little) · *don't **squeal** on peers*. Enforcement ran through name calling, ridicule and **punches to the upper arm**. The group operated well below capability.

### Positive and negative norms

**Positive norms produce positive outcomes only with other factors present.** Evolutionary accounts suggest groups facing high threat develop strong interaction and cooperation norms — plus **norms to punish deviants**. CSR values become powerful when they hold **normative sway**, which happens more where employees are satisfied with their groups.

> [!warning] Apparently positive norms can backfire
> Firefighters and nurses were **less likely to use family-friendly benefits** if they perceived that others in their work group did not use them. And when promoting diversity, **raising awareness of how prevalent stereotyping is can backfire**, producing *more* stereotype-consistent behaviour.

**Negative norms** facilitate deviance. Absences set the tone: if teammates and the supervisor do not show up, most employees will not either. Three organizational sources: **"bad apples come from bad barrels"** (negative group attributes) · mistreatment by supervisors, coworkers or customers · pushing employees to do more with less. Nearly **half** of employees who suffered incivility considered changing jobs and **12% actually quit** over it.

The chain reaction in high-deviance groups: negative behaviours (shirking, undermining, uncooperativeness) → **collective negative moods** → poor coordination → lower group performance.

**Typology of deviant workplace behaviour:**

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Production** | Leaving early, intentionally working slowly, wasting resources |
| **Property** | Sabotage, lying about hours worked, stealing from the organization |
| **Political** | Showing favouritism, gossiping and spreading rumours, blaming coworkers |
| **Personal aggression** | Sexual harassment, verbal abuse, stealing from coworkers |

> [!note] Gossip and exclusion are not automatically toxic
> **Prosocial gossip** exposes exploitative behaviour and can produce positive change — the bully feels exposed and conforms, and those hearing and spreading it also cooperate more. **Ostracism** is a felt punishment and produces cooperation from the ostracized person hoping to be readmitted. Together they produce cooperation to a **much greater degree** — but only when the gossip is **truthful and work-related**, the opportunity to rejoin with full standing remains, and **group norms are positive**.

## Group property 3 · Status

**Status** — a socially defined position or rank given to groups or group members by others. It is a significant motivator, with major behavioural consequences when someone perceives a gap between the status they believe they have and what others perceive.

**Status characteristics theory** — status derives from three sources:

1. **The power a person wields over others** — controlling group resources and outcomes
2. **Ability to contribute to the group's goals** — contributions critical to success
3. **Personal characteristics positively valued by the group** — looks, intelligence, money, a friendly personality

**Status and norms.** High-status individuals **deviate from norms more** when they identify weakly with the group, resist conformity pressure better, and eschew pressure from lower-ranking members of other groups (physicians resisting insurance-company administrators). Adding high-status members improves performance **only up to a point**, since they may import counterproductive norms — and high-status employees more readily **justify unethical behaviour as necessary and ethical**.

**Status and interaction.** People seeking higher status become more **assertive**: speaking out more, criticizing more, giving more commands, using more jargon, doing more favours, interrupting more. Lower-status members **participate less** — and where they hold expertise the group needs, that silence directly reduces group performance, which supervisors can offset by encouraging them to speak. But a group of *only* high-status people is worse: **performance suffers when too many high-status people are in the mix**; adding some high-status members to a mid-status group is the better configuration.

**Status inequity** creates disequilibrium and corrective behaviour. Large within-group status differences are associated with **poorer individual performance, worse health, and stronger intentions to leave** among the lower-status. The type matters: **power-based** status inequity drives interpersonal conflict, while other forms promote healthy competition for advancement. Threat of lost status is dangerous — senior politicians under that threat were **more likely to engage in large-scale corporate bribery**.

## Group property 4 · Size and dynamics

| Goal | Better size |
|---|---|
| **Fact-finding, idea generation, diverse input** | **A dozen or more** |
| **Performance and productivity**; effective transformational leadership | **About seven** |

Size also carries stereotypes — smaller groups are perceived as more trustworthy — and we feel **less compassion and empathy toward large groups**, so we help them less.

### Social loafing

**Social loafing** — the tendency for individuals to expend **less effort when working collectively** than when working individually. It directly contradicts the assumption that group productivity at least equals the sum of individual productivity.

**Two causes:** the belief that others are not carrying their fair share (specifically, that the loafer is **exploiting** the others), and **diffusion of responsibility** — because output cannot be attributed to any one person, individuals become **free riders**.

**Moderators:** a stronger **work ethic** reduces it; higher group **conscientiousness and agreeableness** keep performance high whether loafing occurs or not; and **virtual groups** are vulnerable, especially with dissimilar members carrying family responsibilities. Greater performance diversity produces more loafing the longer a group stays together.

**Five preventions:**

1. Set **group goals** so there is a common purpose
2. Increase **intergroup competition** focused on the shared outcome
3. Use **peer evaluations**
4. **Select** members high in motivation who prefer group work
5. Base rewards partly on **each member's unique contribution**, and structure work so contributions are identifiable

## Group property 5 · Cohesion

**Cohesion** — the shared bond driving group members to work together and stay in the group. It grows from time spent together, small size, high interaction, or **external threat**. Connections made in cohesive teams may stay active even after the team disbands.

> [!important] Cohesion × performance norms — the key table
> | | **High performance norms** | **Low performance norms** |
> |---|---|---|
> | **High cohesion** | **High productivity** | **Low productivity** |
> | **Low cohesion** | Moderate productivity | Moderate to low productivity |
>
> A cohesive group with weak performance norms is the **worst** cell. Cohesion amplifies norms; it does not replace them.

Cohesion matters most where members' roles and responsibilities are **highly interdependent** (project teams). Seven ways to build it: make the group smaller · encourage agreement with group goals · increase time spent together · raise the group's status and the perceived difficulty of joining · stimulate competition with other groups · reward the group rather than individuals · physically isolate the group.

## Group decision making

### Groups versus individuals

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| **More complete information and knowledge** — aggregating several people's resources | **Time-consuming**, especially for virtual groups |
| **Increased diversity of views** — more approaches and alternatives considered | **Conformity pressures** — the desire for acceptance squashes overt disagreement |
| **Increased acceptance of the solution** — participants support it and encourage others to accept it | **Domination by one or a few members** — damaging when they are low-ability or their information is unreliable |
| Members are more accepting when dissent is framed as **debate rather than disagreement** | **Ambiguous responsibility** — accountability is diluted |

**The verdict, by criterion:**

- **Accuracy** — group decisions beat the *average* individual but lose to the **most accurate** individual
- **Speed** — individuals win
- **Creativity** — groups win
- **Acceptance of achievable solutions** — groups win
- **Efficiency** — groups consume **more work hours**, except where a lone decision maker would have to spend enormous time gathering the same diverse input

Two fixes worth noting: allowing minority members **secret conversations** in virtual contexts improved decision quality, and setting a norm permitting **"lone dissenters"** reduces conformity and the influence of dominating members.

### Groupthink and groupshift

| Pathology | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Groupthink** | A phenomenon in which the **norm for consensus overrides realistic appraisal** of alternative courses of action — pressure deters critical appraisal of unusual, minority or unpopular views |
| **Groupshift** *(group polarization)* | Group members **exaggerate their initial positions** during discussion, arriving at a more extreme version of what they already held — generally toward **greater risk** |

**Groupthink** aligns closely with Asch: dissenters are pressured to suppress, withhold or modify their real beliefs. The **Abilene Paradox** is the extreme case — a group collectively decides something **no individual member actually wanted**, as with people signing petitions they disagree with out of fear of how the group will judge them.

It occurs most often **early in a group's tenure**, where there is a clear group identity, where members hold a **positive image of the group they want to protect**, and where the group perceives a **collective threat** to that image. Groups focused on **performance rather than learning** are especially vulnerable.

> [!tip] Five ways to minimize groupthink
> ① **Monitor group size** — people grow more intimidated and hesitant as it grows. ② Have leaders play an **impartial role**, actively seeking input from all members **especially early**. ③ Appoint a **devil's advocate** to overtly challenge the majority. ④ Use exercises that stimulate discussion of alternatives **without threatening the group's identity**. ⑤ **Discuss the dangers and risks first**, delaying discussion of possible gains — focusing initially on the negatives of an alternative makes the group less likely to stifle dissent.

**Groupshift's** explanations: discussion is persuasive and makes members comfortable enough to voice more extreme versions of their views; the group **diffuses responsibility**, freeing any individual from accountability; and people take extreme positions to **demonstrate difference from the outgroup**. Which way a group shifts is a function of members' **prediscussion inclinations**.

### Three techniques

| Technique | How | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Interacting groups** | The default — verbal and nonverbal interaction | **Commitment** to a solution |
| **Brainstorming** | Leader states the problem clearly; members freewheel alternatives; **no criticism allowed**, even of the most bizarre suggestions; all ideas recorded | **Group cohesiveness** |
| **Nominal group technique** | Members are physically present but operate independently: ① each **independently records** ideas before any discussion ② each presents **one idea**, with no discussion until all are recorded ③ the group discusses for clarity and evaluates ④ each member **silently rank-orders**; highest aggregate ranking wins | **Efficiently generating many ideas** |

> [!warning] Brainstorming's efficiency problem
> Research consistently shows **individuals working alone generate more ideas than a group brainstorming session**. The cause is **production blocking** — when many people talk at once, it blocks individual thought processes and impedes idea sharing. **Nominal groups generally outperform brainstorming groups**, because members meet formally without restricting independent thinking.

## Key takeaways

- A **group** is interacting and interdependent people with shared objectives; **formal** groups are structurally defined, **informal** ones arise from social needs and still shape behaviour deeply.
- **Social identity theory** explains emotional investment: self-esteem is tied to the group's fate. Group identification is **stronger than organizational** identification — and **favouritism, not hostility**, may drive most discrimination.
- Temporary groups follow the **punctuated-equilibrium model**: inertia → **midpoint transition** → inertia → final burst. Schedule the mid-project review deliberately.
- **Roles**: expectations shape perceptions; the **psychological contract** breach costs performance, CWB and turnover; **role conflict** is a major stressor.
- **Norms** need acceptance by all. Asch showed **75% conform at least once**; Hawthorne showed a group norm of "fair output" beating a wage incentive plan, enforced socially and physically.
- **Status**: from power, contribution and valued characteristics. **Inequity** damages performance, health and retention; too many high-status members hurts the group.
- **Size**: ~7 for performance, 12+ for ideas. Bigger means more **social loafing**, driven by perceived exploitation and diffusion of responsibility.
- **Cohesion multiplies norms** — high cohesion plus low performance norms is the worst outcome.
- **Groupthink** suppresses dissent; **groupshift** amplifies extremity. **Nominal group technique beats brainstorming**, which suffers production blocking.
