# Chapter 6 — Perception and Individual Decision Making

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch05-personality-and-individual-differences|Ch 5]] · Next: [[ch07-motivation-concepts|Ch 7 — Motivation Concepts]]

---

## Core summary

**Perception** is the process by which individuals organize and interpret sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment — and what we perceive can differ substantially from objective reality. That matters because **behaviour and decisions follow perception, not reality**.

The chapter builds in five stages:

1. **What shapes perception** — factors in the perceiver, the target and the situation.
2. **How we judge others** — **attribution theory** (distinctiveness, consensus, consistency), its two systematic errors, and the everyday shortcuts (selective perception, halo/horns, contrast, stereotyping) with their organizational applications.
3. **How we decide** — the **rational model**, the **bounded rationality** we actually operate under, and **intuition** — plus a catalogue of **nine biases and errors**.
4. **What constrains decisions** — individual differences and organizational constraints.
5. **Ethics and creativity** — three ethical criteria, behavioral ethics, lying, and the **three-stage model of creativity**.

> [!important] The through-line
> Every shortcut in this chapter exists because it works — the world produces more stimuli than we can process. The danger is not the shortcut but the **inaccurate generalization**: as the chapter puts it, stereotypes illustrate the warning *"the more useful, the more danger from misuse."*

## What is perception?

**Perception** — a process by which individuals organize and interpret their sensory impressions to give meaning to their environment.

| Factor | Contents | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **In the perceiver** | Attitudes, motives, interests, experience, expectations | Supervisors perceived employees who started work **earlier in the day** as more conscientious and higher-performing — but supervisors who were night owls themselves made that error less |
| **In the target** | Novelty, motion, sounds, size, background, proximity, similarity | Across 10,000+ microloan transactions, the **more pleasant the applicant's appearance**, the more likely and faster the approval — machine learning could predict approval from appearance with ~60% accuracy |
| **In the situation** | Time, work setting, social setting | A **messy office** makes a visiting client read you as less conscientious, more disagreeable and more anxious |

Two more perceiver effects worth noting: **status** shapes response — a low-status observer of workplace incivility is more likely to avoid the perpetrator and quietly support the victim rather than confront; and **memory reconstruction** — leaders who narrate their experiences repeatedly find it hard to separate what happened from what they recall, so they act on beliefs about what worked. William Maxwell: *"In talking about the past, we lie with every breath we draw."*

The remedy the chapter offers is **awareness plus objective measures**: people more aware of their own racial biases are more motivated to control prejudice and better at perceiving their own bias.

## Person perception

### Attribution theory

**Attribution theory** explains the ways we judge people differently depending on the meaning we attribute to a behaviour — specifically whether it was **internally** or **externally** caused.

- **Internally caused** — behaviour the observer believes is under the person's own control (late because they are lazy)
- **Externally caused** — behaviour the situation forced (late because of traffic)
- **Relational** (newer) — the relationship is the cause (a customer left because they did not get on with the new account manager)

Three factors decide which attribution we make:

| Test | Question | High → | Low → |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distinctiveness** | Does the person behave differently in different situations? | External | Internal |
| **Consensus** | Does everyone facing the same situation respond the same way? | External | Internal |
| **Consistency** | Does the person respond this way over time? | Internal | External |

*Worked example:* if Alex performs about the same on related tasks (**low distinctiveness**), other employees perform differently on this task (**low consensus**), and Alex's performance here is stable over time (**high consistency**), observers will hold Alex **personally responsible** — an internal attribution.

> [!warning] Two systematic errors
> **Fundamental attribution error** — we underestimate external factors and overestimate internal ones when judging others. It explains perceiving the wealthy as intelligent and innately effective without weighing the external factors behind their success.
> **Self-serving bias** — we attribute our own successes to internal factors and blame failures on external ones, treating ambiguous information as flattering, accepting positive feedback and rejecting negative.
>
> Note the counterweight the chapter adds: **modest executives** experience better career success and upward mobility, draw positive investor reactions, and lead higher-performing teams and firms.

**Culturally:** evidence is mixed but mostly indicates **collectivist cultures make external attributions more often**. Chinese arbitrators imposed harsher penalties than American ones on organizations violating contracts, attributing responsibility to organizations and context rather than individuals. In business scandal coverage, American newspapers referred more to the **individual**, Japanese newspapers more to the **institution**.

### Relational attributions in practice

- Supervisors are **more likely to exploit** subordinates they judge to be "passionate" about the work — more hours, more tedious tasks — reasoning they will not mind.
- Working alongside a **star performer** shields you from blame but also strips you of credit, as others attribute your performance to the relationship.
- If you believe your leader treats you well only to **keep the peace**, you feel less special and do **fewer OCBs**.

### The common shortcuts

| Shortcut | Definition | Organizational effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Selective perception** | Choosing what to interpret based on interests, background, experience and attitudes | Leaders focus on financial metrics and ignore satisfaction — but if top management **signals** satisfaction matters, other managers stop ignoring it |
| **Halo effect** | Drawing a **positive** general impression from a single characteristic | A friendly, sociable applicant is assumed to also be clever and good at the job |
| **Horns effect** | Drawing a **negative** general impression from a single characteristic | A loud, off-putting applicant is assumed not to be capable |
| **Contrast effect** | Evaluation distorted by comparison with others recently encountered | Everyone appraised after the office star looks worse. A new leader is judged more positively when the **previous** leader was ineffective |
| **Stereotyping** | Judging someone by our perception of the group they belong to | 6,500+ US professors sent identical inquiry letters differing only in stereotypically Black, Chinese, Hispanic, Indian and White names responded **significantly more to White males** |

> [!note] Even positive stereotypes hurt
> A positive stereotype induces **pressure to perform at the stereotypical level**, so people "choke." In the free-throw study, the worst performers were those given a **negative** stereotype — but the **positive** stereotype group also underperformed. **The best performance came from the group given no stereotypical information at all.** Positive stereotypes also make their targets feel depersonalized, and they reinforce group-based difference in either direction.

### Where the shortcuts do damage

**Employment interviews.** We form impressions within a **tenth of a second** from first glance, and most interviewers' decisions change very little after the first four or five minutes. Early information therefore outweighs later information, and a "good applicant" is characterized more by the **absence of unfavourable** characteristics than the presence of favourable ones. Individual intuition about candidates is not reliable — **multiple independent evaluators** are.

**Performance expectations.** The **self-fulfilling prophecy** (Pygmalion effect): behaviour is determined by others' expectations. Expect big things and the team delivers; expect minimal performance and they meet that. Demonstrated across students, soldiers, accountants and other occupations.

**Performance evaluations** depend heavily on perceptual processes, and subjective ratings "sometimes say as much about the evaluator as about the employee."

**Social media.** About **four in ten organizations** screen applicants via social media or online search. Research shows the bias directly: when an applicant's displayed **political affiliation matched the hiring manager's**, the manager rated them more similar, more likeable, a stronger performer and more hirable. Facebook profiles supply demographic and personal information that should not inform hiring — and **even systematically structuring the assessment did not eliminate the bias**. Work-focused networks like LinkedIn show more promise.

**Remedies.** AI-assisted assessments and other decision-support systems help: one review found **mechanical methods 50% more effective** than subjective methods at judging job performance — with the caveat from [[ch01-what-is-organizational-behavior|Ch 1]] that AI can encode bias depending on how it is built. Since decision errors stem partly from lapses in attention and memory, giving employees control over their environment (including remote work) protects the attentional resources that errors feed on.

## Decision making in organizations

A **decision** is a choice from among two or more alternatives, made in reaction to a **problem** — a discrepancy between the current state and some desired state.

> [!tip] Problem recognition is itself perceptual
> One manager sees a 2% quarterly sales decline as a serious problem demanding immediate action; a counterpart with the same 2% decline finds it acceptable. **Awareness that a problem exists is a perceptual issue**, not an objective one. And motivation distorts it: employees *should* be receptive to problems during economic instability, but when jobs are scarce they may be motivated to **deny or ignore** problems.

Surveys find many managers spend at least 31% of their time on decision making — and **over 60% report that less than half of that time is effective**, regardless of how much time they spend.

### Three approaches

**Rational decision making** — consistent, value-maximizing choices within specified constraints, in six steps:

1. Define the problem
2. Identify the decision criteria
3. Allocate weights to the criteria
4. Develop the alternatives
5. Evaluate the alternatives
6. Select the best alternative

It assumes complete information, unbiased identification of all options, and choice of the highest-utility option. Most decisions do not work this way — people settle for an acceptable solution rather than an optimal one, and are **remarkably unaware** they are deciding suboptimally.

**Bounded rationality** — a simplified process of perceiving and interpreting the essential features of problems without capturing their complexity, then behaving rationally within those boundaries. Its characteristic output is **satisficing**: seeking a solution that is merely "good enough."

> [!note] Why satisficing is often correct
> Full rational analysis costs time, energy and money, and many problems are too complicated for the model. Worse, you can hit an **intractable problem** — one that changes entirely or becomes irrelevant before you finish organizing, gathering, analyzing and judging. A simple process is frequently more sensible than the rational model.
>
> To limit the damage: ask **multiple questions** to understand the situation, draw on **multiple sources**, evaluate those sources, and **leave enough time** to decide.

**Intuitive decision making** — an unconscious process created out of distilled experience. It occurs outside conscious thought, relies on **holistic associations** between disparate pieces of information, is **fast**, and is **affectively charged**.

Intuition is not inherently bad and does not always contradict rational analysis; in crises and highly uncertain contexts we may need experts' intuitions. But most experts are skeptical: it is hard to measure, and it can lead you to "close your eyes to follow your heart," ignoring contradictory information to protect the hunch. Dozens of studies suggest intuition, though it "feels right," does not correspond with rationally made decisions.

> [!important] The usable rule
> *"Intuition can be very useful as a way of setting up a hypothesis but is unacceptable as 'proof.'"* Speculate from experience — then **test the hunch with objective data and dispassionate analysis**.

## Nine biases and errors

| Bias | Definition | How it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| **Overconfidence bias** | Being too confident about our own or others' abilities, usually without awareness | Those weakest in intellectual and interpersonal ability **most** overestimate themselves. Optimistic entrepreneurs start faster, persist too long, and are **least successful**. Founder CEOs are far more overconfident than their successors — using optimistic language, inflating forecasts, believing their firms undervalued. Overconfident people are **more likely to be selected as leaders** (they ease others' ambiguity) — until the overconfidence is revealed and reputation collapses |
| **Anchoring bias** | Fixating on initial information and failing to adjust adequately | Salary negotiation: your prior salary anchors the offer. **Precision matters** — say \$55,000 and the boss reads \$50–60k as the range; say \$55,650 and they read \$55–56k |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seeking information that reaffirms past choices, discounting what contradicts | A supervisor who sees you as unreliable notices your unreliability and not your reliability. Social media is an **echo chamber**, and ad algorithms profit by targeting people already inclined to believe. Walt Whitman's corrective: *"Listen to all sides and filter them from yourself"* |
| **Availability bias** | Judging on readily available information — vivid, emotional or recent | Explains why appraisals overweight **recent** behaviour over that of six to nine months earlier. Also why long-tenured employees discount newer employees' experiences as "easier" — their own hard times are more salient |
| **Escalation of commitment** *(sunk cost effect)* | Staying with a decision despite clear evidence it is wrong | Most likely when we see ourselves as responsible for the outcome or believe we will eventually succeed — **and it happens whether we chose the course or were assigned it**. Shared decision authority raises escalation. In entrepreneurial teams, **hope of attaining the goal trumps fear of failure** |
| **Randomness error** | Believing we can predict the outcome of random events | Superstitions, either contrived ("never decide on Friday the 13th") or reinforced (Tiger Woods' red Sunday shirt). Stress reduces the sense of control, so people reach for rituals to regain it |
| **Risk aversion** | Preferring a sure moderate gain over a riskier outcome with a higher expected payoff | Most people prefer a certain \$50 to a coin flip for \$100 — sometimes even to a flip for \$200. **Framing flips it**: positive framing (a gain of \$50) makes people risk-averse; negative framing (a loss of \$50) makes them risk-seeking. Kahneman's Nobel-winning finding. CEOs at risk of termination become **exceptionally risk averse** even when riskier strategy serves the firm |
| **Hindsight bias** | Believing falsely, after the outcome is known, that we would have predicted it | Malcolm Gladwell: *"What is clear in hindsight is rarely clear before the fact."* |
| **Outcome bias** | Judging decision **quality** by the desirability or believability of the **outcome** | Sales managers who fixate on increased sales may miss how it was achieved (fudged numbers, high-pressure tactics). It also lets people dismiss inconvenient evidence as "fake news." Salespeople exploit it deliberately: *"Imagine a life free of back pain"* |

**Countermeasures.** Four suggestions from the chapter: **focus on goals** (without them you cannot know which information is relevant); **look for information that disconfirms your beliefs**; **do not create meaning out of random events**; **increase your options** (your final choice can be no better than the best option in the set you considered).

Three practical additions: reflect and **reframe** the problem in different ways; train in **structured decision approaches** to rely less on fast heuristics; **set explicit standards** for decisions. Newer research locates all biases in **focusing on the most salient information and insensitivity to alternatives** — so genuinely considering non-salient options is the general antidote.

## What influences decision making

### Individual differences

| Difference | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Trust in intuition** | Leads to riskier decisions and harsher condemnation of unethical behaviour |
| **High self-esteem** | Strongly motivated to protect it, so more prone to **self-serving bias** — blaming others for failure, taking credit for success |
| **Narcissism** | Naturally prone to overconfidence, overclaiming and self-serving bias |
| **Openness** | Less prone to selective perception and **confirmation bias**, being more receptive to contradictory information |
| **Extroversion** | Linked to riskier CEO behaviour — more acquisitions, more equity-threatening behaviour |
| **Conscientiousness facets** | **Achievement-striving** people hate failing so they **escalate commitment** (and are more susceptible to hindsight bias, needing to justify actions); **dutiful** people do what is best for the organization, so they escalate less |
| **Gender identity** | Mostly the **gender similarities hypothesis** — little to no difference. The exceptions: men are non-trivially more prone to sensation seeking and risk taking; men (not women) show moderate gender-role bias in employment decisions for stereotypically masculine positions |
| **Intellectual ability** | GMA helps avoid **only some** errors. Smart people are just as likely to fall prey, because being smart does not alert you to being overconfident or emotionally defensive. **Cognitive flexibility** — selecting the decision strategy that matches the problem — does produce better decisions |
| **Culture** | Shapes problem selection, depth of analysis, weight given to logic, and whether decisions are made by a leader or collectively. Swedish teams took longer than German teams (consensus orientation vs formal roles); Turkish employees expect senior managers to decide but want to be consulted; Japanese team decision making emphasizes consensus |

### Organizational constraints

- **Performance evaluation systems** — managers are shaped by the criteria they are judged on. If a division manager takes no negative feedback as a sign of good operation, plant managers spend their time ensuring bad news never reaches them.
- **Reward systems** — signal which choices pay off personally. Wells Fargo's aggressive incentives drove employees to open **two million fake accounts**.
- **Formal regulations** — limit choice but also **structure decisions to reduce bias**; CEO succession planning produces better and larger candidate pools.
- **Time constraints** — deadlines make full information gathering impossible and harm decision performance.
- **Historical precedents** — "the largest determinant of the size of any given year's budget is last year's budget." Even MBA curricula leave traces: CEOs graduating **before 1970** were more likely to pursue diversification strategies than those graduating after.

> [!warning] Crisis makes all of this worse
> During COVID-19 decision makers faced unprecedented uncertainty with rapidly incoming information — no template and no time to consider carefully. Crisis decisions are **more emotionally charged and more intuitive**, and biases distort systematically: expert credit-rating agencies issue more pessimistic ratings during crises **even when the target's creditworthiness is unaffected**. Perceptions of **justice** matter especially then, shaping both employee attitudes and customer reactions.

## Ethics in decision making

### Three criteria

| Criterion | The rule | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Utilitarianism** | Decide solely by outcomes, ideally providing the greatest good for all | Dominates business decision making — consistent with rationality, efficiency, productivity and profit. But **not as objective as it sounds**: asking people to answer the footbridge dilemma **in a non-native language** raised the proportion who would push the man from ~20% to 33–44%. The emotional distance of a second language fostered a utilitarian view |
| **Rights** | Decide consistently with fundamental liberties and privileges — privacy, free speech, due process | This is what protects **whistleblowers** who report unethical practice to press or government |
| **Justice** | Impose and enforce rules fairly and impartially for an equitable distribution of benefits and costs | Often approached from **deonance** — you "ought to" behave a certain way per moral norms, principles, rules or laws. The "ought force" operates **whether or not** an organizational rule exists |

Utilitarianism feels comfortable in for-profit organizations, where "best interests" can justify layoffs, price rises, plant closures and offshoring. The difficulty: **rights and justice create far more ambiguity** than efficiency and profit, so there is no longer a single measure of a good decision. Public pressure and CSR now push back — consumers buy from responsible organizations and high performers are attracted to them, so sustainability affects the balance sheet directly.

### Behavioral ethics

**Behavioral ethics** analyzes **why people behave the way they do** when confronted with ethical dilemmas — put plainly, **the study of why good people can still do bad things**.

| | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Business ethics** | The *how* and *what* — "how do we manage ethical behaviour?", "what are the outcomes of injustice?" |
| **Behavioral ethics** | The *why* — "what psychological processes inform (un)ethical behaviour?" |

It operates at three levels, mirroring the OB model: **individual** (ethically relevant traits, cognitive, affective and identity processes) · **group** ((un)ethical leadership, power, corruption, collective action) · **organizational** (ethical infrastructure such as botched coercive reward systems, environmental pressures, ethical climates and cultures).

> [!warning] The everyday-violation problem
> Behavioral ethics research finds **more than 50%** of people engage in unethical behaviour — mostly without malicious intent, through **blind spots** where they do not recognise the behaviour as unethical. Three conditions make it likely: **ambiguous norms**, an **invisible victim**, and **unrealistic performance goals** (exactly the Wells Fargo configuration). Left unchecked, minor violations escalate.
>
> Four recommended responses: raise **awareness** of what is unethical · communicate real **consequences** · actively encourage **speaking up** (with channels, verification, and removal of retaliation risk) · build formal and informal **detection systems**, including role models and norms.

There are **few global standards** for ethical decision making. Where bribery is common, a Canadian working in China faces a genuine dilemma, and organizations that ignore cultural difference in defining ethical conduct may **encourage unethical conduct without knowing it**.

### Lying

Lying is common, partly because it is **hard to detect**: across 200+ studies, people correctly identified liars only **47% of the time** — worse than chance. Body language does no better; psychologist Maria Hartwig: *"The common-sense notion that liars betray themselves through body language appears to be little more than a cultural fiction."* The "looks up and to the right" police technique could not be substantiated, nor could facial-expression methods.

**Lying is deadly to decision making** — nobody can decide well on misrepresented facts and false motives. The lasting solution is not lie detection or entrapment (unreliable) but **building environments not conducive to lying**.

## Creativity, creative decision making and innovation

**Creativity** — the ability to produce novel and useful ideas. Novel means different from what has been done before **and appropriate to the problem**.

### The three-stage model

**Causes → Creative behaviour → Creative outcomes (innovation)**

### Creative behaviour, in four steps

1. **Problem formulation** — identifying a problem or opportunity requiring a solution that is yet unknown.
2. **Information gathering** — knowledge is sought and possible solutions incubate. The advice: think beyond routines and comfort zones — have lunch with someone outside your field, because *"you can't use buzzwords because people don't know what you mean."*
3. **Idea generation** — developing possible solutions from relevant information. **Curiosity** drives it. NASA's Curiosity rover team spent three days scribbling on whiteboards.
4. **Idea evaluation** — evaluating potential solutions to identify the best.

> [!tip] Separate the evaluators from the generators
> Two reasons. **Ideator's bias** — people derive self-worth and identity from their own ideas, so they evaluate them leniently. And **hierarchy flips role**: it *harms* idea generation (people feel less free to share) but *helps* idea evaluation, because authority figures will actually choose, where a leaderless group hesitates to voice which idea to drop.

### Causes: creative potential

| Ingredient | The finding |
|---|---|
| **Intelligence** | Some degree is necessary. Smart people are better at complex problems and have greater **working memory** — recalling more information relevant to the task. High **need for cognition** correlates with creativity |
| **Personality** | **Openness to experience** correlates (less conformist in action, more divergent in thinking), plus proactive personality, self-confidence, risk taking, tolerance for ambiguity, perseverance, hope, self-efficacy and positive affect |
| **Expertise** | The foundation of all creative work — Tarantino's video-store years. **Others'** expertise counts too: larger social networks bring exposure to diverse ideas and informal access to resources |
| **Ethics** | Genuinely unclear. Cheaters may be **more** creative (both may stem from a rule-breaking desire) — yet creative people are also better at **imagining consequences** of their actions, and so more ethical |

### Causes: creative environment

Potential is not enough. **Intrinsic motivation** — working on something because it is interesting, exciting, satisfying and challenging — correlates strongly with creative outcomes.

- **Reward and recognize creative work.** Health care team creativity translated into innovation **only when the climate actively supported it**.
- **Allow free flow of ideas** with fair, constructive judgment, and **freedom from excessive rules** over what work is done and how. Both **structural** and **psychological empowerment** relate to creativity — but a **competitive climate where achievement is valued at any cost stymies** it.
- **Job characteristics** — complex, autonomous jobs with clear role expectations for innovation.
- **Managers as brokers.** Linking teams to additional information and resources makes **radical creativity** (ideas that break the status quo) more likely — and **weaker ties** to manager networks may matter more than teams' own strong ties, because weak sources supply more divergent thinking.
- **Diversity** — **surface-level diversity is unrelated** to team creativity; **deep-level diversity raises it**, especially with interdependent tasks and collocated members ([[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]]).
- **Leadership** — transformational and empowering leadership are positively related to creativity through empowerment, LMX, trust, knowledge sharing and creative climate.

### Outcomes: innovation

**Innovations** are ideas or solutions judged **novel and useful by relevant stakeholders**. Creative behaviour does not automatically produce innovation — an employee may never share the idea, management may reject it, a team may isolate whoever proposes something different. Many people are biased against creative ideas because **ideas create uncertainty**, and uncertainty blocks the ability to see an idea as creative at all.

> [!important] "Ideas are useless unless used"
> Creative ideas do **not implement themselves**. In one agribusiness study, ideas were implemented when someone was **motivated to translate the idea into practice and had strong networking ability**. Across 100+ studies in 42 countries, **family-owned businesses** were better at converting creative ideas into innovations, likely through strong relational ties. Translating creativity into innovation is a **social process** requiring power and politics, leadership and motivation.

## Key takeaways

- **Perception becomes the reality from which we act.** It is shaped by the perceiver, the target and the situation — and people are largely unaware of the shaping.
- **Attribution** turns on distinctiveness, consensus and consistency, and is systematically warped by the **fundamental attribution error** and **self-serving bias**.
- The shortcuts — **selective perception, halo, horns, contrast, stereotyping** — infect interviews (decided in the first four or five minutes), appraisals, expectations (**self-fulfilling prophecy**) and social media screening.
- Decisions run on **bounded rationality** and **satisficing**, not the rational model. **Intuition sets hypotheses; it never counts as proof.**
- Nine biases matter enough to memorise: **overconfidence, anchoring, confirmation, availability, escalation of commitment, randomness error, risk aversion, hindsight, outcome**. The general antidote is genuinely considering **non-salient alternatives**.
- Ethics runs on three criteria (**utilitarianism, rights, justice**); **behavioral ethics** asks why good people do bad things, and finds ambiguous norms, invisible victims and unrealistic goals to be the conditions.
- **Creativity = potential × environment**, expressed through four behavioural steps — and **innovation requires the social work of implementation**, not just the idea.
