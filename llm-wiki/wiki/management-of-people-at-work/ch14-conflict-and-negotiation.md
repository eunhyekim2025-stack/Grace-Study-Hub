# Chapter 14 — Conflict and Negotiation

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch13-power-and-politics|Ch 13]] · Next: [[ch15-foundations-of-organization-structure|Ch 15 — Organization Structure]]

---

## Core summary

**Conflict** is a process beginning when one party perceives that another has negatively affected, or is about to negatively affect, something they care about. The chapter's structure:

1. **Types and loci** — functional vs dysfunctional; **task, relationship, process**; **dyadic, intragroup, intergroup**
2. **The five-stage conflict process** — potential opposition → cognition and personalization → intentions → behavior → outcomes
3. **Managing conflict**, including cross-culturally
4. **Negotiation** — **distributive vs integrative** bargaining, the **five-step process**, and **BATNA**
5. **Individual differences** — personality, mood and emotion, culture and race, gender
6. **The social context** — reputation and relationships

> [!important] The opening lesson
> Tesla's board was accused of being "asleep at the wheel," unwilling to create conflict where some was needed — partly because all the corporate information reaching them was **filtered through Musk**. The case shows that **the absence of conflict can itself be the signal** that important issues are not being discussed and controversial ideas are not being challenged.

## Types and loci of conflict

### Functional vs dysfunctional

| | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Functional conflict** | Supports the group's goals and improves performance — a **constructive** form. It is an **antidote to groupthink** ([[ch09-foundations-of-group-behavior|Ch 9]]), preventing passive rubber-stamping of decisions based on weak assumptions. It challenges the status quo, furthers new ideas, prompts reassessment of goals, and raises the odds the group responds to change |
| **Dysfunctional conflict** | Hinders group performance — a highly personal struggle for control that distracts from the task. It damages interpersonal dynamics, fosters anger, frustration and annoyance, and **divides rather than unites** |

The curvilinear relationship: **too little conflict** produces apathy, stagnation, non-responsiveness to change and no new ideas; **optimal** conflict produces a viable, self-critical, innovative unit; **too much** produces disruption, chaos and non-cooperation. Both extremes produce **low performance**.

### Three types

| Type | Definition | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Relationship conflict** | Based on **interpersonal relationships** | **Almost always dysfunctional.** Friction and hostility increase clashes and decrease mutual understanding, derailing team processes by reducing openness to collaboration and increasing avoidance and competition. It strongly depletes **trust, cohesion, satisfaction, job attitudes and positive affect**, reduces OCB and increases deviance — yet it is only **weakly related to team performance**. Its worst effects are **psychological**, and of the three it is the most **exhausting** |
| **Task conflict** | Over the **content and goals** of the work | Contested. Early research linked it to higher group performance; a review of **95 studies** found it **essentially unrelated** to group performance (lower in non-management positions, higher in decision-making teams). It slightly reduces collaboration and moderately increases competition, damaging trust and job attitudes |
| **Process conflict** | Over **how work gets done** — delegation and roles | Conflicts over delegation revolve around perceived **shirking**; conflicts over roles leave members feeling **marginalized**. They become highly personalized and **quickly devolve into relationship conflict** — and arguing about how to do something takes time from doing it. Strong negative effect on trust and attitudes; weak effect on performance |

> [!warning] Type combinations matter more than type alone
> **If task and relationship conflict occur together, task conflict turns negative**; alone, task conflict is more likely to be positive. They tend to co-occur early in a relationship and stabilize over time — and **merely perceiving relationship conflict during a task conflict** is enough to make people cling to their initial positions.
>
> **Perception matters too.** Very low task conflict means people are not engaging the real issues; very high task conflict degenerates into infighting. **Moderate levels may be optimal** — and conflict is most likely to help performance when **a few members perceive strong task disagreement while most perceive weak disagreement**, because the minority then presents its disagreement carefully, cooperatively and openly.

### Three loci

**Dyadic** (between two people) · **Intragroup** (within a group or team) · **Intergroup** (between groups).

Nearly all research addresses **intragroup** conflict. For intragroup task conflict to help performance, the team needs a **supportive climate where mistakes are not penalized** and members "have each other's back" — and it helps when members are **high in need for achievement**.

Intergroup conflict is different. Where groups compete so only one can win, it is **almost inevitable** and rivalries sprout — but intense intergroup conflict strains relationships, producing ostracism, verbal denigration, riots and threatened violence.

> [!tip] A counterintuitive finding on who should manage intergroup conflict
> Group members who were **relatively peripheral in their own group networks** were **better at resolving conflicts between their group and another**. Being at the **core** of your work group does not make you the best person to manage conflict with other groups.

## The conflict process

### Stage I — Potential opposition or incompatibility

Three categories of antecedent conditions:

**Communication.** Barriers including communication apprehension, crises and cross-cultural norm differences are potential antecedents ([[ch11-communication|Ch 11]]). Potential for conflict rises with **too little *or* too much** communication — it is functional up to a point, after which **overcommunication** raises conflict. Even framing matters: **framing task conflict as a debate** increases receptivity to others' opinions.

**Structure.** Group size, task specialization, role clarity, member–goal compatibility, leadership styles, reward systems, and degree of dependence. **Larger, more specialized groups** produce more intragroup conflict. **Tenure and conflict are inversely related** — so conflict potential is greatest where members are **younger and turnover is high**. **Shared leadership** can produce conflict where power is scarce, making people "fight over their power turfs."

**Personal variables.** Personality, emotions and values. People high in **disagreeableness, neuroticism or self-monitoring** spar more often and react worse; the **politically skilled** initiate conflict less, being better at managing perceptions. Emotions cause conflict even when not directed at anyone — an employee arriving irate from a commute carries it into a tension-filled meeting; and mismatched emotions do too (pessimistic employees, optimistic supervisors). Disagreement about **desired achievement levels** creates task conflict; about **desired interpersonal closeness**, relationship conflict; about **desired power**, status conflict.

### Stage II — Cognition and personalization

**Perceived conflict** — awareness that conditions creating conflict exist. **Felt conflict** — emotional involvement producing anxiety, tension, frustration or hostility. Stage II matters because it is where **the parties decide what the conflict is about**.

> [!warning] Emotions shape the definition
> **Negative emotions** lead us to **oversimplify issues, lose trust and put negative interpretations** on the other party's behaviour. **Positive feelings** increase the tendency to see relationships among the elements of a problem, take a broader view and develop innovative solutions.
>
> A trap: venting to a confidant feels good, but if that person is **responsive and reaffirming** they **validate your perspective** — which can undermine resolution. Better: take time to **reflect and reappraise** how you are feeling before conflict escalates (**without ruminating**), and aim for a state of **mindfulness**, which facilitates constructive conflict management.

### Stage III — Intentions

**Intentions** are decisions to act in a particular way, intervening between perception/emotion and overt behaviour. They **do not always match what we actually do**, and they are **not fixed** — they change if a party sees the other's point of view or responds emotionally.

Two dimensions produce the five **Thomas–Kilmann conflict modes**:
**Assertiveness** (attempting to satisfy your own concerns) × **Cooperativeness** (attempting to satisfy the other's).

| Mode | Position | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Competing** | Assertive, uncooperative | Satisfying your own interests regardless of the impact on others. More likely when **resources are scarce**, with competition-prone personalities, when you are **close to satisfying your own interests**, or where the culture supports competition |
| **Collaborating** | Assertive, cooperative | All parties want to fully satisfy everyone's concerns — solving the problem by **clarifying differences** rather than accommodating points of view. More likely if the party is seen as competent, rational and open to collaborating |
| **Avoiding** | Unassertive, uncooperative | Withdrawing from or suppressing conflict. Two emotion-driven forms: **withdrawal and exit** (usually driven by annoyance) and **silence** (usually driven by anxiety) |
| **Accommodating** | Unassertive, cooperative | Placing the other's interests above your own to maintain the relationship |
| **Compromising** | Midrange on both | No winner or loser — **each party intends to give up something** |

> [!important] Which intentions actually work
> Across multiple studies, **accommodating and collaborating** were associated with **superior** group performance; **avoiding and competing** with **significantly worse** — effects nearly as large as those of relationship conflict.
>
> Collaboration is **especially effective for innovation tasks** but can produce mistrust when groups splinter into subgroups of two or three by task. And **people assigned power have a harder time using collaborative strategies**.
>
> The lesson: it is **not the existence or even the type of conflict** that creates problems, but **how people respond to it and manage the process** once it arises.

### Stage IV — Behavior

Statements, actions and reactions, usually as overt attempts to implement intentions — though **miscalculation or unskilled enactment** makes behaviour deviate from intention. Competing produces contention and individual effort without collaboration; collaborating produces investigation of multiple solutions; avoidance produces refusal to discuss and reduced effort toward group goals; accommodating puts relationships ahead of issues; compromising produces mutual sacrifice.

**The conflict-intensity continuum**, from bottom to top:

no conflict → **minor disagreements or misunderstandings** → **overt questioning or challenging of others** → **assertive verbal attacks** → **threats and ultimatums** → **aggressive physical attacks** → **overt efforts to destroy the other party** (annihilatory conflict)

**Functional conflict is confined to the lower levels**; conflicts reaching the upper range are almost always dysfunctional.

### Stage V — Outcomes

**Functional outcomes**: conflict is constructive when it improves decision quality, stimulates creativity and innovation, encourages interest and curiosity, provides a medium for airing problems and releasing tension, and fosters self-evaluation and change. Over time the right amount of **task (not relational)** conflict improves team relations, social interaction quality and meaningful communication, and mild conflicts generate **energizing emotions**.

**Dysfunctional outcomes**: poor communication, reduced cohesiveness, and the subordination of group goals to infighting. Note the sting: **all forms of conflict — even the functional varieties — appear to reduce member satisfaction and trust**. At the extreme, conflict halts group functioning and threatens survival.

> [!note] The realistic managerial expectation
> Workplace conflict can be beneficial, but usually only **under special circumstances** — most of the time the dysfunctional outcomes **outweigh the functional ones in severity**. So managers will spend most of their time **reducing dysfunctional conflict rather than stimulating functional conflict**.

## Managing conflict

**Conflict management** — using resolution and stimulation techniques to achieve the desired level of conflict.

At the **organizational** level: develop conflict management systems of policies and procedures, increase lower-level management involvement, socialize dysfunctional conflict out of the culture, and improvise to adapt. Three domains to target: **strategy** (designing activities with an understanding of system effects, securing managerial support), **function** (systemic adoption of practices, understanding employee needs, job design), **worker** (improving perceptions of conflict, fostering ties and cohesion, adaptively addressing conflict as it emerges).

At the **leader** level, three modes: **instigation** (deliberately creating conflict when needed) · **engagement** (escalating, involving others, or strategically observing) · **managing** (handling or resolving).

**Four practical guidelines:**

1. **Recognize when there really is a disagreement** — encourage open, frank discussion, listen to and understand opposing views, then **integrate them constructively**
2. Have opposing groups **pick the parts of the solution most important to them** and focus on satisfying each side's top needs — neither gets everything, both get their priorities
3. **Discuss differences openly** and prepare to manage conflict before it arises — **the most disruptive conflicts are those never addressed directly**
4. **Emphasize shared interests** so groups do not entrench and take conflicts personally — cooperative styles with strong identification with overall goals beat competitive styles

> [!warning] Conflict-management strategies leak
> Strategies suited to one form of conflict can **backfire and create another**: adopting a **shared identity** resolves relationship quality but may **blur role distinctions**, stimulating task conflict. And they **spill over**: clearly outlining who does what during a task conflict can stimulate **relational** conflict. Hence the move toward a **strategic systems view** — treating conflict as a chain of events full of thoughts, emotions and motivations, approached flexibly and adaptively.

**Cross-culturally:** **collectivists** see people as embedded in connective social networks, so they seek to preserve relationships, **avoid direct expression** of conflict, prefer indirect methods and **third parties**, and favour **compromising and avoiding**. **Individualists** confront differences directly and openly, use **competing** tactics more, and are more likely to see offers as unfair and reject them. (Though interviews suggest top management teams in **collectivist high-tech firms prefer collaboration** over compromising and avoiding.)

Cross-cultural negotiation also breeds **trust problems**: Indian and US negotiators reported **less trust in cross-culture counterparts**, which meant **less discovery of common interests**, because they were less willing to disclose and solicit information. Both US and Chinese negotiators showed **ingroup bias**, favouring partners from their own cultures — for Chinese negotiators especially when **accountability requirements were high**.

## Negotiation

**Negotiation** — a process in which two or more parties communicate and confer to reach a mutual agreement on the exchange of goods or services. (Used interchangeably with *bargaining*.)

> [!important] Every negotiation is also a relationship
> We think of outcomes in one-shot transactional terms, but **every negotiation in an organization also affects the relationship between negotiators and how they feel about themselves**. Depending on future interaction, **maintaining the relationship and behaving ethically can matter as much as the immediate outcome**.

### Two bargaining strategies

| Characteristic | **Distributive** | **Integrative** |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Get as much of the pie as possible | **Expand the pie** so both parties are satisfied |
| **Motivation** | Win–lose | Win–win |
| **Focus** | **Positions** ("I can't go beyond this point") | **Interests** ("Can you explain why this issue is so important to you?") |
| **Interests** | Opposed | Congruent |
| **Information sharing** | Low — sharing only lets the other side take advantage | High — sharing lets each find ways to satisfy the other |
| **Duration of relationship** | Short term | Long term |

**Distributive bargaining** operates under **zero-sum** conditions over a **fixed pie**. Each party has a **target point** (what they would like) and a **resistance point** (the lowest acceptable outcome, beyond which they walk). The gap is the **aspiration range**; where the two ranges overlap lies the **settlement range**.

> [!tip] Anchoring — and its limits
> Making the first offer exploits the **anchoring bias** ([[ch06-perception-and-decision-making|Ch 6]]), and scores of studies show anchors **greatly favour the setter**. **Precision amplifies it**: ask \$50,000 and the other side considers \$40–60k; ask **\$51,000** and they consider \$50–52k.
>
> Three costs. It **conveys information about your priorities** to a counterpart motivated to maximize their own return. **Framing matters** — a first proposal framed as a **request** ("I request your B for my A") hurts you compared with an **offer** ("I offer my A for your B"), because it highlights a **loss** for the other party. And an overly precise offer made **before negotiations have really begun** can make the other party **less willing to negotiate**, reading you as inflexible.

**Hardline vs softline.** It depends on the outcome you want: **softline** (eliciting concessions through your own) preserves the **relationship**; **hardline** (minimal concessions, extreme offers) produces a **higher economic return**. Hardline works best when **physically interacting**, when the other party is male, when both are motivated to maximize individual outcomes, and when both know what is bargainable. Softline works only where you can adequately give concessions.

Two softline devices: **phantom anchors** ("I was going to ask for \$10,000, but since you're making that concession I'll offer \$8,000") communicate a concession even when overstated. And **multiple equivalent simultaneous offers (MESOs)** — presenting several equivalent first offers to choose from — make you appear **flexible and sincere** and raise the chance of an immediate fit. When one attractive alternative is later withdrawn, the negotiator becomes **more assertive and engaged** (having experienced loss) and gets better outcomes, though **less satisfied with the process**.

**In salary negotiation:** when asked what starting salary you want, **you have the chance to set the anchor** — ask for the highest salary you think the employer could reasonably offer. Asking for a million looks ridiculous; the far more common error is **asking for less than you could have obtained**. Go in knowing industry rates and trends, build your case beforehand, and **make it about the value you provide**, not about you.

**Integrative bargaining** assumes a settlement can create win–win rather than zero-sum. All parties must be engaged; it is **more likely with multiple negotiators than with two**, since two people more readily see binary win–lose trade-offs. Technique matters: **silent pauses between offers and counteroffers** signal that you are genuinely considering the proposal and working to create value.

> [!important] Sequence the two within one episode
> **Integrative first, distributive second.**
> ① **Clarify needs** — understand your own and the other party's ② **Articulate interests**, not positions ③ **Avoid early compromise** — compromising too soon produces sub-optimal agreements before both sets of interests are on the table ④ **Set high goals** ⑤ **Slice the pie** — draw down progressively smaller concessions.
>
> The illustration: an employee who prematurely accepted "half a day off per week" would have missed the far better arrangement available once both sides said what they actually wanted — the employer needed coverage only during a **core time**, and was fine with flexibility outside it.

**Why integrative bargaining is rarer than it should be.** It builds long-term relationships, bonds negotiators, and lets both leave feeling they won — while distributive bargaining builds animosity and deepens divisions among people who must keep working together. But it requires conditions: parties **open with information**, **candid about concerns**, **sensitive to the other's needs**, **trusting** and **flexible**. Evidence from nearly 200,000 people found the **financially vulnerable** more likely to construe negotiations in **zero-sum terms**, which cuts off integrative solutions.

## The negotiation process

**Five steps:**

| Step | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **1 · Preparation and planning** | **The most important part.** What is the history? Who is involved and how do they see it? Write your goals with a range from "most helpful" to "minimally acceptable." Then assess the **other party's** goals, intangible or hidden interests, and what they might give up — **people who underestimate their opponent's willingness to give end up with lower outcomes** |
| **2 · Definition of ground rules** | Who negotiates, where, under what time constraints, on which issues, and what happens at an impasse. Initial proposals are exchanged |
| **3 · Clarification and justification** | Explain, amplify, clarify, bolster and justify your original demands. **Need not be confrontational** — it is an opportunity to educate each other on the issues and provide supporting documentation. **Framing matters here** |
| **4 · Bargaining and problem solving** | The actual give-and-take, where both sides concede. **Relationships change as a result** — if you could "win" but push the other side into resentment, a more compromising style may be wiser. People who feel good about the **process** of a job-offer negotiation are more satisfied and **less likely to leave a year later regardless of the actual outcome** |
| **5 · Closure and implementation** | Formalizing the agreement and building procedures to implement and monitor it — a formal contract for major negotiations, a handshake for others |

### BATNA

**BATNA** — the **best alternative to a negotiated agreement**; the least a party should accept. It determines the lowest value acceptable to you: any offer above your BATNA beats an impasse, and you should not expect success unless you can offer the other side something **more attractive than their BATNA**.

> [!warning] Three complications
> **The party with superior alternatives usually does better**, so solidify your BATNA before any interaction — but there is an exception: negotiators with **absolutely no alternative** sometimes "go for broke," because they never consider what happens if the negotiation collapses.
>
> A BATNA safeguards against an inferior agreement but is **not a route to the optimal one**. Some advise focusing on **mutual dependence rather than alternatives**, and framing the negotiation positively — "a learning experience with hidden potential," not "a frightening minefield."
>
> **Phantom BATNAs**: setting a BATNA does not make it probable or even possible. Holding one leaves you having to accept or reject a final offer when your supposed alternative **never existed**.

> [!note] Intuition fails in negotiation
> The ethical implications of a decision are apparent while preparing, then **fade during the negotiation** as pragmatic concerns take over. Negotiators use an **insider lens** (deeply engaged, intuitive) by default and an **outsider lens** (removed, rational) only deliberately — and the outsider lens is what prevents bias from producing irrational decisions.
>
> And on preparation specifically: research comparing **empathizing** (feeling what the other party feels) with **perspective taking** (seeing the world from their point of view) found people are **better prepared when they imagine how their counterpart is thinking, not how they feel**.

## Individual differences in negotiation

**Personality.** Personality and negotiation outcomes are only **weakly related**. People who are **ambitious and likeable** fare best. **Agreeableness** is only weakly related to outcomes overall — though agreeable negotiators do worse against partners **low in honesty or humility**, and worse in distributive negotiations if they seek to preserve the relationship at all costs. What matters more is **similarity**: negotiators with similar personalities (even traits others see as negative) reach agreement faster, perceive less conflict, display more positive emotions and form better impressions of each other.

**Self-efficacy** is the one individual difference **consistently** related to outcomes — confident negotiators stake stronger claims, back down less and project intimidating confidence; the less confident, and those experiencing threats to self-worth, are less assertive and do worse. But **do not overdo it**: negotiating with someone who has power over you (a supervisor who will judge your performance) makes preserving the relationship worth more, and a **sense of entitlement** produces excessive demands, unethical behaviour and a damaged reputation.

**EI and emotion recognition** are linked to performance gains and to being seen as more cooperative and likeable. An **honest reputation** engenders trust and reduces attempts to deceive you — *but break it and watch out*.

### Moods and emotions

> [!important] Anger works, conditionally
> Anger can induce concessions because the other side concludes no further concessions are possible. **Five conditions:**
> ① **Power** — show anger only if you have **at least as much power** as your counterpart; with less, it provokes hardball reactions.
> ② **Genuineness** — "faked" anger from **surface acting** does not work; **deep-acted** genuine anger does ([[ch04-emotions-and-moods|Ch 4]]).
> ③ **Do not suppress it either** — suppressing anger hurts performance by distracting the negotiator, when the anger is **integral** to the negotiation.
> ④ **History helps** — a track record of showing anger induces more concessions, as the negotiator is read as "tough."
> ⑤ **Culture** — East Asian negotiators showing anger induced **more** concessions than US or European ones, apparently because they are perceived as **less quick to anger**.
>
> **The long-run cost:** negative emotions **spill over** between negotiations with the same partner, producing worse subsequent outcomes — and someone who "lost" to your anger is **less likely to follow through** with the deal and less likely to work with you again.

**Other emotions:**

- **Anxiety** — anxious negotiators use **deception more often**, expect lower outcomes, respond to offers faster and exit sooner, producing worse outcomes
- **Sadness** — elicits concessions, but only when the other negotiator sees the expresser as **lower in power**, anticipates future interaction and the relationship is collaborative
- **Sympathy** — displaying it appeals to perceptions of rationality and fairness, but **excessive** sympathy and empathy, while improving the relationship, **hurts value capture** by shifting focus to one person's situation
- **Unpredictability** — negotiators who express positive and negative emotions unpredictably **extract more concessions**, because it makes the other party feel less in control. **Ambivalence**, by contrast, produces more integrative agreements and concession-making, since the ambivalent person reads as submissive

### Culture and race

**People generally negotiate more effectively within cultures than between them.** Two implications: choose cross-cultural negotiators **high in openness**, and avoid factors such as **time pressure** that inhibit learning about the other party. Be aware of emotional dynamics — East Asian cultures do not see anger as a desirable or legitimate tactic, so counterparts may **refuse to cooperate** when you become upset.

Language differs too: in the US, language emphasizing **cognitive, rational, logical gains and losses** promotes integrative bargaining; in Egypt the same language **fails**, and language emphasizing **honour, moral integrity, and protecting one's image and strength** is preferable. Rituals also matter — in Western cultures a **handshake** signals cooperative intent.

> [!warning] Race in negotiations
> Black job seekers in the US are ① **expected to negotiate less** than White job seekers ② **penalized when they do negotiate** ③ **penalized more heavily when the negotiator is more racially biased**.

### Gender

The stereotype is that women negotiate more cooperatively and get worse outcomes. Decades of research show the influence of gender is **highly context dependent**, with very slight differences favouring men — and women may be **more effective** in collectivist cultures with high harmony and low assertiveness norms.

| Aspect | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Behaviour** | Women behave **slightly** less assertively, less self-interestedly, more cooperatively and concessionally — differences that **shrink when communication is restricted**. Women are actually **more competitive than men** when the counterpart plays "tit-for-tat." And **men behave more unethically** in negotiations, acting more opportunistically and morally disengaging |
| **Initiation** | Women are **less likely to initiate**, especially where the situation is **ambiguous** and where gender-role-reinforcing information is present. **52% of men vs 40% of women** report having ever negotiated their pay |
| **Outcomes** | An updated meta-analysis found **no significant difference**. Men's apparent advantage shrank when controlling for **negotiation experience and knowledge of the bargaining zone**, when negotiating **on behalf of others**, and when **gendered role norms were not activated**. One analysis of thousands of negotiators found the gap existed **only when women had a strong outside alternative** |

> [!tip] Toward gender equity
> **Organizational culture** — where an organization even unwittingly reinforces gender-stereotypic negotiating behaviour, anyone going against stereotype is penalised. Men and women both need to know the **full range of negotiating behaviours is acceptable**. Designing negotiations around **well-defined, work-related terms** minimises the ambiguous space in which stereotypes operate — and incidentally focuses negotiations on what improves organizational performance.
> **Stereotype reduction** — long term, gender identity awareness and eliminating role stereotypes is the answer. But there is a notable exception to the general disapproval of "blindness": research shows **gender-blindness in negotiation contexts empowers women to act and builds confidence**, moving attention from gender to the resources actually being exchanged.

## Negotiating in a social context

Most organizational negotiations are **open-ended and public** — with someone you already know and will work with again, and whose outcome people will talk about.

**Reputation** — the way other people think and talk about you. A reputation for being **trustworthy** opens the door to integrative strategies that benefit both sides. The way to build it is **behaving honestly across repeated interactions**, which makes others comfortable making open-ended offers. People with a reputation for **integrity** are seen as likelier to keep promises and present information accurately, so others accept their promises as part of a bargain — and they are **better liked, with more friends and allies**, giving them more implicit power.

**Relationships** — with repeated negotiation, people move beyond valuing only what is good for themselves toward what is best for the other party and the relationship. Trust broadens the option set, since **a favour today can be repaid later**. Repeated negotiation also facilitates integrative problem solving, because people come to see partners more personally and share emotional bonds.

> [!important] The closing point
> **An effective negotiator needs to think about more than the outcomes of a single interaction** — about the relationship they have and will continue to have with the partner once an agreement is reached.
>
> On ethics: **the larger the reward, the more likely we are to lie**, and people lie **more when negotiating with groups** than with one person, because group interactions feel less personal — which people use to justify unethical behaviour. Two countermeasures: **build relationships** (negotiators use fewer unethical tactics with someone they know and trust, since the relationship is at stake) and **negotiate in person** (direct face-to-face contact reduces ethically ambiguous tactics compared with email or phone).

## Key takeaways

- **Conflict is a perception**, and its relationship with performance is **curvilinear** — too little produces stagnation, too much produces chaos.
- **Relationship conflict is almost always dysfunctional** and the most psychologically exhausting; **task conflict** is essentially unrelated to performance on the evidence, and turns negative when it **co-occurs** with relationship conflict; **process conflict** quickly becomes relationship conflict.
- The **five stages**: potential opposition → cognition and personalization (**where the parties define what the conflict is about**) → intentions → behavior → outcomes.
- Of the five **conflict-handling intentions**, **accommodating and collaborating** produce superior group performance; **avoiding and competing** produce significantly worse. **How people respond matters more than the conflict itself.**
- **Distributive** bargaining divides a fixed pie; **integrative** expands it. Sequence them: **integrative first (clarify needs, articulate interests, avoid early compromise), distributive second (set high goals, slice the pie).**
- **Anchor first and be precise** — but know the costs. **BATNA** sets your floor, and **phantom BATNAs** are a real trap.
- **Anger induces concessions only with equal-or-greater power, genuine feeling, and at long-run relational cost.**
- Gender **outcome** differences essentially vanish once experience, bargaining-zone knowledge and role-norm activation are controlled — but women still **initiate less**.
- **Reputation and relationships** make integrative bargaining possible; negotiate in person and build relationships to keep it ethical.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Conflict** | A process beginning when one party perceives another has negatively affected something they care about |
| **Functional / dysfunctional conflict** | Supports group goals and improves performance / hinders performance |
| **Relationship / task / process conflict** | Based on interpersonal relationships / over content and goals / over how work gets done |
| **Dyadic / intragroup / intergroup conflict** | Between two people / within a group / between groups |
| **Perceived / felt conflict** | Awareness that conditions for conflict exist / emotional involvement producing anxiety and hostility |
| **Competing / collaborating / avoiding / accommodating / compromising** | The five conflict-handling intentions |
| **Conflict management** | Using resolution and stimulation techniques to reach the desired level of conflict |
| **Negotiation** | Two or more parties conferring to reach mutual agreement on an exchange |
| **Distributive bargaining** | Dividing a fixed amount of resources — a win–lose situation |
| **Fixed pie** | The belief that there is only a set amount to be divided |
| **Integrative bargaining** | Seeking settlements that create a win–win solution |
| **BATNA** | The best alternative to a negotiated agreement — the least a party should accept |
