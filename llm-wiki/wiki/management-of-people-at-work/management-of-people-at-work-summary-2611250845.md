## AI Adoption: Psychology, Biases & Organizational Impact  

AI adoption is not just a technical rollout; it is a socio‑technical change that must account for human behavior.

> [!info] **AI project failure rate** – >70 % of AI initiatives fail because organizations treat AI as a pure engineering problem and ignore behavioral dimensions.  

### Core Psychological Barriers  

> [!info] **Algorithm aversion** – Users lose trust after a few errors, even when the algorithm outperforms humans overall.  

> [!info] **Fairness concerns (uniqueness neglect)** – Uniform AI treatment sparks resistance in high‑stakes domains such as hiring and medicine.  

> [!info] **Black‑box problem** – Model internals are opaque; this lowers confidence despite the fact that human cognition is also opaque.  

> [!info] **Pro‑human bias** – Preference for human decisions masks the fact that humans are equally (or more) biased via their training data.  

> [!info] **Processing fluency** – Concrete human credentials (e.g., years of experience) are trusted more than abstract algorithmic attributes (e.g., data source).  

> [!info] **Dual perception** – AI is simultaneously seen as a magical savior and a dangerous monster, echoing historic tech cycles (e.g., electricity).  

> [!warning] **Over‑reliance & awe** – “Magical” framing can cause users to over‑trust AI, assuming supernatural capabilities.  

### Anti‑AI Bias in the Workplace  

- AI‑assisted workers receive lower compensation and are judged as **lazier, more replaceable, less competent, less diligent, and less warm**.  
- Leadership potential ratings drop for AI‑augmented individuals because implicit leadership theory equates leadership with hard work.  
- Freelancers/gig workers suffer the steepest penalties; formal contracts can mitigate the bias.  
- Companies are more likely to cut headcount when AI is involved in fixed‑pay scenarios.  
- Many hide AI use (“secret cyborg”) to preserve a competent image, which hampers knowledge sharing.  

| Bias effect | Typical outcome |
|------------|-----------------|
| Pay & compensation | Lower wage offers for AI‑assisted workers |
| Leadership perception | Reduced rating of leadership potential |
| Job security | Higher likelihood of headcount cuts |
| Reputation | Perceived as lazy or replaceable |
| Transparency | Concealment of AI use (“secret cyborg”) |

> [!example] **Pay experiment** – Participants evaluated a partnership “Steve + AI” versus “Steve alone.” The AI‑augmented pair achieved a **46 % win‑win score**, indicating a clear devaluation of AI‑assisted workers in compensation decisions.  

### Human‑Centered AI Design  

> [!info] **Behavioral human‑centered AI** – Design that explicitly incorporates creator and user psychology, mitigating inventor’s bias (the tendency of creators to over‑estimate benefits and under‑estimate risks).  

> [!warning] Ignoring behavioral factors leads to project failure even when the underlying model is technically superior.  

- Treat AI adoption as an ongoing socio‑technical program: continuous monitoring, iterative tweaks, and alignment with evolving organizational contexts.  

### Preparing for an AI‑Augmented Future  

1. **Foresight exercise** – Teams pick a profession and predict a plausible AI‑driven transformation by 2035 using first‑principles reasoning.  
2. **Second‑order consequences** – Identify resources that become abundant or scarce and the resulting value shifts.  
3. **Reverse‑engineer preparation** – Decide today which skills will make you an early adopter of the predicted shift.  

*Sample prediction*: AI‑augmented design tools enable rapid, innovative creation.  
*Second‑order consequence*: Face‑to‑face collaboration declines → communication & empathy become premium skills.  

**Recommended skill focus**  
- Soft skills: communication, empathy, relationship building.  
- Emerging tech awareness: autonomous/flying vehicles, generative AI.  
- AI security, ethics, and auditability.  

> [!info] **“Stay where the puck is going”** – Prioritize future‑oriented skill development over defending current roles.  

### Broader Societal Trends  

- The middle class is projected to capture the largest share of AI‑driven benefits (finance, law, etc.).  
- Rapid AI rollout can cause “languishing,” a mild, persistent demotivation distinct from clinical depression.  
- Demand for meaningful human experiences (bespoke travel, curated events) will rise as automation handles routine tasks.  
- Transparency & auditability become critical: open‑weight, value‑aligned models allow stakeholders to contest decisions; closed‑source frontier models lack explainability.

## Decision‑Making Biases & Team Effectiveness  

Understanding how defaults, framing, and other biases shape choices, and how to build high‑performing teams, is essential for effective people management.

### Decision‑Making Biases  

> [!info] **Default effect** – Pre‑set options dramatically shift choice frequencies; e.g., opt‑out organ‑donation in Singapore raises consent from ~20‑30 % to ~80‑90 %.  

> [!info] **Framing effect** – Different wording (“how happy?” vs. “how unhappy?”) leads to different responses.  

> [!info] **Bounded (or founded) rationality** – Decisions are made with limited information and cognitive capacity.  

> [!info] **Selection & survivorship bias** – Use representative samples; the WWII plane‑repair case missed damage on planes that never returned.  

> [!info] **Sunk‑cost fallacy** – Ignore past investments; evaluate only future expected outcomes.  

> [!info] **Overconfidence / better‑than‑average effect** – People routinely rate themselves above the median.  

> [!info] **Power distance** – Low power distance facilitates rapid crisis coordination; high distance hinders information flow.  

> [!warning] **Avoid** basing decisions on non‑representative data or on past sunk costs; these introduce systematic errors.  

### Group vs. Team  

> [!info] **Group / Work group** – ≥2 individuals who interact but have low interdependence; accountability is individual; output ≈ sum of parts.  

> [!info] **Work team** – Interdependent members, shared explicit objective, positive synergy (output > sum); accountability is collective.  

| Criterion            | Group / Work group | Team / Work team |
|----------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Interdependence      | Low / none        | High            |
| Shared objective     | Weak / optional   | Strong, explicit|
| Synergy              | Neutral/negative  | Positive (> sum)|
| Accountability       | Individual        | Collective      |

- Example contrast: **Audience at a concert** = neither group nor team (no coordinated goal).  
- **Car‑dealership floor** ≈ work group; occasional hand‑offs create brief team‑like interactions.  

### Team Types & Effectiveness  

**Four common team types**  
1. **Problem‑solving** – tackle a specific issue.  
2. **Self‑managed** – self‑directed, interdependent.  
3. **Cross‑functional** – blend functions for a project.  
4. **Virtual** – geographically dispersed, tech‑mediated.  

**Three pillars of effectiveness**  
1. **Composition** – diversity (personality, culture, function), clear roles, optimal size.  
2. **Processes & states** – conflict management, cohesion, motivation, psychological safety, transactive memory.  
3. **Context** – resources, leadership, structure, culture, incentives, technology, crisis conditions.  

> [!info] **Assess any team** by asking: *Composition? Processes/States? Context?*  

### Bias‑Mitigation Interventions  

- **Financial incentives / nudges** – steer healthier or safer choices.  
- **System design** – prompts, training modules, serious‑games.  
- **Pre‑mortem analysis** – imagine future failure, work backward to identify preventive actions.  

### Desert‑Survival Ranking Exercise (Worked Example)  

> [!example] **Steps**  
1. **Individual ranking** – each participant orders 15 salvaged items (1 = most essential).  
2. **Team ranking** – the group has **50 minutes** to reach consensus on a single unified list; no edits to personal lists after the ranking phase begins.  
3. **Submission** – all members record the agreed team list; instructor gives a final 5‑minute warning.  

- **Items discussed**: rain/water, air map, compass, cosmetic mirrors, flashlights, temperature‑management code, pistol, etc.  
- **Key debate**: whether rain is needed only for shelter or is essential for survival.  
- **Rule**: once the ranking phase starts, **no edits** to individual lists; the team must submit one combined ranking.
