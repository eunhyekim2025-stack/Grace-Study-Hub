## Chapter 6 — Perception & Decision Making

**Theory & concepts**
- People decide under **bounded rationality** — limited information and cognitive capacity — so the *way a choice is presented* moves the outcome as much as its substance.
- Classic biases to control for: the **default effect**, **framing**, **survivorship/selection bias**, the **sunk-cost fallacy**, **overconfidence**, and the effect of **power distance** on who speaks up.

> [!info] **Default effect** — pre-set options dramatically shift choices; opt-out organ donation in Singapore lifts consent from ~20–30% to ~80–90%.
> [!info] **Framing effect** — wording ("how happy?" vs "how unhappy?") changes the answer.
> [!info] **Survivorship bias** — the WWII plane case: armour was nearly added to the *wrong* spots because damage on planes that never returned was unseen.
> [!warning] **Sunk-cost fallacy** — ignore past spend; judge only future expected value.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Bounded rationality | Deciding with limited information and cognitive capacity. |
| Default effect | Influence of pre-set options on choice frequency. |
| Framing effect | Change in response caused by how a question is worded. |
| Survivorship / selection bias | Drawing conclusions from a non-representative, "surviving" sample. |
| Sunk-cost fallacy | Continuing because of past investment rather than future value. |
| Overconfidence (better-than-average) | Tendency to rate oneself above the median. |
| Power distance | Expected acceptance of unequal power; high distance slows crisis information flow. |

**Bias-mitigation interventions**
- **Nudges / financial incentives** steer safer or healthier choices without removing freedom.
- **System design** — prompts, training modules, serious games.
- **Pre-mortem** — imagine the project has failed, work backward to the preventable causes.

## Chapters 9–10 — Foundations of Group Behavior & Understanding Work Teams

**Theory & concepts**
- A **work group** is ≥2 people who interact but with **low interdependence** and **individual** accountability — output ≈ the sum of parts. A **work team** is **interdependent**, shares an explicit goal, and produces **positive synergy** (output > sum) under **collective** accountability.
- Assess any team on three pillars: **composition** (diversity, roles, size), **processes & states** (conflict management, cohesion, psychological safety, transactive memory), and **context** (resources, leadership, structure, culture, incentives).

| Criterion | Group / Work group | Team / Work team |
|-----------|--------------------|------------------|
| Interdependence | Low / none | High |
| Shared objective | Weak / optional | Strong, explicit |
| Synergy | Neutral / negative | Positive (> sum) |
| Accountability | Individual | Collective |

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Work group | Low-interdependence collection; output ≈ sum of parts. |
| Work team | Interdependent members, shared goal, positive synergy. |
| Team composition | Diversity of personality/culture/function, role allocation, size. |
| Team processes & states | Conflict management, cohesion, motivation, psychological safety, transactive memory. |
| Psychological safety | Shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is safe. |
| Transactive memory | Collective awareness of who holds which expertise. |
| Four team types | Problem-solving, self-managed, cross-functional, virtual. |

**Cases & examples**
- *Concert audience* = neither group nor team (no coordinated goal); *car-dealership floor* ≈ work group with brief team-like hand-offs.
- **Desert-Survival ranking**: members rank 15 salvaged items alone, then the team has **50 minutes** to reach one consensus list (no edits to personal lists once ranking starts) — a live test of how a group becomes a team.

## Week 11 — AI and the Future of Work

**Theory & concepts**
- AI adoption is a **socio-technical** change, not a pure engineering rollout: **>70% of AI projects fail** when the behavioral dimension is ignored.
- Trust barriers: **algorithm aversion** (trust collapses after a few errors even when AI is better on average), **fairness / uniqueness neglect**, the **black-box problem**, **pro-human bias**, and **processing fluency** (concrete human credentials feel more trustworthy than abstract model attributes). AI is held in **dual perception** — magical savior and dangerous monster.
- **Anti-AI bias**: AI-assisted workers are rated lazier, less competent and more replaceable, get **lower pay and leadership ratings**, and so hide their use ("**secret cyborg**"), which blocks knowledge sharing. **Human-centered AI** design counters the creator's **inventor's bias** and iterates continuously.

> [!example] **Pay experiment** — "Steve + AI" vs "Steve alone": the AI-augmented pair scored only a 46% win-win, revealing clear devaluation of AI-assisted work in compensation.
> [!info] **"Stay where the puck is going"** — prioritise future-oriented soft skills (communication, empathy), plus AI ethics, security and auditability, over defending today's role.

**Key terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Algorithm aversion | Loss of trust in algorithms after a few errors despite overall superiority. |
| Fairness concerns (uniqueness neglect) | Resistance to AI's uniform treatment in high-stakes domains (hiring, medicine). |
| Black-box problem | Opacity of model internals that lowers confidence. |
| Pro-human bias | Preference for human decisions that masks equally biased human judgement. |
| Anti-AI bias | Systematic penalising of AI users in pay, leadership perception and job security. |
| Secret cyborg | Employee who uses AI covertly to avoid stigma. |
| Human-centered AI | Design incorporating creator/user psychology; mitigates inventor's bias. |
| Second-order consequence | Indirect effect of an AI change on the value of other resources/skills. |

**Beyond the textbook**
- **Foresight exercise**: pick a profession, predict a plausible 2035 AI transformation from first principles, map the **second-order consequences** (what becomes scarce/abundant), then reverse-engineer which skills to build now.
- Societal trends: the middle class may capture the largest share of AI benefit (finance, law); rapid rollout can cause **"languishing"**; demand rises for meaningful human experiences; **transparency & auditability** (open-weight, value-aligned models) let stakeholders contest decisions.
