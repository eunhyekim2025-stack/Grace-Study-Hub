# Chapter 5 — Personality and Individual Differences

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch04-emotions-and-moods|Ch 4]] · Next: [[ch06-perception-and-decision-making|Ch 6 — Perception & Decision Making]]

---

## Core summary

Personality is **the total number of ways in which an individual reacts to and interacts with the world**, described through **personality traits** — enduring characteristics that appear frequently across situations and persist over time.

The chapter's five moves:

1. **Fit** comes first: organizations no longer just match people to jobs (**person–job fit**, Holland) but to the whole organization (**person–organization fit**), plus group and supervisor fit.
2. **Two frameworks, very unequal evidence.** The **MBTI** is the most used and among the least defensible; the **Big Five** is the scientific standard. The **Dark Triad** covers the socially undesirable side, and **HEXACO** and **DiSC** are contenders.
3. **Three further traits matter for OB**: core self-evaluations, self-monitoring, proactive personality.
4. **Situation matters as much as trait** — **situation strength** suppresses personality, **trait activation** draws it out. The nature/nurture debate resolves as nature **and** nurture.
5. **Ability and values** are the other two stable inputs: intellectual and physical abilities, and terminal versus instrumental values.

> [!important] The corrective the chapter keeps making
> Traits predict **averages and tendencies**, never a whole person, and their predictive power is **conditional on the situation**. The strongest single finding to carry: **conscientiousness is the best overall predictor of job performance** — and even it has pitfalls at the extreme.

This is the textbook counterpart to [[management-of-people-at-work/mpw-2-1|MPW 2-1]], which covers the same MBTI-vs-Big-Five argument from the lecture.

## Linking individuals to the workplace: fit

Organizations once cared about personality mainly to match people to **specific jobs**. Today managers are less interested in the ability to perform one job than in the ability to **further the mission and stay** — so fit is assessed more broadly.

### Person–job fit — Holland's typology

**Personality–job fit theory** identifies six personality types and proposes that satisfaction and propensity to leave depend on how well personality matches the job. It is measured with the Vocational Preference Inventory (160 occupational titles) and is well supported: person–job fit strongly predicts **job satisfaction, organizational commitment and intention to quit**.

| Type | Prefers | Characteristics | Congruent occupations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Realistic** | Physical activity requiring skill, strength, coordination | Shy, genuine, persistent, stable, conforming, practical | Mechanic, assembly-line worker, farmer |
| **Investigative** | Thinking, organizing, understanding | Analytical, original, curious, independent | Biologist, economist, mathematician, news reporter |
| **Artistic** | Ambiguous, unsystematic activity allowing creative expression | Imaginative, disorderly, idealistic, emotional, impractical | Painter, musician, writer, interior decorator |
| **Social** | Helping and developing others | Sociable, friendly, cooperative, understanding | Social worker, teacher, counselor, clinical psychologist |
| **Conventional** | Rule-regulated, orderly, unambiguous activity | Conforming, efficient, practical, unimaginative, inflexible | Accountant, corporate manager, bank teller, file clerk |
| **Enterprising** | Verbal activity with chances to influence and gain power | Self-confident, ambitious, energetic, domineering | Lawyer, real estate agent, PR specialist, small business manager |

> [!note] Fit is culturally conditioned
> In **individualistic** countries, where workers expect to be heard, tailoring the job to the person raises satisfaction. In **collectivistic** countries person–job fit is a **weaker** predictor, because people do not expect a job built around them — so managers there should **select people who will thrive in already-structured jobs** rather than redesign jobs individually. Autonomy in job design also pays off more for **older** employees, who know their own strengths and interests better.

### Person–organization fit

People are **attracted to and selected by** organizations matching their values, and **leave** organizations that are not compatible. A values–culture match predicts high satisfaction and commitment and low turnover.

Two subtleties: people care more about how an organization's values would be preferred by **the typical person** than about their own idiosyncratic preferences; and fit perceptions are **relatively automatic affective judgments**, not deliberate comparisons.

This is why online image management matters — job seekers treat websites and social media as part of the pre-application process, and improvements to both **usability and substance** (policies, CSR) generate more applicants. One study of founders' religious-values messaging found co-religionists more likely to apply, but **most applicants less likely** to apply when a company messaged about religious values at all.

### Other dimensions

**Person–group fit** matters in team settings; **person–supervisor fit** matters because poor fit lowers satisfaction and performance — and there is even truth to "misery loves company," since leader–follower **cynicism congruence** predicts follower performance.

Collectively these are **person–environment fit**. A meta-analysis across East Asia, Europe and North America found person–organization and person–job fit strongest as predictors in **North America**, less so in Europe, and **least important in East Asia**.

## Personality

**Personality** — the total number of ways in which an individual reacts to and interacts with the world around them.
**Personality traits** — enduring characteristics that describe an individual's behaviour. The more consistent over time and the more frequent across situations, the more important the trait is in describing that person.

> [!note] Personality itself is culturally patterned
> Many traits emerge across cultures, but some do not, and unique ones appear. Research in Chinese contexts has uncovered distinct traits focused on **interpersonal relatedness** together with a relative **absence of openness traits**.

### Measuring personality

**89 of the Fortune 100** and **57% of all large US companies** use personality assessments, and an estimated 200 federal agencies pay for personality testing in training. Usage by level: executives 32%, middle managers 28%, individual contributors 20%, hourly workers 17%.

| Method | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-report survey** | Cheapest, most common | **Faking** — people rate themselves much higher on desirable traits when scores are used for hiring than when taking it to learn about themselves |
| **Observer ratings** | **Predicts job success better than self-ratings alone**; raters who know you well are more accurate than strangers | Requires a competent observer — EI affects rating accuracy |

**The best answer is both.** A combination of self-report and observer report predicts performance better than either alone.

AI now helps: machine learning selects the most informative items, detects fakers, infers personality from social-media language, and even scores applicants' personal essays.

## Personality frameworks

### The Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

A 100-question test sorting people into 16 types across four binaries: **Extroverted/Introverted · Sensing/Intuitive · Thinking/Feeling · Judging/Perceiving**. It is the most widely used personality instrument in the world.

> [!warning] Five problems with the MBTI
> 1. **Unscientific origin** — built subjectively on Carl Jung's neo-Freudian theories. "Jung's theories are not considered to be solid. He wasn't an empiricist. He didn't collect data."
> 2. **No validity evidence** — most of the evidence runs against it. Dan Ariely: *"Next time, just look at the horoscope. It is just as valid and takes less time."*
> 3. **Forced binaries** — you are introverted or extroverted, with no in-between.
> 4. **Unstable** — people often get different results on retake.
> 5. **Interpretation is difficult** — facets carry different weights and combinations have separate meanings, requiring trained interpretation.
>
> **And the one that matters most for OB: it does not predict job performance or other important organizational outcomes.**

So why the popularity? Merve Emre's account: the test is written to be so **nonjudgmental** that every result is desirable and appealing, and it satisfies the desire to know yourself in a simple way — an "appealing fantasy" of coherent self-understanding.

### The Big Five Model

Five basic dimensions encompassing most differences in human personality. Scores predict real-life behaviour well and stay relatively stable over time, with daily variation.

| Trait | High scorer | Low scorer |
|---|---|---|
| **Conscientiousness** | Responsible, organized, dependable, persistent | Easily distracted, disorganized, unreliable |
| **Emotional stability** | Calm, self-confident, secure; positive, optimistic, fewer negative emotions, generally happier | Nervous, anxious, insecure |
| **Extroversion** | Gregarious, assertive, sociable; more positive emotions, freely expressed | Thoughtful, reserved, timid, quiet |
| **Openness to experience** | Creative, curious, artistically sensitive | Conventional, comfortable with the familiar |
| **Agreeableness** | Cooperative, warm, trusting; usually people's **first choice** for a team | Cold, antagonistic |

### How each trait behaves at work

**Conscientiousness — the strongest predictor.** "Personal attributes related to conscientiousness are important for success across many jobs, spanning low to high levels of job complexity, training, and experience." Conscientious employees develop higher **job knowledge** (they learn more, and it links to GPA), perform better, engage in more OCB and fewer CWBs, think less about leaving, adapt to changing task demands, behave less unsafely and have fewer accidents. Among **313 CEO candidates** at private equity companies, conscientiousness — as persistence, attention to detail and setting high standards — mattered more to success than communication, teamwork, flexibility, enthusiasm or listening.

> [!warning] Even conscientiousness has a ceiling
> Extremely conscientious people can be **too deliberate and perfectionistic**, reducing happiness and performance (task, safety and OCB alike). They may become **too focused on their own work to help others**, and they are often **less creative**, especially artistically.

**Emotional stability** — most strongly related of the five to **life and job satisfaction**, and to reduced burnout and intention to quit. High scorers adapt to unexpected demands; low scorers may burn out, experience work–family conflict and ostracism, engage in more CWB, less OCB, and be less motivated.

**Extroversion** — a small, persistent career advantage. A review of **97 published meta-analyses** supports the "extrovert advantage," and attributes it primarily to the **positive emotions extroverts experience**, not (as usually assumed) to social skill. Extroverts adapt better to career change, have high satisfaction and lower burnout, and extroversion is a **relatively strong predictor of leadership emergence**. Downside: they can appear dominating and are prone to risk-taking, which hurts in jobs without frequent social interaction.

**Openness** — creative and innovative, more likely to be effective leaders, more comfortable with ambiguity, coping better with organizational change. Not related to *initial* job performance, but open individuals are **less susceptible to performance decline over time**. Also less work–family conflict.

**Agreeableness** — better in interpersonally oriented jobs like customer service; less work–family conflict, less turnover, more OCB, less CWB, less likely to be ostracized. But **associated with lower career success, especially earnings**, perhaps because highly agreeable people are less willing to assert themselves. Agreeableness is a **stronger predictor of organizational commitment in collectivistic than individualistic cultures** (review of ~20,000 employees).

> [!tip] For expatriate assignments
> **Extroversion** is the most important trait predicting expatriate adjustment, followed by **emotional stability** and **openness** — corroborated by a study of 2,500 international exchange students where extroversion significantly reduced the stress of a new culture.

### The Dark Triad

Three socially undesirable traits everyone has to some degree. **Not clinical pathologies** — they show up most strongly when someone is under stress and unable to moderate inappropriate responses. They may have short-term upsides, but sustained high levels derail careers and personal lives. One study of 101 hedge fund managers found narcissistic and psychopathic managers had **worse financial performance** than peers.

| Trait | Definition | At work |
|---|---|---|
| **Machiavellianism** | Being pragmatic, maintaining emotional distance, believing ends justify means ("if it works, use it") | High Machs manipulate more, win more, are persuaded less and persuade more. More aggression and CWB — and they will even do **OCB instrumentally** to acquire power or status. But Mach **does not positively predict overall job performance**: short-term wins are lost long-term because they are not well liked. High-Mach job seekers care less about CSR |
| **Narcissism** | Arrogance, a grandiose sense of self-importance, need for excessive admiration, entitlement | Largely unrelated to effectiveness or OCB (except where it makes them look good), but **one of the largest predictors of CWB in individualistic cultures** — not in collectivist cultures that discourage self-promotion. They think they are overqualified, tune out disconfirming feedback but work harder for rewards, and select subordinates who give non-critical feedback (silencing everyone's voice). **Upside:** more charismatic, more likely to be chosen as leaders, and **medium** narcissism correlates positively with leadership effectiveness |
| **Psychopathy** | A lack of concern for others and lack of guilt or remorse when actions cause harm | Evidence is inconsistent: little correlation with performance or CWB in one review; the closely related antisocial personality was **positively related to advancement** but unrelated to other career success. Related to hard influence tactics (threats, manipulation), bullying, and inappropriate meeting behaviour without remorse. The cunning may **gain power but not use it toward healthy ends** |

> [!note] The narcissism debate, illustrated
> Michael Maccoby argues for **"productive narcissists"** — leaders whose narcissism supplies the confidence to convince others of an idealistic vision (Grove, Jobs, Gates). Charles O'Reilly counters that narcissistic CEOs are more likely to commit fraud and white-collar crime, and to fire or ignore anyone who challenges them. His practical test: interviews are **easy for a narcissist to manipulate**, so gather **ratings from people who actually worked with them**.

### Other frameworks

**DiSC** — a circumplex model clustering people into types by primary characteristic: **D**ominating, **i**nfluencing, **S**teadiness, **C**onscientiousness, while acknowledging "people are not their types." Popular as an MBTI alternative, but **few academic studies have examined its validity**.

**HEXACO** — the Big Five plus **honesty-humility (H)**: sincere, fair, modest, humble, uninterested in status, wealth or money. The H dimension emerged from cross-cultural work (Korean, Hungarian and other non-European studies) that revealed a dimension the English-centric original research had missed. It predicts ethically relevant outcomes such as **abstaining from cheating even under temptation** — directly relevant where employees might cut corners or steal.

## Three further OB-relevant traits

| Trait | Definition | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Core self-evaluations (CSE)** | Bottom-line conclusions about one's capabilities, competence and worth | Positive CSEs → set more ambitious goals, commit to them, persist longer; better customer service, more popular, more creative solutions, faster-ascending careers. Adapt better to career change and to new cultures. **Downside:** excessively high CSEs are viewed unfavourably, especially if the person seems to avoid helping others |
| **Self-monitoring** | The ability to adjust behaviour to external, situational factors | High self-monitors adapt readily and read external cues, sometimes showing striking contradictions between public and private selves. They show **less organizational commitment** but get **better performance ratings**, emerge as leaders more, are more career-mobile and occupy more central positions. **Downside:** may be seen as inauthentic, self-serving or unprincipled |
| **Proactive personality** | Identifying opportunities, showing initiative, taking action and persevering until meaningful change occurs | Higher performance and creativity, need little oversight (so get more autonomy), more satisfied, committed and engaged, achieve career success. Teams with proactive members are more innovative and build more trust. **Downside:** more likely to be **envied**, and subject to social undermining or withheld help |

## Personality and situations

### Situation strength theory

**Situation strength** is the degree to which norms, cues or standards dictate appropriate behaviour. Strong situations show what the right behaviour is, pressure you to exhibit it and discourage the wrong one; in weak situations "anything goes." Therefore **personality traits predict behaviour better in weak situations than strong ones**.

Four components (the four C's):

| Component | Meaning | Strong ← → Weak |
|---|---|---|
| **Clarity** | How available and clear the cues about duties are | Janitor ← → Hollywood actor's agent |
| **Consistency** | How compatible those cues are with one another | Acute care nurse ← → manager |
| **Constraints** | How limited freedom to decide or act is by outside forces | Bank examiner ← → forest ranger |
| **Consequences** | How important decisions are for the organization and others | Surgeon ← → language teacher |

> [!warning] Why organizations should not simply maximize situation strength
> Beyond the obvious basics (nobody should feel free to harass or falsify accounts), three costs appear: ① tightly ruled jobs are **dull and demotivating** ② strong situations **suppress innovation** ③ when strong situations block a preferred way of working, **other traits break through anyway** — a policy that stops your conscientiousness from shining may activate your disagreeableness into CWB, ignoring the policy or shirking out of anger at it.
>
> The positive use: a construction supervisor who prioritises safety creates a strong situation whose norms both promote safety behaviour **and** leave less room for low-conscientiousness personality to express itself.

### Trait activation theory (TAT)

**TAT** predicts that some situations, events or interventions **"activate" a trait more than others**. Commission-based pay activates **extroversion** (extroverts are more reward-sensitive); jobs encouraging creativity make **openness** the better predictor.

| Job demand | Trait activated |
|---|---|
| Detail orientation required (air traffic controller, accountant) | Conscientiousness (+) |
| Social skills required (clergy, therapist, concierge) | Extroversion (+), Agreeableness (+) |
| Competitive work (coach/scout, financial manager, sales rep) | Extroversion (+), **Agreeableness (−)** |
| Innovation required (actor, systems analyst, advertising writer) | Openness (+) |
| Dealing with angry people (correctional officer, telemarketer, flight attendant) | Extroversion (+), Agreeableness (+), Emotional stability (+) |
| Time pressure / deadlines (news analyst, editor, airline pilot) | Conscientiousness (+), Emotional stability (+) |

TAT works negatively too: electronic monitoring activated **fear of failure** in online learners, who then learned significantly less. And positively: supportive coworkers activated **conscientiousness**, producing a stronger sense of duty and more information sharing.

> [!important] Nature *and* nurture
> Together the two theories reframe the old debate. Personality and situation both affect work behaviour, **and they interact** — when the situation is right, the power of personality to predict behaviour is even higher.

## Ability

**Ability** — an individual's current capacity to perform the various tasks in a job. Two families: intellectual and physical.

### Intellectual abilities

The capacity to do mental activities — thinking, reasoning, problem solving. Smart people generally perform better, earn more, are promoted more often and reach higher-level jobs. But the link is **conditional on complexity**: where tasks are highly routine with no discretion, high IQ **is not as important**.

The seven most-cited dimensions:

| Dimension | Job example |
|---|---|
| **Number aptitude** — speedy, accurate arithmetic | Accountant computing sales tax |
| **Verbal comprehension** — understanding what is read or heard | Plant manager following hiring policy |
| **Perceptual speed** — identifying visual similarities and differences quickly | Fire investigator identifying arson clues |
| **Inductive reasoning** — identifying a logical sequence and solving | Market researcher forecasting demand |
| **Deductive reasoning** — using logic to assess an argument's implications | Supervisor choosing between two employee suggestions |
| **Spatial visualization** — imagining an object repositioned in space | Interior decorator redecorating an office |
| **Memory** — retaining and recalling past experience | Salesperson remembering customer names |

These correlate positively enough that researchers recognise an overall factor — **general mental ability (GMA)**, found in 73.2% of countries in a study of 50,000+ individuals. IQ scores vary somewhat across cultures, but the gap is narrowing and shrinks considerably once education and economics are accounted for.

The **Wonderlic Ability Test** — 50 questions in **twelve minutes**, measuring both speed (almost nobody finishes) and power (questions get harder). Average score is about **21 out of 50**. It is used by Aveda, Duracell, Subway and U-Haul as a cheap source of valid data, **added to** rather than replacing applications and interviews.

> [!warning] Two cautions on ability testing
> **Disparate impact.** Intellectual ability tests in hiring may produce disparate impact ([[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]]). Managers should monitor whether a test is excluding members of underrepresented or protected groups.
> **Intelligence is not satisfaction.** Intelligence helps performance but **does not make people happier or more satisfied**. High-cognitive-ability high performers may even be **victimized, bullied and mistreated** by peers out of envy and social comparison.

### Physical abilities

Nine basic abilities identified across hundreds of jobs. Performance is highest when the job's requirement on each ability matches the employee's:

- **Strength factors** — dynamic strength · trunk strength · static strength · explosive strength
- **Flexibility factors** — extent flexibility · dynamic flexibility
- **Other** — body coordination · balance · stamina

Physical ability tests raise discrimination problems since men score substantially better on average; physical fitness training can raise the proportion of women meeting minimum qualifications. The general rule: **do not use ability measures for HR purposes unless the ability is genuinely required for the job**.

## Values

**Values** — relatively stable, enduring basic convictions that some actions and outcomes are more morally, socially or personally preferable than others. They carry a **judgmental element** (ideas about what is right, good or desirable) and have two attributes:

- **Content** — that a mode of conduct or end-state *is* important
- **Intensity** — *how* important it is

Ranking values by intensity produces a **value system**. Values lay the foundation for attitudes and motivation and shape perception; most are established early by parents, teachers and friends, and are more often reinforced than changed. Personality partly determines them — open people value pursuing new ideas, agreeable people value caring and cooperation.

Values can also **cloud objectivity**. If you believe pay should follow performance and join an organization that rewards seniority, dissatisfaction follows — and so does reduced effort ("it's probably not going to lead to more money anyway").

> [!note] Espoused values vs lived values
> Wells Fargo stated it puts "customers at the center of everything we do" — while employees opened millions of fake accounts and enrolled customers in online banking without consent to hit sales goals. Fred Perrotta's line, quoted in the chapter's Uber case: **"Your values are what you live, not what you write."**

### Terminal versus instrumental values

Rokeach's split:

| | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Terminal values** | Desirable **end-states** — the goals a person wants to achieve in a lifetime | Prosperity and economic success, freedom, health and well-being, world peace, meaning in life |
| **Instrumental values** | Preferable **modes of behaviour** — the means of achieving terminal values | Autonomy and self-reliance, personal discipline, kindness, goal-orientation |

**Schwartz** organized these into ten dimensions, evidenced across twenty countries: achievement, hedonism, stimulation, self-direction, universalism, benevolence, tradition, conformity, security, power. Some promote effectiveness and treating others well (universalism, benevolence); some cling to what is customary (tradition, conformity, security); some are self-enhancing (power, achievement).

### Generational values — handle with care

Cohorts (baby boomers, Gen X, millennials, Gen Z) are fascinating to think about, but **the classifications lack solid research support**.

> [!warning] What the evidence actually shows
> - Early research had **methodological problems** making it hard to tell whether differences exist at all, and reviews find many generalizations **overblown or incorrect**.
> - Leisure valuation *has* risen and work centrality declined from boomers to millennials — but millennials did **not** show more altruistic work values.
> - Research has **failed to support** that baby boomers have a higher work ethic.
> - On the "participation trophy" charge: a study of 10,000+ people found **no generational differences in entitlement** — and suggested **baby boomers become more entitled as they age**.
> - Testing whether major events shape a cohort's values, generational values in China were **largely similar before, during and after the Social Reform era**.

## Key takeaways

- **Fit is the organizing idea**: person–job (Holland's six types), person–organization (values vs culture), person–group and person–supervisor — collectively person–environment fit, and strongest as a predictor in North America.
- The **MBTI** is the most used and among the least valid instruments: unscientific origin, forced binaries, unstable retests, and **no prediction of job performance**. Use the **Big Five**.
- **Conscientiousness is the best overall predictor of job performance**; **emotional stability** best predicts satisfaction; **extroversion** predicts leadership emergence and carries a positive-emotion advantage; **agreeableness** helps in service roles but is linked to **lower earnings**.
- The **Dark Triad** is not clinical, surfaces under stress, and mostly costs more than it pays — narcissism being the interesting exception at **medium** levels.
- **Situation strength** suppresses trait expression; **trait activation** elicits it. Nature *and* nurture, interacting.
- **Ability** splits into intellectual (seven dimensions → GMA) and physical (nine abilities). Use ability tests only where the ability is genuinely required, and watch for **disparate impact**.
- **Values** are terminal (ends) or instrumental (means); **generational value claims are largely unsupported**.
