# Chapter 1 — What Is Organizational Behavior?

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Next: [[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2 — Diversity, Equity & Inclusion]]

---

## Core summary

Business schools historically taught the technical half of management — accounting, finance, quantitative method — and left the human half implicit. **Organizational behaviour (OB)** is the field that fills that gap: a distinct body of knowledge about how individuals, groups and structure shape behaviour at work, used to make organizations more effective.

The chapter makes five moves:

1. **Defines the cast** — worker, manager, organization — and describes what managers do through four complementary lenses (functions, roles, skills, activities).
2. **Argues for systematic study** over intuition, and introduces **evidence-based management (EBM)** together with its modern extensions, big data and AI.
3. **Names the parent disciplines** — psychology, social psychology, sociology, anthropology.
4. **Warns that OB has few absolutes**: findings are contingent, so the honest form of an OB claim is *"x leads to y, under conditions z."*
5. **Builds the OB model** — inputs → processes → outcomes, at three levels of analysis — which is the architecture of every remaining chapter.

> [!important] The one sentence to keep
> OB is not "soft skills." It is the systematic, evidence-based study of behaviour at work — and its outcome variables (performance, citizenship, withdrawal, productivity, survival) are exactly the ones the business cares about.

---

## Why OB matters

The practical case, as the chapter argues it:

- **Interpersonal skills decide advancement.** A survey of over 2,100 CFOs across 20 industries put *lack of interpersonal skills* as the top reason employees fail to advance. Job-posting analysis puts communication at the top of the desired-skills list, followed by problem-solving and influence.
- **Good workplaces perform better financially** — firms known as good places to work generate superior financial performance, and developing interpersonal skills helps attract and retain scarce high performers.
- **Relationships drive attitudes.** A study of hundreds of workplaces and 200,000+ respondents found social relationships with coworkers and supervisors strongly related to job satisfaction, lower stress and lower intent to quit.
- **Managers are often unprepared.** More than 58% of managers reported receiving no management training; 25% admitted they were not ready to lead when given the role. A Gallup poll found organizations chose the wrong candidate for management positions 82% of the time.

The four **employability skills** the text targets: think analytically and critically · make better decisions · communicate and collaborate · act with social responsibility.

## Who's who, and what managers do

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Worker** | An individual who contributes to the accomplishment of work goals |
| **Manager** | An individual who achieves goals **through other people** |
| **Organization** | A **consciously coordinated social unit**, of two or more people, functioning on a relatively continuous basis to achieve a common goal or set of goals |

That definition of an organization covers firms, schools, hospitals, churches, military units, nonprofits and government agencies alike — this is the same definition developed in [[management-of-people-at-work/mpw-1-1|MPW 1-1]].

### Lens 1 · The four management functions

| Function | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Planning** | Defining goals, establishing strategy, developing plans to coordinate activities. The need for it grows most sharply moving from lower- to mid-level management |
| **Organizing** | Determining what tasks are done, by whom, how grouped, who reports to whom, where decisions are made |
| **Leading** | Motivating employees, directing activity, choosing communication channels, resolving conflict |
| **Controlling** | Monitoring performance against goals and correcting significant deviations |

### Lens 2 · Mintzberg's ten managerial roles

| Family | Roles | The gist |
|---|---|---|
| **Interpersonal** | Figurehead · Leader · Liaison | Ceremonial duties; hiring/motivating/disciplining; maintaining an outside network |
| **Informational** | Monitor · Disseminator · Spokesperson | Receiving information as the unit's nerve centre; passing it in; representing outward |
| **Decisional** | Entrepreneur · Disturbance handler · Resource allocator · Negotiator | Initiating projects; corrective action under surprise; allocating human/physical/monetary resources; bargaining |

Mintzberg derived these from close observation of executives in the early 1970s, and research indicates the roles have changed remarkably little since.

### Lens 3 · The three management skills

- **Technical skills** — applying specialized knowledge or expertise. Not only school-taught; much is developed on the job.
- **People skills** — understanding, communicating with, motivating and supporting others. The bottleneck skill, since managers work *through* people.
- **Conceptual skills** — the mental ability to analyze and diagnose complex situations; identifying problems, generating and evaluating alternatives, then organizing and executing a plan.

### Lens 4 · Luthans — effective ≠ successful

Luthans and associates studied 450+ managers across four activity categories, and split the sample two ways: **successful** (fast promotion) versus **effective** (performance quality plus employee satisfaction and commitment).

| Activity | Average manager | Successful (promoted fast) | Effective (performs well) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Traditional management | 32% | 13% | 19% |
| **Communication** | 29% | 28% | **44%** |
| HR management | 20% | 11% | 26% |
| **Networking** | 19% | **48%** | 11% |

> [!warning] The finding that stings
> Successful and effective managers give almost **opposite** emphases. Networking dominates promotion; communication dominates actual effectiveness. This directly challenges the assumption that promotion tracks performance — and it flags **political skill** as a real determinant of getting ahead (developed further in [[ch13-power-and-politics|Ch 13]]).

## Complementing intuition with systematic study

You have been "reading" people your whole life, and casual reading produces confident but erroneous predictions. The premise of the field is that **behaviour is not random**: consistencies exist, so behaviour is broadly predictable, and predictability is what makes management possible.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Systematic study** | Looking at relationships, attributing cause and effect, and drawing conclusions from scientific evidence gathered under controlled conditions |
| **Evidence-based management (EBM)** | Basing managerial decisions on the best available scientific evidence — pose a question, search for evidence, apply it |
| **Intuition** | An instinctive feeling not necessarily supported by research |

EBM is a **balancing act** across three evidence streams: scientific evidence, evidence collected from your own business, and the opinions of experts and respected practitioners. It is not a ban on intuition — Jack Welch's line was "the trick is to know when to go with your gut" — but deciding purely on gut means deciding on half the data.

Why intuition alone fails:

- We **overestimate what we know**. Many "commonsense" management beliefs are flatly refuted by evidence, and managers hold onto them anyway — reacting defensively when evidence threatens their beliefs, self-image, self-interest or identity.
- The **business press runs on fads**. Successful companies get scrutinized for underlying truths, but often there is no underlying truth — they happened to be in the right place at the right time. Without a systematic approach you cannot tell which is which.
- One review of hundreds of studies found **data-driven judgments about 10% more accurate** than human intuitive judgment.

This is the same argument developed at length in [[management-of-people-at-work/evidence-based-management|Evidence-Based Management (Pfeffer & Sutton)]].

### Big data and AI

**Big data** — extensive statistical compilation and analysis — began in online retail marketing. Amazon's problem was distinguishing statistics that were **persistent** (stable over time) from **predictive** (steady causality between input and outcome), then building algorithms on the latter.

Current uses cluster into three purposes: **predicting** events, **detecting** risk, and **preventing** catastrophes. **Machine learning** (software that improves from incoming data and feedback) takes about 60% of AI investment; roughly a third of Amazon transactions are estimated to come from AI-facilitated recommendations.

Novel OB data sources: robotics · AI/machine learning · serious games and gamification · "always-on" devices · mobile sensors and biometrics · social media · text and sentiment analysis.

> [!warning] Limits worth remembering
> **Privacy and surveillance.** Surveillance may raise task performance and citizenship behaviour in the short term, but the Hawthorne studies suggested productivity is driven by whether workers believe management cares and by group norms — social forces, not the watching itself. Taylor's 1911 surveillance analytics were ultimately surpassed by Sloan's success through meaningful work.
> **Atheoretical prediction.** An unsupervised algorithm might include pizza-topping preference in predicting workplace theft. Machine learning needs supervision to avoid nonsense predictors.
> **Behaviour stays elusive.** Management is more than the sum of the data.

## The parent disciplines

| Discipline | Unit of analysis | Contributes |
|---|---|---|
| **Psychology** | Individual (micro) | Learning, motivation, personality, emotions, perception, leadership effectiveness, job satisfaction, individual decision making, attitude measurement, work stress, selection, work design, performance appraisal |
| **Social psychology** | Group | Behavioural and attitude change, communication, group processes, group decision making |
| **Sociology** | Group / organization system | Formal organization theory and structure, organizational technology, change, culture, communication, power, conflict, intergroup behaviour |
| **Anthropology** | Organization system | Comparative values and attitudes, cross-cultural analysis, organizational culture and environment |

The study of emotions and moods produced what the text calls an **"Affective Revolution"**, whose influence runs across essentially every OB topic ([[ch04-emotions-and-moods|Ch 4]]).

## There are few absolutes in OB

Physical-science laws generalize; humans do not. Two people act differently in the same situation, and the same person acts differently across situations — you behave differently in a job interview than with friends on a Saturday.

So OB claims must carry **contingency variables**: situational factors that moderate the relationship between two variables. The honest form is **"x leads to y, but only under conditions z."**

Often both a general effect *and* contingencies operate at once. Money motivates most people (general effect), but some are more money-motivated than others and some situations are more about money than others (contingencies). Understanding OB means holding both.

## Challenges and opportunities

| Challenge | The OB angle |
|---|---|
| **Workforce diversity and inclusion** | **Diversity** = the workforce is heterogeneous in gender identity, age, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation and more. **Inclusion** = creating and maintaining workplaces that *support and leverage* that diversity, where workers feel involved, welcomed, valued and treated as insiders. Rising longevity plus falling birth rates mean a lasting shift to an older workforce → [[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]] |
| **Continuing globalization** | Worldwide integration and interdependence across borders. Practical consequences: **expatriate** assignments (one estimate: ~\$311,000 per year, with ~40% ending in failure) and working with people from different cultures — different motivators, different norms around directness, different legal and financial regulation |
| **Technology and social media** | Hiring/discipline questions around social media; the "always-on" culture; the workplace redefined as anywhere a laptop goes; belonging problems for virtual workers; 1 in 4 employees showing burnout signs; work–life balance named by 56% of respondents as their definition of career success |
| **(Un)ethical behaviour** | **Ethical dilemmas** = situations requiring you to define right and wrong conduct. **Unethical behaviour** violates widely accepted moral norms. Note the nuance: not all unethical behaviour is counterproductive — an entrepreneur's "legitimacy lie" about production capacity may help the firm and is still immoral |
| **Corporate social responsibility (CSR)** | Self-regulated action to benefit society or environment **beyond what the law requires**. Positively linked to attitudes, turnover intentions, performance — and especially to OCB. A 42-study meta-analysis links CSR to financial performance |
| **Positive work environments** | **Positive organizational scholarship** studies how organizations develop strengths, foster vitality, build resilience and unlock potential — rather than only diagnosing what is broken |
| **The Gig Economy** | A new era where many work independently and autonomously ("a company of one"). About one-fifth of US workers work independently; gig employment rose 5.7% over 2005–2015, and nearly all employment growth in that decade was attributable to gig work |
| **OB during crises** | Recessions and COVID-19 shifted the salient topics from rewarding and retaining toward stress, decision making and coping |

> [!note] CSR's three failure modes
> The chapter is careful not to sell CSR uncritically. It disappoints when ① projects are not equally meaningful to everyone yet participation is expected; ② the organization prescribes *how* you must contribute; ③ the initiative feels disconnected from the employee's real work. Pressuring people to go "above and beyond" in ways unnatural to them burns them out — especially when the CSR visibly benefits the organization's own PR. People want CSR to be **genuine**.

## The OB model

A **model** is an abstraction of reality — a simplified representation of a real-world phenomenon. The book's model proposes **three types of variables** across **three levels of analysis**.

| | **Inputs** *(the nouns)* | **Processes** *(the verbs)* | **Outcomes** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Individual** | Diversity · personality · values | Emotions and moods · motivation · perception · decision making | Attitudes and stress · job performance · citizenship behaviour · withdrawal behaviour |
| **Group** | Group structure · group roles · team responsibilities | Communication · leadership · power and politics · conflict and negotiation | Team performance |
| **Organizational** | Structure · culture | HR management · change practices | Productivity · survival |

- **Inputs** are variables that set the stage, mostly determined in advance of the employment relationship — personality and values from genes and childhood; group roles assigned at formation; structure and culture built over years.
- **Processes** are actions that individuals, groups and organizations take as a result of inputs, leading to outcomes.
- **Outcomes** are the key variables you want to explain or predict.

**Outcomes loop back into future inputs** — which is why the model is a cycle, not a pipeline.

### The outcome variables, defined

| Outcome | Definition | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Attitudes** | Evaluations employees make, positive to negative, about objects, people or events | Attitudes have behavioural consequences; satisfied and fairly treated employees are more willing to engage in citizenship behaviour |
| **Stress** | A psychological process occurring in response to environmental pressures | → [[ch18-stress-and-health|Ch 18]] |
| **Job performance** | The total value of a worker's contributions through their behaviours over a period of time | **Task performance** — how well the specific tasks of the job are accomplished — is a major component, but performance also includes communicating, initiative, persistence and lifting teammates |
| **Organizational citizenship behaviour (OCB)** | Discretionary behaviour, not part of formal job requirements, contributing to the psychological and social environment | Organizations with such employees outperform those without |
| **Withdrawal behaviour** | The set of actions employees take to separate themselves from the organization | Lateness → absenteeism → turnover. Average direct cost of unscheduled absence ≈ 6.7% of payroll; replacing an employee costs roughly 0.5–2× salary |
| **Team performance** | The quantity and quality of a team's work output | More than the sum of individual task performances |
| **Productivity** | The combination of **effectiveness** (meeting clientele needs) and **efficiency** (achieving ends at low cost) | A hospital is effective when it meets patient needs, efficient when it does so cheaply |
| **Organizational survival** | The degree to which an organization can exist and grow over the long term | Depends on productivity **and** fit with the environment — a firm efficiently making things nobody values does not survive |

> [!tip] Turnover: zero is not the goal
> Turnover splits into voluntary (quitting), involuntary without cause (layoff), and involuntary with cause (firing). In 2019 roughly 62% of US turnover was voluntary. Janet Yellen's point: people quit because they are **optimistic** about outside prospects. If the *right* people leave — the poorer performers — quits open promotion paths, refresh ideas and reduce the need for management-initiated layoffs. High turnover usually signals withdrawal and hurts performance, but the useful management question is **who is leaving and why**, not how to get the number to zero.

## Key takeaways

- **OB = individuals + groups + structure → behaviour → organizational effectiveness.** Three determinants, one applied purpose.
- Managers can be described four ways: **functions** (plan/organize/lead/control), **Mintzberg's ten roles**, **three skills** (technical/people/conceptual), and **Luthans' four activities**.
- **Successful ≠ effective**: networking gets you promoted, communication makes you effective.
- Replace casual reading of people with **systematic study**; practise **EBM** by weighing science, local data and expertise together — intuition is an input, not a verdict.
- **Few absolutes.** Every OB claim carries **contingency variables**: *x → y, under conditions z*.
- The **OB model** (inputs → processes → outcomes, across individual/group/organizational levels) is the map for Chapters 2–18, and outcomes feed back into future inputs.
