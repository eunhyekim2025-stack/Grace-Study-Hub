# Chapter 7 — Motivation Concepts

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch06-perception-and-decision-making|Ch 6]] · Next: [[ch08-motivation-applications|Ch 8 — Motivation Applications]]

---

## Core summary

**Motivation** — the processes that account for an individual's **intensity, direction and persistence** of effort toward attaining a goal. It is both a personality trait and a temporary state.

The chapter is organized as a verdict on theories:

1. **Three classics from the 1950s** — Maslow, Herzberg, McClelland — are the vocabulary managers still use, and all three are of **questionable validity**.
2. **Contemporary theories** split into three families: **content** (what fundamentally motivates), **context** (how the environment conditions behaviour), **process** (how people choose and strive toward goals).
3. **Organizational justice** gets its own treatment because fairness is both a motivational input and an ethical requirement.
4. An **integrating model** built on expectancy theory shows how the surviving theories fit together rather than compete.

> [!important] Why the classics are still taught
> They are intuitively logical, easy to understand, and most managers worldwide are familiar with their recommendations. Knowing them is about **speaking the language**; deciding with them is not evidence-based practice.

---

## Motivation defined

$$\text{Motivation} = \text{intensity} \times \text{direction} \times \text{persistence}$$

- **Intensity** — how hard a person tries. The element most people mean by "motivation" — and **high intensity does not produce good performance unless the effort is channelled in a direction that benefits the organization**.
- **Direction** — effort aimed at and consistent with organizational goals.
- **Persistence** — how long effort is maintained. Motivated people work intensely on an appropriate task **long enough** to reach the goal.

Remote workers' self-reported biggest struggles illustrate both the needs view and the goals view: not being able to unplug (27%), loneliness and distractions at home (16% each), staying motivated (15%), collaboration/communication (12%), time zone differences (7%).

## The classic theories

### Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Five needs, each becoming dominant as the previous one is satisfied:

| Level | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Self-actualization** | Growth, achieving potential, self-fulfilment |
| **Esteem** | Internal — self-respect, autonomy, achievement; external — status, recognition, attention |
| **Social-belongingness** | Affection, love, belongingness, acceptance, friendship |
| **Safety** | Security and protection from physical and emotional harm |
| **Physiological** | Hunger, thirst, shelter, other bodily needs |

> [!warning] Two corrections
> **Maslow never drew the pyramid.** He outlined need categories and suggested some may take precedence *depending on the situation*. The ladder/pyramid is a later popularization.
> **Most research does not support its validity**, and it has not been frequently researched since the 1960s. Be aware of its public acceptance; do not treat psychological needs as a complete portrait of workplace motivation.

### Herzberg's two-factor theory

Herzberg asked people to describe situations in which they felt exceptionally good or bad about their jobs, and found the two lists **did not mirror each other**.

| | Factors | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Motivators** (intrinsic) | Achievement, recognition, the work itself, responsibility, advancement, growth | Produce **satisfaction** when present |
| **Hygiene factors** (extrinsic) | Company policy and administration, supervision, relationship with supervisor, work conditions, salary, relationship with peers | Produce **dissatisfaction** when inadequate; when adequate, they produce **no satisfaction** |

> [!important] The dual continuum
> The opposite of "satisfaction" is **"no satisfaction"**, and the opposite of "dissatisfaction" is **"no dissatisfaction."** Removing dissatisfying characteristics does not make a job satisfying — it **placates** rather than motivates.

**Criticisms:** the original methodology and its assumptions — people recalling times they felt good or bad are biased recallers — and the logical point that if hygiene and motivational factors are equally important to a person, **both should motivate**. The theory has not been well supported, but it has been highly influential.

### McClelland's theory of needs

Needs as **motivating factors** rather than survival prerequisites:

| Need | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Need for achievement (nAch)** | The need to excel or achieve to a set of standards |
| **Need for power (nPow)** | The need to make others behave in a way they would not have otherwise |
| **Need for affiliation (nAff)** | The need to establish friendly and close interpersonal relationships |

**High achievers perform best at a perceived probability of success of about 0.5** — a fifty-fifty chance. They dislike easy odds because there is no challenge to their skills, and dislike near-impossible odds. (In the chapter's bonus scenario, choosing the Level 3 goal that only half of attempters reach marks you as a high achiever.)

Supporting findings: high nAch employees show more positive moods, are more interested in the task, and perform very well in **high-stakes conditions** such as walkthroughs or sales encounters. Teams composed of high-**nAff** employees perform best, communicate most openly and have the least conflict.

> [!warning] Why it is hard to use
> The degree to which we hold each need is **difficult to measure**. One behaviour can serve many needs and many behaviours can serve one need, so needs are hard to isolate. The concepts are helpful; they are rarely used objectively.

## Contemporary theories: content-based

### Self-determination theory (SDT)

A **meta-theory** — a collection of related theories — proposing that well-being and performance are shaped by **the nature of the motivation**, not just its amount. Its key distinction: motivation is either **autonomous** (freely chosen) or **controlled** (produced by others' pressure or direction). Data from ~40,000 schoolteachers showed autonomous motivation had pronounced effects on well-being, stress and teaching quality.

**Cognitive evaluation theory (CET)** — extrinsic rewards reduce intrinsic interest in a task. The chapter's illustration: someone who loved volunteering fifteen hours a week at the Humane Society stopped finding it fun once hired full-time at \$11/hour doing identical work. When you are paid for work, it feels less like something you *want* to do and more like something you *must* do.

> [!tip] The precise finding on rewards
> **Intrinsic motivation contributes to the quality of work; incentives contribute to the quantity of work.** And the effects of intrinsic motivation are **weaker when incentives are directly tied to performance** — a bonus per call in a call centre.

**Self-concordance theory** — how strongly people's reasons for pursuing goals match their interests and core values. People pursuing self-concordant goals are more satisfied, fit their organizations better and may perform better. Across cultures, people pursuing goals from intrinsic interest are more likely to attain them, happier when they do — and **happy even when they fail**, because they feel competent and well-fitted. Those working from obligation can still perform acceptably, but at **higher levels of strain**.

**Three basic psychological needs:**

| Need | Meaning | What it predicts best |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Feeling in control and self-directed at work | **Attitudinal and affective outcomes** — the most important of the three |
| **Competence** | Feeling good at what you do and proud of it | **Performance** |
| **Relatedness** | Positive connections with others (very close to nAff) | Belonging outcomes |

The managerial implication: design motivating jobs, provide recognition, support growth and development — **especially during onboarding**, when employees are still deciding whether the job fits. Employees can satisfy these needs by helping others — but **do not pressure them to help each other**.

### Regulatory focus theory

People differ in how they regulate thought and behaviour during goal pursuit, falling into two **foci** (and they can hold both, and shift between them as motivational states):

| Focus | Strategy | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Promotion focus** | Striving for advancement and accomplishment; approaching conditions that move you toward goals | **Task performance, citizenship behaviour, innovation** |
| **Prevention focus** | Fulfilling duties and obligations; avoiding conditions that pull you away from goals | **Safety performance** |

Studying for an exam illustrates both: reading the material (promotion) versus refraining from video games (prevention).

**Fit matters more than which one.** COVID-19 compliance messaging worked best when matched: promotion-focused people responded to *"what you can do to help you stay healthy"*, prevention-focused people to *"what you can do to keep America safe."* Regulatory focus is **contagious and trickles down** from managers, and matching your supervisor's focus predicts higher relationship quality, commitment and supervisor-directed OCB.

### Job engagement theory

**Job engagement** — the investment of an employee's **physical, cognitive and emotional energies** into job performance. The nurse who becomes completely absorbed, loses track of time, and feels uplifted by the work.

> [!note] Why engagement appears twice in the book
> OB views engagement **two ways**: as a **job attitude** (enthusiasm toward the work — [[ch03-job-attitudes|Ch 3]]) and as a **motivational state** (this chapter). It predicts task performance and citizenship behaviour, and disengagement costs US businesses up to **\$550 billion annually** — but the construct is partially redundant with commitment.

Predictors: beyond proactive personality, conscientiousness and extroversion, the strongest trait predictor is **the tendency to experience positive moods and emotions**. Context predictors: job characteristics, sufficient resources, person–organization value fit, inspirational leadership.

## Contemporary theories: context-based

### Reinforcement theory

**Behaviour is a function of its consequences.** Reinforcement theory is **behaviorist**: it treats behaviour as caused by the environment and deliberately ignores internal cognitive events. What controls behaviour are **reinforcers** — consequences that, immediately following a response, increase the probability the behaviour repeats.

**Operant conditioning** — people learn to behave a certain way to get something they want or avoid something they do not. Skinner's **behaviorism** holds that behaviour follows stimuli relatively unthinkingly, and his radical version rejects feelings and thoughts as causes altogether. Skinner's three demonstrated points: people engage in desired behaviours when **positively reinforced**; rewards are most effective when they **immediately follow** the response; unrewarded or punished behaviour is **less likely to repeat**.

The reverse case matters as much: promised compensation for three weeks of overtime that never materialises at appraisal time teaches you to decline the next request.

> [!warning] Reinforcement contradicts CET
> Reinforcement theory predicts rewards essentially always reinforce behaviour. **Cognitive evaluation theory predicts rewards can undermine behaviour** by damping intrinsic motivation. Both have support; the resolution is contextual, which is why the chapter says strict behaviorism is not recommended — **people have rich psychological worlds** — while granting that there is "a great deal of truth to be found in behaviorist approaches."

### Social learning theory

We learn through **both observation and direct experience** — by watching models: coworkers, supervisors, customers. Like reinforcement theory it assumes behaviour follows consequences, but it adds that **people respond to the way they perceive and define consequences, not the objective consequences**.

The modelling sequence: attend to what others do → commit it to memory → reproduce it from memory → repeat it if the model is reinforced.

The organizational finding that matters: employees are motivated to act **pragmatically, autonomously and even unethically if they observe their supervisors doing so**. And contrary to intuition, team members do **not** pick up the slack when a leader defaults — they are **less** likely to fill a leadership gap when the supervisor is slacking off.

## Contemporary theories: process-based

### Expectancy theory (Vroom)

The strength of our tendency to act depends on the **strength of our expectation of a given outcome and its attractiveness**. Three links:

$$\text{Effort} \xrightarrow{\text{Expectancy}} \text{Performance} \xrightarrow{\text{Instrumentality}} \text{Rewards} \xrightarrow{\text{Valence}} \text{Personal goals}$$

| Link | Question |
|---|---|
| **Expectancy** (effort → performance) | Will my effort actually produce this performance? |
| **Instrumentality** (performance → reward) | Will performing at this level actually get me the reward? |
| **Valence** (reward → personal goals) | Do I actually want the reward? |

**The chain breaks at its weakest link** — which is why so many employees do only the minimum. In the chapter's parking-spot contest: some want the spot and some do not (**valence**); soliciting donations may or may not produce donations (**expectancy**); and if there is only one spot that the same colleague wins every year, **instrumentality** is near zero.

### Goal-setting theory (Locke & Latham)

Intentions to work toward a goal are a major source of work motivation. Goals do four things: **direct attention · mobilize effort · encourage persistence · facilitate strategy development**.

**Core finding:** specific goals increase performance, and **difficult goals, when accepted, produce higher performance than easy ones**. "Do your best" fails because it is unverifiable — "apply to ten to fifteen jobs per week" defines when and how much.

**Where goals come from.** Evidence on participation is mixed — self-set goals do not reliably produce better performance. But they have **psychological** benefits: self-set goals generate greater enthusiasm, while supervisor-set goals raise anxiety, uncertainty and threat, with downstream effects on emotional exhaustion and OCB. If goals are assigned, the supervisor must make their **purpose and importance** clear. We also **subconsciously "catch" goals** from other people.

**Five factors that help or hurt:**

| Factor | What matters |
|---|---|
| **Goal commitment** | The person must believe they *can* achieve it and *want* to. Most likely when effort is expected to pay off, attainment is attractive, and they participated in setting it. Undermined by **mimicry** (friends underachieving) and shaped by the **"goal audience"** — unease about being evaluated against peers. Paradoxically, **abandonment after an initial failure is more likely for people with strong core values**, who internalize the failure more heavily |
| **Task characteristics** | Goals work better on **simple** than complex tasks, and on **independent** rather than interdependent ones. On interdependent tasks, **group-centred goals** emphasizing your contribution to the group beat individual goals — and where people depend on each other but do not know each other, **goals to cooperate** motivate effectively |
| **Feedback** | Generally raises performance by identifying the gap between what you did and what to do next. **Self-generated feedback beats externally generated feedback.** But usefulness depends on the giver, the receiver, the task and the quality — and feedback lands badly when the person feels unable to change or lacks the resources to act on it |
| **Goal orientation** | **Mastery** (master the task) → positive outcomes; **performance-avoid** (avoid looking bad) → undesirable outcomes including hiding and manipulating information from teammates; **performance-prove** (prove yourself) → weak or mixed. To build mastery orientation: step back and **focus on what you are excited to learn** from the situation |
| **Goal conflict** | Hundreds of studies link goal conflict to **stress and anxiety**, and it can spill over onto unrelated tasks. Managing it: **splice** (recognise and accept the conflict) → **activate** (focus on actions serving both) → **repress** (do not let impossible actions block progress on either). Where the conflict is between groups, inspire collaboration and co-construct solutions |

**Management by objectives (MBO)** — a systematic goal-setting programme with four ingredients: **goal specificity · participation in decision making · an explicit time period · performance feedback**. Organizational objectives cascade into divisional, departmental and individual objectives — but because lower-unit managers help set their own, MBO works **bottom-up as well as top-down**. When MBO fails, the causes are unrealistic expectations, lack of top-management commitment, and unwillingness to allocate rewards based on goal accomplishment.

> [!warning] Goal setting has a dark side
> **Overdoing it costs:** goals can impede **learning** (too focused on achievement), the wrong goal form can impede performance, and goals feed **escalation of commitment** ([[ch06-perception-and-decision-making|Ch 6]]).
>
> **Goals and ethics.** When **money is tied to goal attainment**, people focus on the money, become willing to compromise ethically, and morally disengage. **Time pressure** near a deadline tempts unethical shortcuts. **Depletion** does too — an exhausted, overloaded kitchen staff takes food-safety shortcuts. And **performance-prove or performance-avoid** orientations raise unethical behaviour relative to mastery.

### Self-efficacy theory

**Self-efficacy** — an individual's belief of being capable of performing a task. In difficulty, low-efficacy people reduce effort or give up; high-efficacy people try harder.

**Four ways to raise it:**

1. **Enactive mastery** — relevant direct experience with the task. This is why training works, particularly when interactive with feedback afterward
2. **Vicarious modelling** — watching someone else do it (the social-learning route)
3. **Verbal persuasion** — being told you have what it takes
4. **Arousal** — getting "psyched up." Counterproductive for low-key tasks like writing a sales report

**Goal setting and self-efficacy compound**: a supportive manager setting difficult specific goals raises the employee's self-efficacy, which leads them to set **higher self-set goals**, producing higher performance.

> [!note] The limits
> Self-efficacy can create a positive spiral — but it can also make people **cocky**, performing worse and losing motivation. Entrepreneurs who experienced **more variability** in self-efficacy were more successful at starting businesses, apparently because they showed humility and adapted to setbacks instead of getting cocky. The goal is efficacy **when and where it counts**.

**The Pygmalion effect** — a self-fulfilling prophecy where what one person expects of another comes true. Identify people as high-potential leaders, treat them accordingly, and they become leaders. Its effects are replicable and stronger where leader–subordinate relationships are strong. The caution from Dov Frohman: *"Leaders are found in the strangest places. Often the best candidates turn out to be people from outside the mainstream."*

## Organizational justice

### Equity theory

Employees compare their **outcomes** (pay, promotion, recognition, office) to their **inputs** (effort, experience, education), forming an O/I ratio, and compare that ratio to a **referent** — usually a similar coworker.

- $O/I_A = O/I_B$ → **equity**, perceived as fair
- $O/I_A < O/I_B$ → inequity, **underrewarded**
- $O/I_A > O/I_B$ → inequity, **overrewarded**

This explains the chapter's puzzle: the intern earning \$20/hour is pleased while the manager earning \$30/hour is dissatisfied — because the manager's referent is managers at other pharmaceutical companies.

**Six responses to perceived inequity:** change inputs · change outcomes · distort perceptions of self · distort perceptions of others · choose a different referent · **leave the field** (quit).

Equity theory has partial support — people differ in **equity sensitivity** (some more entitled, some more benevolent), and its overreward predictions have not held up (few people return salary). Its lasting contribution is as the precursor to **organizational justice**.

### The four justice types

**Organizational justice** — the overall perception of what is fair in the workplace.

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Distributive** | Perceived fairness of the **amount and allocation** of rewards | "I got the pay raise I deserved" |
| **Procedural** | Perceived fairness of the **process** used to determine the outcome | "I had input into how raises were decided and was told why I got mine" |
| **Informational** *(interactional)* | Whether employees get **truthful explanations** for decisions and are kept informed | Being told candidly why the decision went the way it did |
| **Interpersonal** *(interactional)* | Whether employees are treated with **dignity and respect** | "When telling me about my raise, my supervisor was very nice" |

**Distributive justice** is not as cold as the ratio suggests. Judgments rest on **feeling and emotional reaction** and are "hot" rather than calculated — as in the 2018 US government shutdown worker: *"most of us live paycheck to paycheck and cannot afford to be unpaid and still go to work for long. It is not fair."* People care most about **pay** differences, though recognition and challenging assignments matter too.

**Procedural justice** rests on six rules: give people a **say**; decide **consistently** across people and time; **avoid bias**; use **accurate information**; **consider** the affected groups; remain **open to appeals**. Its key payoff: **if the process is judged fair, employees accept unfavourable outcomes** — and giving voice makes people feel better even when outcomes stay poor.

> [!warning] The AI-fairness paradox
> Organizations deploy AI-assisted decisions to make procedures fairer and less biased. But research across thousands of participants finds employees may see these tools as **reductionist and blind to their specific situations** — so a tool that objectively reduces bias can be **perceived as more biased**.

**Informational justice** works better as an **excuse after the fact** ("I wanted to give you the office, but it wasn't my decision") than as a **justification** ("I gave it to Sam, but having it isn't a big deal"). Managers are often hesitant to share, especially bad news — which is exactly when it matters.

**Interpersonal justice** is unique in occurring in **everyday interactions**, giving managers constant opportunities to build or squander it. A sting in the research: leaders from underrepresented groups who *do* adhere to interpersonal justice rules can **still be treated with bias in return**, as stereotypes about their motives get activated.

### Justice outcomes

All four types are linked to **higher task performance and citizenship behaviour** and **lower counterproductive behaviour** — with a division of labour:

- **Distributive and procedural** justice → more strongly associated with **task performance**
- **Informational and interpersonal** justice → more strongly associated with **citizenship behaviour**

Even **sleep quality and health** are linked to fair treatment. The mechanisms: fairness enhances commitment, signals that the organization cares, builds **trust** in supervisors (reducing uncertainty and fear of exploitation), and elicits **positive emotions** that prompt citizenship.

> [!tip] Third-party justice
> Observers' reactions matter as much as victims'. Read about a restaurant chain conducting mass unannounced layoffs with no warning or assistance — research says you may **stop going**. Injustice is visible to customers.

**Consistency is its own variable:** when an organization is inconsistent and employees ride "a roller coaster of abuse and unfair treatment," they become detached, cooperate less and hold lower job attitudes.

### Culture and justice

The **same basic principles of procedural justice** are respected across nations — workers worldwide prefer rewards based on performance and skill over seniority. But inputs and outcomes are **valued differently**.

A study of **190,000+ employees across 32 countries** found justice perceptions most important in cultures that are **individualistic, feminine, uncertainty-avoiding and low power distance**. Employees in high-power-distance Confucian societies are **less affected** by abusive supervision. And materialistic cultures treat cash compensation as the relevant outcome, while relational cultures weight **social rewards and status**.

## Integrating the theories

The surviving theories are **complementary, not competing**. The integrating model is built on expectancy theory:

- **Opportunity** and **ability** either aid or hinder individual effort; **goals direct behaviour** (goal-setting theory feeds the effort box).
- For effort to produce performance, the person must have the **ability** and must see the **performance appraisal system as fair and objective**.
- The **performance–reward** link is strong only if performance — rather than seniority or favouritism — is what actually gets rewarded.
- The **reward–goal** link is strong when rewards satisfy the person's **dominant needs**.
- **High nAch people bypass the chain** — they jump from effort straight to personal goals, being internally driven when the job provides personal responsibility, feedback and moderate risk. They are not concerned with the three expectancy linkages.
- **Reinforcement** enters where organizational rewards reinforce performance; **equity comparison and organizational justice** enter where people judge outcome favourability relative to others *and* relative to how they were treated.

## Key takeaways

- Motivation is **intensity × direction × persistence** — intensity without direction wastes effort.
- The three classics are the **shared vocabulary**, not the evidence base: Maslow largely unsupported (and never a pyramid), Herzberg methodologically criticized but influential (**hygiene removes dissatisfaction; only motivators satisfy**), McClelland conceptually sound but hard to measure.
- **SDT**: autonomous beats controlled motivation; **extrinsic rewards can crowd out intrinsic interest** (CET); intrinsic motivation drives **quality**, incentives drive **quantity**.
- **Regulatory focus**: promotion → performance, citizenship, innovation; prevention → safety. Fit to the demand matters more than which.
- **Expectancy theory** is the master framework: motivation collapses at the **weakest** of expectancy, instrumentality and valence.
- **Goal setting**: specific and difficult beats vague and easy — moderated by commitment, task type, feedback, orientation and conflict. **Money tied to goals invites unethical behaviour.**
- **Self-efficacy** is built by mastery, modelling, persuasion and arousal, and compounds with goal setting.
- **Four justice types** all raise performance and OCB and lower CWB — distributive/procedural for task performance, informational/interpersonal for citizenship. **Fair process makes unfavourable outcomes acceptable.**
