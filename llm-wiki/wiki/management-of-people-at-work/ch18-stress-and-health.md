# Chapter 18 — Stress and Health in Organizations

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch17-human-resource-systems|Ch 17]] · **Final chapter**

---

## Core summary

Public policy experts, researchers, politicians, managers and employees are all coming to recognize that **the detrimental effects of mismanaged stress at work are a public health crisis**. The chapter builds a single model — **stressors → stress → strain** — then applies it to **physical health** (sleep, illness and injury), **mental health** (job insecurity, workaholism, burnout, depression), **four theories** of how stress emerges, **work–life balance**, and finally **individual and organizational approaches to managing stress**.

> [!note] Beating burnout — Massachusetts General Hospital's emergency department
> Even caring for patients with a deadly, highly contagious and poorly understood disease, MGH's leaders **minimized burnout and in some cases raised physician job satisfaction**. What they did:
> - **Emotional rather than financial rewards** — collecting and sharing appreciative patient feedback with employees who would never otherwise have heard it
> - **Meaningful interaction** — video conferencing so families unable to attend could still speak with staff; physicians who had meaningful interactions with patients and families reported **improved well-being**
> - **Transparent communication** — near-daily emails to the whole staff explaining how leadership handled difficult choices, plus **twice-daily "rounds"** with frontline doctors and nurses, because email alone was overwhelming
> - **Reasonable workloads** — **more physicians in the critical care pod**, so each had fewer patients. Financially counterintuitive, but interactions had become more time-consuming and emotionally draining, and staff could **mentally recharge**
> - **Community** — virtual "wellness sessions" for open discussion; **two-thirds of staff surveyed reported a greater sense of unity and purpose than before the pandemic**
>
> The transferable principle: **empowering individuals to do their jobs effectively in a safe and supportive environment**.

## The nature of stress

Three terms that everyday speech collapses into one:

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Stressor** | Conditions or events an individual **perceives as challenging or threatening** — the **antecedent** |
| **Stress** | A generally **unpleasant perception and appraisal** of stressors |
| **Strain** | The **psychological, physiological and behavioral consequences** of stress |

An upcoming project deadline is a **stressor**; the unpleasant appraisal of it is **stress**; the anxiety, lost sleep and procrastination that follow are **strain**.

Work is a top source of stress for young adults (18–23): money 78%, **work 64%**, housing costs 52%, job stability 51%, the economy 44%. And responses differ — an OB Poll found **57% invigorated by stress vs 43% paralyzed by it**.

### Challenge vs hindrance stressors

- **Challenge stressors** — workload, pressure to complete tasks, time urgency
- **Hindrance stressors** — things that keep you from reaching your goals: red tape, office politics, confusion over job responsibilities

**Both lead to strain**, but hindrance stressors lead to **more** of it. Challenge stressors produce more **motivation, engagement and performance**; hindrance stressors negatively affect **safety compliance and participation, engagement, job satisfaction, commitment, performance and withdrawal**.

> [!important] The conditions matter more than the category
> - Social situations were traditionally treated as hindrance stressors (interpersonal conflict), but some people — sales professionals, development officers — **view winning others over as an invigorating challenge**, navigated well by the politically skilled.
> - Challenge stressors improve engagement **when employees are in a good mood**.
> - **Conscientious** employees view *both* types negatively — they are more perceptive and aware that these are stressors — producing strain and increased turnover.
> - Those who **hold power** see stressors as challenging rather than hindering — **until they realize others depend on them**, at which point the challenges become hindrances.
> - **Inconsistent** challenge stressors (coming and going rather than constant and predictable) actually cause strain and performance losses.
> - **Unnecessary tasks act as hindrance stressors that thwart the positive aspects of challenge stressors** — too many hindrances cancel out the good.
> - **Challenge stressors improve performance only in a supportive work environment; hindrance stress reduces performance in all environments.**

### Three sources of stressors

| Source | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Environmental** | **Economic uncertainty** — recessions make people anxious about job security. **Political uncertainty** — in the 2020 US turmoil, a majority named "the future of the nation," "the current political climate" and "political unrest" as significant stressors |
| **Organizational** | **Task demands** — job design (autonomy, task variety, automation), working conditions, physical layout. **Role demands** — **role conflict** (expectations hard to reconcile), **role overload** (expected to take on too much), **role ambiguity** (unclear expectations). **Interpersonal demands** — fights, bullying, incivility, abusive supervision, racial/sexual harassment, all **very strongly related** to workplace stress |
| **Personal** | Sleep quality and quantity; **family issues** (even good ones) closely tied to work–life conflict; **personal economic problems** from overextended finances, which siphon attention away from work |

> [!warning] Stressors are additive
> **Stress builds up.** Incivility from a customer or coworker can cost you a night's sleep, producing strain **beyond** the incivility itself. A single stressor may be unimportant on its own, but **added to an already high level it can be too much**. To appraise someone's total stress you must add up **all** the sources and severities — which cannot be easily quantified or observed. So managers should stay aware of potential stress loads: **many employees will tell a caring manager what their perceived stress load is.**

### Three kinds of strain

- **Physiological** — changes in metabolism, increased heart and breathing rates and blood pressure, headaches, heart attacks; also backaches, eye strain, sleep disturbances, dizziness, fatigue, loss of appetite, gastrointestinal problems and **cardiovascular disease**. Stress also **impairs episodic memory** (though it enhances memory *after* the stressful event).
- **Psychological** — job dissatisfaction, tension, anxiety, irritability, boredom. Jobs low in **variety, significance, autonomy, feedback and identity** create stress and reduce satisfaction and involvement.
- **Behavioral** — reduced productivity; more absences, safety incidents and turnover; changed eating habits; increased smoking or alcohol consumption; rapid speech; fidgeting; sleep disorders.

### Eustress and the inverted U

**Eustress** (Greek *eu*, "good") — a **healthy, positive and constructive appraisal** of stressors. Physiologically, the nervous system, hypothalamus, pituitary and adrenal glands supply stress hormones; heartbeat and breathing accelerate to increase oxygen while muscles tense for action. Athletes and stage performers **rise to the occasion**; many professionals treat heavy workloads and deadlines as positive challenges that improve their work and satisfaction.

The classic **inverted-U model** says low-to-moderate stress stimulates the body and improves performance, while too much stress places impossible demands and lowers it.

> [!warning] The inverted U is intuitive but weakly supported
> Despite its popularity, the model **has not earned much empirical support**. It may miss the links between stressors, felt stress and performance — sometimes we are stressed but feel fine for other reasons (individual differences, mood, context). Treat it as a **neutral starting point**, and expect the curve to be **shifted left or right** for particular employees in particular environments.

## Physical health at work

Health matters for organizational functioning: **one in four essential workers has been diagnosed with a mental health disorder** since the onset of the pandemic, and roughly **one in three received treatment** from a mental health professional. Physical health also influences interpersonal relationships, productivity and withdrawal — **immune system health, inflammation and the gut biome** all shape emotion, motivation, thinking and behaviour at work.

### Sleep

Sleep **quality** has a moderately negative impact on job attitudes, psychological health and mood at work, and **sleep deprivation** substantially affects memory, cognitive task performance, task performance, attendance, safety behaviour and **CWBs**. The mechanism: poor sleep harms performance by **depleting cognitive resources**, and raises CWB through its **negative effect on mood and self-control**.

Work stressors that impair sleep: **unfinished work tasks, social stressors, poor leadership, perceived discrimination, role conflict** — and they compound, producing a **steady decrease in sleep quality across the workweek** that leaves you drained by Friday. Remedies beyond more good sleep: **physical activity**, **recovery experiences with social support groups**, possibly **blue light-blocking glasses**.

> [!note] Myth or Science? "When you are working hard, sleep is optional" — false
> Sleeplessness costs US employers **\$63.2 billion per year, almost \$2,300 per employee**, and has been cited as a contributing factor in heart disease, obesity, stroke and cancer. US military researchers report sleep deprivation is **one of the top causes of friendly fire**, and **20% of auto accidents** involve drowsy drivers. More than 158 people died on Air India Flight 812 when the pilot awoke from a nap and, suffering **sleep inertia**, overshot the runway.
>
> **One-third of US employees** in most industries get fewer than six hours (seven to nine are recommended). Over **50% of adults 19–29**, 43% of 30–45 and 38% of 46–64 rarely or never get a good night's sleep on weekdays. Lack of sleep **impairs learning and problem-solving** — which is why law enforcement organizations, Super Bowl-winning teams and **half the Fortune 500** employ fatigue management specialists.
>
> The workarounds backfire: prescription sleep aids **increase sleep time by only eleven minutes** and cause short-term memory loss; sleep labs may not help after the sessions end; caffeine (**74% of US adults daily**) shows diminishing returns, requiring ever-larger doses that leave users jittery then exhausted. And we are **not good at assessing our own impairment** when sleep deprived. **There is no substitute for a solid night's sleep.**

### Illness and injury — presenteeism

**Presenteeism** — the act of **working while ill or injured**. Nine out of ten employees in one large survey admitted going to work sick before the pandemic, with hesitance attributed to **losing sick leave and inflexible or coercive supervisors**.

Why it is costly: ① working close to others while contagious **increases the risk that teammates fall ill** (and raises their anxiety about working with you) ② people are **less effective when ill or injured** and slow work processes — an effect **multiplied** if the illness spreads.

Who does it? People **compelled by work forces**, those with a **chronic physical condition** (e.g. asthma), and — notably — people who **love their job and want to work**. **Organizational culture** matters a lot (see [[ch16-organizational-culture-and-change|Ch 16]]): cultures encourage presenteeism, discourage it, or emphasize individual choice. Some argue it is a **matter of degree** — people with chronic or minor conditions can be effective if given **flexibility and resources**.

### Work as a risk factor

We spend nearly **one-third of our waking lives at work**, and one review of nearly 300 studies suggests **what we do in the workplace is just as much of a risk factor for illness as secondhand smoke**. The chapter's stark examples: a **21-year-old Merrill Lynch intern collapsed and died after working 72 hours straight**; a 56-year-old employee died of a heart attack shortly after his steel mill closed.

The link is usually **indirect** — people **behave unhealthily to cope**: skipping lunch, eating convenient unhealthy food, smoking, neglecting sleep, abusing substances. Across nearly 5,000 workers, **regular hindrance stressors** produced moderate increases in **high-risk eating and cigarette smoking**, which raised objective indicators — **BMI, cholesterol, insulin resistance** — the metabolic risk factors behind obesity, higher company health costs, absenteeism, injury, and even **prejudice and discrimination**. Stress also leads to injury as people **neglect safety behaviour**: worry about COVID health and financial threats led many workers to **relax their safety behaviours**.

What helps: **planning**. Employees who plan for healthy eating and exercise follow through more than those acting on convenience or impulse — and **resilience programmes** reduce stress-related physical health risks.

## Mental health at work

Treating employees with **both** physical and mental health conditions costs **two to three times more** than treating those without co-occurring illness. **51.5 million US adults** reported a mental health disorder in 2019, and conditions like depression **reduce cognitive performance 35% of the time at work**.

### Job insecurity

**Job insecurity** — the perception that one's job is at risk or that employment is not stable. It is a **highly emotional perception** affecting sleep, depressive symptoms and burnout. It is signalled less by increased job demands than by a **lack of social and structural resources** — fair interpersonal treatment and communication, LMX, support and trust from coworkers — most often where unemployment is high.

As a stressor it is **relatively unique**: it prompts employees to preserve their jobs, **adjust effort to signal their viability**, build stronger relations with coworkers and supervisors to reduce the insecurity — **or reconsider their employment entirely**.

> [!note] Toward a Better World — the Freelancers Union and gig workers
> Job insecurity is an **especially salient stressor for gig workers**, and it damages their job attitudes. Despite far greater autonomy than traditional employees, **gig workers experience more strain and work–family conflict**, and face workload fluctuations they must meet to be hired again and protect their reputation.
>
> During the pandemic, rideshare drivers were classified **essential** and kept working while demand plummeted — one Cincinnati Uber driver recalled a day of dollar tips: *"my life is worth more than that."* The **CARES Act** temporarily extended unemployment eligibility to gig workers; many lost it when states cut Pandemic Unemployment Assistance early. Normal-time hindrance stressors for gig work: **unclear employment status, non-reimbursed expenses, declining reimbursement rates, inability to make a living wage, competition with other gig workers, employer double standards, dishonest clients, constant "hustling," and no clear career advancement.**
>
> Louis Hyman's argument in *Temp*: gig work carries **autonomy and independence** — normally highly coveted job characteristics — so the way forward is to **reconnect gig workers with the job resources and support** of traditional employment. *"Americans need life security… not job security."* The **National Labor Relations Act does not apply to gig workers**, though 80% of Los Angeles rideshare drivers said they would join a union. The **Freelancers Union** (technically not a union — no collective bargaining power) unites **500,000 members** of the 56.7 million US gig workers, offering free membership, insurance options (health, dental, term life, disability, vision, liability), expert and peer social support, legal and financial tools, and legislative advocacy.

### Workaholism

**Workaholism** — a maladaptive mental state characterized by ① **feeling compelled to work due to internal pressures** ② **thinking about work even when not working** ③ **going above and beyond what is reasonably expected, to one's own detriment**. It can be a response to job insecurity, or simply come from loving the job and feeling a calling.

**Leaders are especially prone** — sacrificing personal life and recovery time to "keep the ship running." **Self-employed entrepreneurs** cope with job insecurity by overworking, and **earn more because of it, despite the damage to their health**.

> [!warning] Working hard ≠ working compulsively
> Workaholics can look like ideal employees — hence the reflexive interview answer *"I just work too hard."* But **workaholic behaviour does not necessarily produce more productivity**, and the strain eventually wears the person down into **work–life conflict, burnout and higher blood pressure**. Among nearly 2,000 dentists, workaholic behaviour raised **challenging workplace stressors** along with an eventual increase in **structural resources** — possibly improving job security. **The burnout cost suggests it is not worth the trouble.**

### Burnout and depression

**Burnout** — a work-related mental health syndrome with three components:

| Component | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Emotional exhaustion** | Feeling overextended and emotionally depleted |
| **Depersonalization** | Maladaptively coping with the job through **callousness and distancing** |
| **Reduced personal accomplishment** | Losing a sense of productivity or contribution |

Some consider burnout effectively the **opposite of job engagement**. It has a moderate effect on outcomes — more absenteeism and turnover, less job performance — and **its effects compound in a vicious cycle**: stressors cause burnout, burnout reduces performance, reduced performance draws supervisor disciplinary action, which is **a new stressor**.

**Who burns out:** those **low on emotional stability** are more prone to emotional exhaustion, while **high extroversion** predicts less exhaustion and a higher sense of personal accomplishment. **Women are more likely to experience emotional exhaustion; men are more likely to experience depersonalization.** Cultures with **tight, masculine norms** are more burnout-prone than looser, egalitarian societies.

**Depression** — a mental health disorder characterized by **sadness, loss of interest, despondency and dejection**, often co-occurring with anxiety and anger. Some researchers argue burnout closely resembles it. It is the **most common mental health condition**, affecting about **9.5% of the US population annually**.

> [!important] The job-insecurity and bullying loops
> Depressive states are **heavily predicted by constant job insecurity** — and depressive symptoms **reinforce perceptions of job insecurity**, because suffering employees are more acutely aware of it. Likewise, **bullying and social isolation** cause depression over time, and those bullied *while* depressed can experience a sting painful enough that **some contemplate suicide**.
>
> The good news: **immediate cessation of bullying reduces depressive symptoms very quickly** in some cases, and interventions that **reduce mental health stigma and convey appreciation** for employees effectively reduce depression.

## Four theories of stress and health

### Conservation of resources (COR)

Employees **strive to obtain, foster, retain and protect the resources and things they value**. **Resources** are factors within an individual's control that can be expended toward fulfilling desires, attaining goals or meeting task demands — and gaining or replenishing them costs time and energy.

Two asymmetries: employees are **more aware of resource losses than gains** (a missed opportunity worries you less than losing something cherished), and once resources are lost people become **motivated to replace them** — when overextended or exhausted, they become **desperate, defensive, aggressive and irrational** in that pursuit.

Supporting evidence: diminished **job control and social support** predict burnout · under stressors employees engage in **less voice behaviour** (conserving resources) · **seeing coworkers treated unfairly**, or learning of a **partner's job insecurity**, causes resource loss and subsequent strain · even "good" stressors like an **enhanced organizational reputation** raise performance demands that threaten resources and drive turnover · leaders conserve resources through **LMX differentiation** and by defaulting to old ways rather than trying resource-intensive new ones · and **transformational leadership**, though good for followers, is **taxing on leaders**, eventually producing resource loss, emotional exhaustion and turnover.

### Effort–reward imbalance (ERI)

Employees experience strain when they **put in a great deal of effort for little reward**, and are motivated to resolve the discrepancy by ① **putting in less effort** or ② **working to increase the reward**.

> [!warning] Why ERI has fallen out of favour
> ① **Which efforts and rewards?** We jump to salary, but **job security and a sense of competence related to health more strongly than salary did**. ② **Facets depend on the job** — for bus drivers, "effort" could mean road conditions *or* interacting with passengers, and a driver perceiving few rewards would **give up on customer service rather than on the road** (where lives are at stake). ③ **Where is the threshold?** It is hard to imagine *any* imbalance causing distress, and hard to pinpoint whether **effort or reward** is the bigger contributor.

### Job demand–control–support (JDCS)

Strain is a function of three factors:

- **Demands** — the responsibilities, pressures, obligations and uncertainties individuals face at work
- **Control** — the degree of discretion employees have over how to do their jobs (like autonomy)
- **Support** — perceptions of assistance from the organization, supervisor and coworkers

The main tenet: **any negative effects of demands on strain can be offset or buffered by control and support.**

Evidence on the original model is **not very supportive in general**, though the **buffering tenet has been supported**, and demands and control have separately been linked to increased and decreased **mortality odds**. The fixes involve **disentangling**: one meta-analysis supported JDCS only when sources of support were separated (**supervisor support mattered more than coworker support**); another found support depended on **gender, nationality and occupation**; and separating demands into challenge vs hindrance found **JDCS supported only for hindrance stressors**, because challenges are not viewed negatively by everyone.

### Job demands–resources (JDR)

Combines COR and JDCS: **demands and resources both contribute to performance through their distinctive effects on strain (e.g. burnout) and engagement.** The performance review example: a good review may bring promotion and higher salary, a poor one may block promotion or end in firing — and to the extent you can **draw on resources** (preparing, putting it in perspective, obtaining social support), you feel less stress and experience less strain.

The JDR is **one of the most popular stress and health theories to date**, and research supports it — both demands and resources lead to strain as expected. Caveats: the **demands→engagement link is weaker** than the others, since challenge demands can *raise* engagement; the influence of resources depends on **organizational climate, supportive leadership and psychological safety** (*will others look down on you if you draw on resources?*); it holds in the **safety context** (strain → safety incidents; engagement → compliant safety behaviour) and among students and teachers. It also explains why **highly educated people are not more satisfied with their jobs** — greater resources (income, autonomy) come with **greater demands** (longer hours, higher pressure).

> [!important] Allostasis — there is no single ideal state
> Early work assumed a **homeostatic** equilibrium where demands match resources perfectly. It is now clearer that **no single ideal state exists**: **allostasis** means working to find stability **by changing our behaviours and attitudes**, and what matters is the **allostatic load** — the cumulative effect of stressors given the resources we draw on.
>
> Feeling confident and well supported **raises your willingness to experience strain** and your ability to mobilize coping resources. Where the allostatic load is **too great and too prolonged**, psychological or physiological symptoms appear.
>
> Preferences also **cycle** — sometimes you want to relax and recover, sometimes you welcome more stimulation — and demands change predictably over the life span: strain comes partly from **financial and economic demands for younger workers**, but from **caregiving demands (parenting, eldercare) for older workers**.

## Work–life balance

Not "balance" as a state of equilibrium — more a **balancing act**. Most employees still aspire to it, and it predicts work and family attitudes and performance.

### A new normal?

The line between work and home had been **progressively blurring** long before the pandemic: employees clung to smartphones (some researchers call them **"adult pacifiers"**), striving to be accessible 24/7, with strong norms compelling them to sacrifice personal life and always be **"hustling."** Then a sizable share of the workforce became remote **seemingly overnight**. Views diverge sharply — one CEO: *"The global pandemic has changed the way we work, blurring what was already a hazy divide between life and work…"*; Goldman Sachs' David Solomon: *"It is not a new normal. It is an aberration that we are going to correct as quickly as possible."*

The conceptual shift: from work–life balance as **a unicorn or achievement to aspire toward** (enjoyed by a few corner-office executives) to **a continuously balancing process** in which we stay self-aware, prioritize, and shuffle demands and commitments in a way that works for us.

### Boundaries

**Work–life boundaries** (or borders) — the lines demarcating our lives. We transition across them **physically and/or psychologically**, and people, things and communications can **cross them as interruptions** (a coworker calling you on vacation; stepping on your child's LEGO mid-conference-call).

It is complicated because people differ in **how integrated they want work and home to be** and how willing they are to move across domains — and both people and their job descriptions are often **limited in negotiating** those boundaries. **Integrators** move between domains easily (friends visiting at work, answering email while making dinner, talking about home at work and vice versa); **segmenters** maintain strong boundaries and **psychologically detach** (turning the phone off once off the clock).

**Boundary management tactics** come in three forms:

| Form | Tactic | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Establishing time boundaries | *"I always leave work at 5 p.m. to be home in time for dinner."* |
| | Marking work spaces | *"I will always work in the second bedroom, and if the door is shut, do not come in."* |
| | Facilitating boundary transitions | *"I bought a house a five-minute bike ride from where I work."* |
| **Psychological** | Prioritizing and goal-setting | *"When the clients are in town this week, I will spend more time building relationships with them."* |
| | Making compromises | *"I will make my working late all week up to my family by going to the movies with them on Friday."* |
| **Behavioral** | Setting expectations | *"Please respect my time by not calling me on vacation unless it is an emergency."* |
| | Negotiating resources | *"I will ask my coworkers if they want to come to a family event this weekend, where we can discuss the new project."* |

Who manages boundaries well? **Conscientious** employees perceive greater boundary control and feel better equipped to manage cross-role interruptions, while those **low in emotional stability** find the same interruptions intrusive and stressful. People who **strongly identify with their family** treat boundaries more strictly and less permeably — ensuring permeability for the more valued domain while **protecting it from intrusion**. And boundary management may be **a skill that improves with age**: older bank employees set and maintain stronger boundaries and achieve better work–life balance.

> [!warning] Boundary violations and over-integration
> Unwarranted interruptions in either domain produce **frustration and negative affect**. Among newly remote workers, those with **segmentation preferences who used clear physical tactics** (explicit workspaces, devices off outside work hours) achieved **better work–life balance**. And **work–life integration has been shown to impair mental health**, because individuals never get time to **"recharge"** from adverse experiences in either domain.

### Spillover, crossover, conflict and enrichment

**Work–life spillover** — when our **psychological responses** to one domain (positive or negative moods) are **carried over into another domain** and affect it. Even the **commute** is a domain that spills over — about **27.6 minutes one-way** in the US, with 5% of workers using public transport, and one bad traffic jam leaving you in a bad mood and late.

**Crossover** takes it to other people: angry customers at the movie theater in the afternoon make you snap at friends in the evening. It **reverberates through a social network** — an abusive supervisor's behaviour spills over into your home, crosses over to your partner, then spills over into *their* workplace. Spillover also **amplifies attitudes toward both domains**: a long call from your supervisor in the middle of a family reunion leaves you **dissatisfied with the family time (interrupted) and with your job (the supervisor)**.

**Work–life conflict** — negative spillover. It caught management's attention in the 1980s with the increased entry into the workforce of women with dependent children, prompting **onsite child care, summer day camps, flextime, job sharing, leave for school functions, telecommuting and part-time employment** — before organizations realized it **affected all employees**, as heavy workloads and travel demands made it harder for every parent to meet both sets of responsibilities.

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| **How harmful?** | Damages job attitudes and performance, and even objective outcomes — **manager performance ratings and upward mobility (promotions)**. Damages home life through poor life, leisure, family and marital strain |
| **What causes it?** | **Work factors drive work→life spillover; life factors drive life→work spillover.** Negative job attitudes, long hours, little support, inflexibility and job stress → life conflict. Family stress, interpersonal conflict, time caring for family, young children → work conflict. **Stress in either domain is the biggest factor** |
| **Who suffers?** | Those **low on emotional stability and high in negative affect**. Those who believe they have **control** over their circumstances and are high in **self-efficacy** handle it better. **Gender does not influence the amount or degree** — everyone experiences it — though some research finds men spend more time at work, less with family, and are **perhaps not as good at establishing boundaries** |
| **Culture?** | The impact of **work on life does not vary across cultures**; the impact of **life on work does** — life-to-work conflict is **stronger in collectivist societies and those with larger economic gender gaps**, though it has **less effect on work–family satisfaction** there. Work unit and family support reduce conflict **in cultures that perceive support as a useful resource** |

**Work–life enrichment** — the positive side, and much less studied. Jeff Bezos once noted that being happy at home brought tremendous energy into the office, and being happy at work brought tremendous energy home. Both directions (work→life and life→work) produce **positive job and life attitudes, increased engagement, decreased strain, better mental and physical health, and increased performance and OCB**. Its main predictor is **increased work engagement**, plus **social support from family and coworkers** and **job autonomy**. Different personality traits predict it than predict conflict: **extroversion and openness to experience**.

### Flexible and supportive policies

> [!important] Time pressure is not the real problem
> The underlying problem is the **psychological incursion** of work into family and vice versa — worrying about personal problems at work and work problems at home. So organizations should spend **less effort on time management** and more on helping employees **segment their lives, navigate boundary transitions, and facilitate enrichment**.

Practices that help: **keeping workloads reasonable, reducing work-related travel, onsite high-quality child care.** **Leaders play a large role** — encouraging use of supportive policies (and helping establish them), building high-quality LMX relationships, and enabling followers' pursuit of balance. Positive leadership behaviours such as **servant leadership** reduce stress and improve health on their own, and perceptions of **family-supportive leadership matter more** than general perceived organizational or supervisor support in reducing work–family conflict. These policies are powerful enough to **cross over and positively affect employees' partners' and family members' work and careers**.

Preferences differ: some prefer **segmenting** initiatives (flextime, job sharing, part-time hours), others prefer **integrating** ones (gym facilities, company family picnics) — but **on average most people prefer an organization that provides support for boundary management**.

## Managing stress and health

Should we do anything at all? Low-to-moderate challenging stressors can be functional, so **management may not be concerned** — but employees are likely to perceive even low levels as undesirable (or see the absence of challenge as boring). **What management considers "a positive stimulus that keeps the adrenaline running" the employee may well see as "excessive pressure."**

The stakes are concrete: across more than 4,000 caregivers in 35 hospitals, a typical **twelve-hour shift without breaks reduced hand-washing and safety compliance by 8.7%**. And **implementation matters** — translating evidence-based health and safety innovation into practice is often glacially slow, merely presenting information without telling people what to do with it, and **poor implementation is a waste of money**.

### Individual approaches

> [!tip] Recover, do not avoid
> The framing matters: you should **not be trying to actively prevent or avoid stress**, but to actively **recover, relax and refresh**. And these individual approaches do not put the onus entirely on employees — **most of them can be facilitated and enhanced by organizational policies**.

**Time management.** A well-organized employee may accomplish twice as much as a poorly organized one; among university professors, time management skills affect **work–family balance, job attitudes and performance**, and elite athletes are **experts who continuously revisit their commitments and adapt to new demands** even while strained and fatigued.

- **Best skills:** ① staying organized with calendars, project management software and to-do lists — **scheduling time for rest and recovery** ② **prioritizing** tasks and working on (or delegating) them in order of priority ③ setting **temporally realistic** long- and short-term goals.
- **Worst skills** — mythically associated with performing better: ① **working faster** ② **working longer**.
- Time management also **minimizes procrastination** by focusing effort on immediate goals, and **self-distancing** from future stressors helps you cope with time pressure.

**Mental wellness and physical fitness.** Physicians recommend **noncompetitive exercise** — aerobics, walking, jogging, swimming, cycling. It decreases detrimental physiological responses and speeds recovery, **buffers the negative spillover of work–life conflict** and improves work–life balance perceptions **even with poor sleep**, and **reduces the impact of others' unethical or unfair behaviour on you** while making you **less likely to be deviant yourself**.

Even **low-intensity activity** such as a lunchtime walk in a park improves mood — **even for people who doubt it will help**. But there is credence to "no pain, no gain": **high-intensity exercise produces better recovery experiences, less negative affect and less rumination**, and physical fitness may enhance intelligence and cognitive skills. A self-care routine might involve **music** (better workouts), an **activity tracker** (reduced strain, increased control perceptions), exercising **with coworkers or friends**, and **rewarding yourself** to keep coming back. Everyone is different — and employees and supervisors must **refrain from body-shaming or ostracism based on physical features**.

**Building resilience** — **resistance to the adverse effects of stress and strain**, especially important as preparation for crises. Three skill clusters:

1. **Emotional resilience** — fostering positive emotions, developing emotion regulation skills, facilitating social connection
2. **Resilient thinking** — flexible thinking, an optimistic perspective
3. **Balance and recovery abilities** — self-care behaviour, prioritizing and balancing demands

Day to day, resilience helps frontline employees **cope with customer incivility and mistreatment**, and **relationship resilience** helps people weather interpersonal conflict — coping with letdowns, forgiving breaches of trust, and continuing to advocate for their needs without becoming defeated. It develops through practice, coaching and training, though **its effects tend to be more temporary** than other individual approaches. Much of it resembles **stress management training** — either exposing employees to stressors until they become accustomed, or teaching **self-regulation, stress management and emotion regulation** skills — producing improved mental health and job satisfaction.

**Relaxation and mindfulness.** Relaxation techniques — meditation, mindfulness, deep breathing — aim at **deep physical relaxation**, focusing energy on releasing muscle tension. They relieve strain, produce a pronounced sense of peacefulness, and change heart rate, blood pressure and other physiological factors; reviews across thousands of employees find they **manage stress and reduce burnout**. Using them **during the day** (plus a lack of frustrating events) helps you relax once home. Even **very short microbreaks** relieve stress and restore energy.

> [!note] Complete detachment is not always best
> You might assume total detachment and rigid work–leisure boundaries are ideal. But **"recovering ponderers"** — people who do not fully detach and continue mulling problems they need to resolve at work, **while still engaging in relaxation activities** — are both **engaged and substantially less stressed**. There may be real benefit to treating boundaries **flexibly**.

**Mindfulness** — receptively **paying attention to and being aware of the present moment**, events and experiences. It has roots in Buddhist meditative practice — the literal translation of the Vedic word *sati*, "intentness of mind."

> [!warning] Promising evidence, overstated claims
> Claims that mindfulness **slows aging, bolsters test performance and facilitates neuroplasticity** are head-turning — and there is reason for skepticism: many studies **disagree on how they conceptualize mindfulness** and do not use the best methodological practices and measures.
>
> Setting the outlandish linkages aside, the empirical core is solid. Mindfulness **reduces unethical behaviour even without instruction to behave ethically**, because it leads people to adopt a **compassionate state**. Reviews and meta-analyses of **randomized controlled trials** — the medical gold standard — find it appreciably reduced **stress, anxiety, strain, psychological distress, chronic pain, depression relapse and addiction**, and improved **well-being and sleep quality**. It also links to **intrinsic motivation** (though it can impair immediate task motivation) and **emotional regulation**.
>
> Proposed mechanisms: **separating oneself from the moment, decreasing automatic thoughts, increasing awareness of one's own body**. Some people are inherently better at it as their **default mode**, and they show more positive motivational states and job attitudes, less strain, and better performance.

**Social support.** Expanding your network gives you someone to hear your problems and offer an objective perspective (**emotional support**) or to take on some of your workload (**instrumental support**). Both are highly related and about equally effective — but **who provides it makes the difference**: **supervisors who are readily available to help are the most valuable**.

> [!warning] Support can backfire
> If you do not expand your network and instead **ruminate with similarly stressed friends**, you can get caught in a vicious cycle. Be proactive and address stress head-on. To give support well, **build others up** so they understand their situation better and feel more capable of managing it — and remember that some people (stubborn, independent ones) are **less receptive to "visible" support** and may be better helped **invisibly**.

> [!important] An Ethical Choice — talking about mental health without overstepping
> **86%** of surveyed individuals believe an organization's culture should support mental health, yet roughly **60% of employees have never spoken to anyone at work about their mental health status** — a cycle where silence breeds stigma. Three practical steps:
> - **Avoid trying to fix people.** Leaders succeed by solving hard problems, but employees who feel you are trying to "fix" them may **lose confidence and question their competence**. Offer resources, or simply ask: *"What would be most helpful for you right now?"*
> - **Discuss health holistically.** Headaches and seasonal allergies are normal conversation; mental health is not. Asking about it **normalizes** it, and **sharing your own struggles** builds psychological safety.
> - **Listen more than you talk.** Active listening is marred by biases, distractions and judgments. Focus on your colleague's experience and separate it from your own; **resist the urge to talk prescriptively**.
>
> **Check-the-box solutions like EAPs are common but not enough.** What employees want is a **more accepting and open culture** with clearer information about the resources available when they need extra support.

### Organizational approaches

**(Re)designing jobs.** Giving employees **more responsibility, more meaningful work, more autonomy and increased feedback** reduces strain — it gives them greater control over work activities, lessens dependence on others, and **demonstrates that the organization cares about their well-being**. Attend to the specific tasks that serve as stressors: across thousands of employees in multiple occupations, jobs with **irregular schedules and conflictual contact** were most prone to bullying, so managers can **design regularity into schedules** or alleviate the effects of that contact. Boring or repetitive jobs are also straining and harmful over time — **variety, or even making jobs more "playful,"** helps.

Routes to redesign: **job crafting** (giving employees control over the nature of their jobs), **idiosyncratic deals** with individual employees, and **opportunities for training, development and growth** (skill-enhancing job design influences job satisfaction, mental well-being and skill adoption). Control and autonomy may also be a **necessary condition for mindfulness training to work** on stress and health.

> [!warning] The wrong redesign backfires
> **Not all employees want enriched jobs.** For employees with a **low need for growth**, the right redesign might be **less responsibility and more specialization**; for those who prefer structure and routine, **reducing skill variety** reduces uncertainty and stress. And giving employees more responsibility for outcomes may cause **proactive employees to burn out** while trying to meet the "challenge." Managers should also **watch that employees are not accepting too many challenges**.

**Enabling a remote work option.** Dozens of companies went permanently or partially virtual, most employees would prefer to work from home at least part of the time, and machine-learning analysis of **1.56 million tweets** before and after the pandemic's onset found public opinion of remote work **increased substantially**. But telecommuting has its **own demands and stressors** — **videoconference ("Zoom") fatigue**, and the heightened work–life integration and reduced segmentation that produce more stress and burnout for some. On balance remote work has a **more positive effect on work outcomes than many assume**; the open questions are whether struggling employees can be **taught to do it effectively** and whether jobs can be redesigned and resources provided to reduce home and life stressors. Above all, organizations should be careful about structuring work so employees are **on call twenty-four hours a day**: **workplace telepressure impedes recovery experiences and increases stress**.

**Offering recovery experiences.** The average American uses only about **54% of available time off**, with just **28% using their full vacation days** — and many skip even "staycations." Why? Beyond those who cannot afford one, employees ① feel they **cannot successfully detach** (still "on call") ② **do not expect positive outcomes** (they will struggle to relax or connect) ③ **expect negative outcomes** (strain, financial impact).

Are the fears warranted? Among hundreds of teachers on mid-term breaks, **perfectionists struggled to relax and stayed emotionally exhausted** — mostly through negative spillover and **taking work with them** — but realized the **full benefit when they refrained from taking work along** (leaving the papers ungraded). In general **vacations reduce strain and raise well-being**, though the effects **fade on return**, and a very high "back to work" workload can be a **jarring shock** that restores pre-vacation stress levels.

**Sabbaticals** — extended voluntary leaves from a few weeks to several months, offered by Charles Schwab, Adobe, Intel, Epic, PayPal, Deloitte, Autodesk, Biogen and Genentech. Among university faculty they **increase job resources and well-being, especially with greater autonomy over how the sabbatical is spent**; employees at a small manufacturing company described sabbaticals as a way to **"find themselves" and "discover who they are,"** contributing to a positive self-identity.

**Wellness programs** — organizationally supported programmes (alongside **EAPs** and work–life programmes) focusing on employees' **total physical and mental health**: workshops to quit smoking, control alcohol use, lose weight, make healthier food and drink choices, and build a regular exercise programme, plus psychological health support. In COR terms, participation **replenishes employee resources**, starting a continuous upward trajectory.

> [!important] Do wellness programs work? Only if people use them
> A meta-analysis of **36 stress-reduction programmes** found that interventions helping employees **reframe stressful situations and use active coping strategies appreciably reduced stress**, and such programmes also affect **job satisfaction, absenteeism and presenteeism**.
>
> But they work **only if people use them** — employees must **see the value**, know their **supervisor supports participation**, and believe the organization supports continued wellness and is **a safe place to seek help without stigma**. When they do, they leave with **better performance, higher job satisfaction and lower turnover intentions**.
>
> The aggregate numbers: Johnson & Johnson reported **\$250 million saved on health care costs in ten years**; across organizations, wellness programmes show a **\$5.93 : \$1.00 savings-to-cost ratio**, **26% lower health costs**, **30% fewer workers' compensation claims** and a **28% reduction in sick days**. Benefits remain contingent on **how much workers put in, how much they participate, and their attitudes on entering**.

> [!note] Point / Counterpoint — should companies encourage stress reduction?
> **Point.** Stress reduction cuts **health-related costs** (stress weakens the immune system, drives presenteeism, and contributes to expensive conditions like heart disease), **facilitates job performance** (overburdened employees cannot concentrate, lose energy and creativity, and treat coworkers and customers badly, ultimately leaving and incurring turnover costs), and carries an **ethical component** — the workplace generates the stress, so employers bear responsibility for offsetting it, and employees reciprocate the concern with commitment.
>
> **Counterpoint.** ① **Operational** — interventions are expensive (facilitators, equipment), slow to show returns, heavy on up-front design costs, and **a growing number of corporations report the expected ROI failed to materialize**; time in the programme is time not working. ② **Invasive** — should your boss tell you how you are supposed to feel? Programmes encouraging **open discussion of stress sources** make it harder to keep work relationships professional. ③ **The line is impossible to draw** between work stress and general life stress — how should a programme operate when the cause is a sick relative or family conflict? It may be more important to **let employees keep their private lives private**.

## Key takeaways

- The model is **stressors → stress → strain**, and **stressors are additive** — a single small one on top of a high load can be too much.
- **Challenge stressors** motivate (in a supportive environment); **hindrance stressors** only damage — and enough of them **cancel the benefits of challenge stressors**.
- **Eustress** is real, but the **inverted-U model is intuitive and weakly supported**.
- **Sleep** is the single most under-rated performance variable; the workarounds (sleep aids, caffeine) backfire, and we cannot judge our own impairment.
- **Presenteeism** spreads illness and slows work — and culture, not just policy, determines whether people come in sick.
- **Work itself is as much an illness risk factor as secondhand smoke**, mostly through the unhealthy behaviours people adopt to cope.
- **Job insecurity → workaholism, burnout, depression**, and burnout compounds in a vicious cycle by generating new stressors.
- Four theories: **COR** (losses hurt more than gains help) · **ERI** (weakly supported; which effort, which reward?) · **JDCS** (control and support **buffer** demands) · **JDR** (the most popular — demands and resources → strain and engagement → performance), with **allostasis** replacing the idea of one ideal equilibrium.
- Work–life balance is a **balancing act**, managed through **boundaries** (physical, psychological, behavioral tactics) — and **time pressure is not the real problem, psychological incursion is**.
- **Spillover crosses over** to partners, family and their workplaces; **enrichment** is the understudied positive twin of conflict.
- Individual approaches: **time management, exercise, resilience, relaxation and mindfulness, social support** — aiming to **recover, not to avoid**.
- Organizational approaches: **job redesign, thoughtful remote work, real recovery experiences, wellness programmes** — the last of which work **only if employees actually use them without stigma**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Stress / stressor / strain** | An unpleasant appraisal of stressors / conditions perceived as challenging or threatening / the psychological, physiological and behavioral consequences |
| **Challenge / hindrance stressors** | Workload, task pressure, time urgency / red tape, office politics, unclear responsibilities |
| **Eustress** | A healthy, positive and constructive appraisal of stressors |
| **Presenteeism** | The act of working while ill or injured |
| **Job insecurity** | The perception that one's job is at risk or that employment is not stable |
| **Workaholism** | Feeling compelled to work from internal pressure, thinking about work when not working, going beyond what is reasonable to one's own detriment |
| **Burnout** | Emotional exhaustion + depersonalization + reduced personal accomplishment |
| **Resources** | Factors within an individual's control that can be expended toward desires, goals or task demands |
| **Demands** | Responsibilities, pressures, obligations and uncertainties faced in the workplace |
| **Allostasis / allostatic load** | Working to find stability by changing behaviour and attitudes / the cumulative effect of stressors given available resources |
| **Work–life boundaries / boundary management** | The lines demarcating our life domains / the tactics used to negotiate time, space and roles across them |
| **Work–life spillover** | Psychological responses to one domain carried over into another |
| **Resilience** | Resistance to the adverse effects of stress and strain |
| **Mindfulness** | Reception, attention and awareness of the present moment, events and experiences |
| **Wellness programs** | Organizationally supported programmes focused on employees' total physical and mental condition |
