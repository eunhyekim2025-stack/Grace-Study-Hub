# Chapter 13 — Power and Politics

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch12-leadership|Ch 12]] · Next: [[ch14-conflict-and-negotiation|Ch 14 — Conflict and Negotiation]]

---

## Core summary

**Power** is the capacity, discretion and means to enforce one's will over others. Its defining property is **dependence**: the more people rely on someone who controls what they want, the more powerful that person becomes — and power **evaporates** when people gain alternatives.

The chapter runs: **power vs leadership** → **five bases of power** → **dependence and networks** → **nine influence tactics** and **political skill** → **how power affects people** (including sexual harassment) → **organizational politics**, its causes and consequences → **voice, silence and impression management** → the **ethics** of behaving politically.

> [!important] Power vs leadership — three differences
> ① **Power does not require goal compatibility, only dependence**; leadership requires some congruence between the leader's goals and the led.
> ② Power focuses on **downward** influence, minimizing the lateral and upward relationships central to leadership.
> ③ Leadership emphasizes **style** ("how supportive should I be?"); power focuses on **tactics for securing compliance**.

## Bases of power

### Formal power — from position

| Base | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Coercive** | Depends on the target's **fear of negative results** from failing to comply | Termination, demotion, undesirable assignments, public embarrassment. **Also comes from withholding key information** — people who hold data others need make them dependent. When subordinates are abused, coercive power is often what stops them retaliating. Even technology carries it: call-centre surveillance systems that machine-score workers against targets and dock them |
| **Reward** | Based on the ability to **distribute rewards** others view as valuable | Financial (pay, raises, bonuses) or nonfinancial (recognition, promotions, interesting assignments, friendly colleagues, preferred shifts or territories). At one large law firm, **political ideology and tenure** predicted uneven reward allocation — older, conservative supervisors distributed discriminately in ways that **perpetuated gender inequality** |
| **Legitimate** | Formal authority to control and use organizational resources, based on **structural position** | So closely associated with hierarchy that **merely drawing longer lines on an org chart** makes people infer the leaders are especially powerful. It includes members' **acceptance** of the position's authority — accepted more readily in fierce disputes, when the person is high status, and when the others are less experienced. It also **dampens other people's coercive power**: an employee high in legitimate power suffers fewer consequences for standing up against something unethical |

### Personal power — from the individual

| Base | Definition | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Expert** | Influence based on **expertise, special skills or knowledge** | As jobs specialize, dependence on experts grows. **But it can backfire**: in turbulence, knowledge-worker teams relying on experts with **entrenched** perspectives can *hurt* innovation, and experts are more likely to ignore others' advice — especially when they see their contribution as an **opportunity** rather than a responsibility to others |
| **Referent** | Influence based on **identification** with someone who has desirable resources or traits | If I like, respect and admire you, you have power over me because I want to please you. Explains celebrity endorsement — and **influencer marketing**, a \$10bn+ business, where **92% of consumers trust an influencer endorsement more than a celebrity one**. Companies like P&G and Cisco now recruit employees their peers consider "influencers" |

> [!tip] Which base works best
> **Referent power is an especially powerful motivator.** Conversely, dependents perceive **coercive and low-referent leaders as ineffective**, become less loyal and direct **deviant behaviour at them**. (Interestingly, dependents read **angry** leaders as higher in formal power, and become more loyal and rate them more effective.)

## Dependence: the key to power

**The general dependence postulate:** the greater B's dependence on A, the more power A has over B. **The more you expand your own options, the less power you place in others' hands** — which is why organizations develop **multiple suppliers** rather than rely on one.

**Three things create dependence:**

| Condition | Meaning | Illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **Importance** | If nobody wants what you have, no dependence forms — degrees run from survival need to fashion, convenience or relational importance | Which is why organizations should have **several people** build client and supplier relationships, not one |
| **Scarcity** | Low supply relative to demand gives holders bargaining power | Photographers are increasing in number and easy to find, so organizations cut salaried roles in favour of contracts. Medical professionals face tight supply and high demand, producing **higher pay and job security** |
| **Nonsubstitutability** | The fewer viable substitutes, the more power | Steinway — "there is nothing like it in the world" — lost its hold when **Yamaha** used mass manufacturing and automation to produce **consistent** quality, while hand-crafted Steinways each sounded different and were hard to adapt to different halls. AI poses the same threat to skilled trades: their skills become **substitutable** |

Money is a potent dependence: **16% of gig workers are financially dependent on a single client**, and public-sector contractors often depend on **one government contract** for continuity — cut or suspended during shutdowns.

### Networks

**Three formal small-group networks:**

| Network | Structure | Speed | Accuracy | Leader emergence | Member satisfaction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chain** | Rigidly follows the chain of command | Moderate | **High** | Moderate | Moderate |
| **Wheel** | A central figure is the conduit for all communication | **Fast** | **High** | **High** | Low |
| **All-channel** | Everyone communicates with everyone — self-managed teams | **Fast** | Moderate | None | **High** |

Two caveats: **informal networks** exist regardless, and hold real political influence — disrupting innovation teams and blocking new relationships when dense. And a structure that *permits* communication does not mean communication *happens* — guarded members in an all-channel network will still share selectively.

**Social network analysis** maps the informal flow. Individuals or groups are **nodes**, links are **ties**; frequent exchange means **strong ties**; unconnected nodes reach each other through **brokers**. The graphical map is a **sociogram** — an org chart shows how authority is *supposed* to flow, a sociogram shows how resources **really** flow.

> [!important] Brokers hold power
> Brokers leverage unique resources acquired from different groups, so many people depend on them. UK National Health Service data show **change agents succeed more if they are information brokers**. Paradoxically, powerful brokers in marketing agency networks often **fail to perceive** opportunities to use that power — partly because executives have unreliable information about strangers in surrounding networks.
>
> Network position is also predicted by personality: **insensitivity to rewards** (making them resistant to influence), **extroversion** and even **Dark Triad** traits. And **stereotype threat** operates — because brokerage demands agency, power and assertiveness, contradicting stereotypically feminine qualities, **women in brokerage positions experience more anxiety and fear of negative evaluation**.

## Influence tactics

**Influence tactics** are the ways individuals translate power bases into specific actions. Nine of them:

| Tactic | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Legitimacy** | Relying on your authority position, or that a request accords with policies or rules |
| **Rational persuasion** | Logical arguments and factual evidence showing the request is reasonable |
| **Inspirational appeals** | Emotional commitment through appeals to values, needs, hopes and aspirations |
| **Consultation** | Involving the target in deciding how to accomplish the plan |
| **Exchange** | Rewarding the target with benefits or favours for agreeing |
| **Personal appeals** | Asking for compliance based on friendship or loyalty |
| **Ingratiation** | Flattery, praise or friendly behaviour **before** making the request |
| **Pressure** | Warnings, repeated demands, threats |
| **Coalitions** | Enlisting others' aid or support to persuade the target |

**Effectiveness.** Rational persuasion, inspirational appeals and consultation are roughly equally effective for performance. **Pressure backfires and is typically the least effective.** Ingratiation improves relational outcomes — but perhaps only where the audience does not much care about the request or it is routine.

**Direction matters:**

| Direction | Preferred tactics |
|---|---|
| **Upward** | Rational persuasion (the **only** tactic effective both up and down, though stronger downward) |
| **Downward** | Rational persuasion · inspirational appeals · ingratiation · legitimacy · consultation · exchange |
| **Lateral** | Rational persuasion · **ingratiation** (most effective here) · legitimacy · personal appeals · exchange · coalitions |

> [!tip] Sequence soft before hard
> Begin with **"softer" tactics resting on personal power** — personal and inspirational appeals, rational persuasion, consultation. If they fail, move to **"harder" tactics** — exchange and coalitions — which lean on formal power and carry greater costs and risks.
>
> **Audience matters too.** People who comply with **soft** tactics tend to be reflective, intrinsically motivated, with high self-esteem and a desire for control. People who comply with **hard** tactics are action-oriented, extrinsically motivated and focused on getting along rather than getting their own way. Research finds **no gender difference** in effectiveness — everyone benefits from soft or neutral rather than hard tactics.

### Automatic and controlled processing

| Mode | Definition | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|
| **Automatic processing** | Relatively **superficial** consideration of evidence, taking little time or effort and using **heuristics** | Everyone, some of the time. Its danger is being fooled by a jingle or a photograph. Illustration: readers **trust bloggers more** when they disclose being paid for a review, because disclosure cues **expert power** |
| **Controlled processing** | **Detailed** consideration relying on facts, figures and logic | Predicted by **need for cognition** — the ongoing desire to think and learn. High-NFC people are persuaded by evidence and evaluate arguments carefully; low-NFC people rely on intuition and emotion |

We do not "choose" one or the other, but leaders can design messaging for both — **emotional messaging and sensory stimulation** where automatic processing dominates (initial social media exposure); **expert power and rational persuasion** where people are motivated to dig deeper (deciding whether to pursue a job).

### Political skill

**Political skill** — the ability to influence others so that one's own objectives are attained. The politically skilled use influence tactics more effectively by drawing on knowledge of others' demands, resources and preferences; recognize opportunities and evaluate the pros and cons of behaviours; and **exert influence without others detecting it** — a key element, since being labelled political is damaging.

**Fit matters.** People who **fit the organization's culture** obtain more influence: extroverts in team-oriented organizations, highly conscientious people where technical solo work is valued — because they perform well in the domains deemed important. The organization itself determines which subset of tactics is acceptable.

**Who develops it:** extroverts most, with agreeableness and conscientiousness also contributing; and motivated, risk-tolerant and **Machiavellian** employees are more motivated to use political influence — sometimes operating under the **"camouflage" of political skill** toward self-interested, destructive ends.

**Payoffs:** self-efficacy, satisfaction, commitment, slightly less stress, better performance and productivity, more OCB — and most importantly **career outcomes**: better reputation, higher income, more prestigious positions and greater career satisfaction. The mechanisms are the **reputation and confidence** the skills bring.

## How power affects people

> [!warning] Does power corrupt?
> Power **energizes** people — and makes them rely more on gut feeling, become self-serving and potentially corrupt. Two mechanisms: power lets people pursue self-interest **because they can**, and it **liberates them to focus inward**, weighting their own aims more heavily. Power also leads people to **"objectify" others** — seeing them as tools for instrumental goals — and to treat relationships as peripheral.
>
> The powerful react **especially negatively to threats to their competence**, holding on to power and taking action to retain it whether or not it harms others. Formal power alters perception — the powerful are **quicker to detect anger**, because it threatens their power. They make more **self-interested decisions under moral hazard**, are **more willing to denigrate others**, and make **overconfident decisions**.

**Who resists corruption:** people with **collectivist values** and **strong moral identities**. **Who succumbs:** people with a strong need for power, a tendency to respond to threat by defending status and legitimacy, and **Machiavellian** traits. **Environmental protections:** identifying with the group you hold power in, and **clear accountability** for actions.

**Power is not permanent.** It is maintained through influence tactics, and propped up by followers through **system justification** and **attribution** ([[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]], [[ch12-leadership|Ch 12]]). It is lost to environmental competition and competitors' skill — but power-holders are often responsible for their own fall through **ethical transgressions producing public outrage, an inability to see threats from a comfortable position, and biased decision making**.

### Sexual harassment

**Sexual harassment** — any unwanted activity of a sexual nature that affects an individual's employment and creates a hostile work environment. The EEOC definition: unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favours, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature that disrupts work performance or creates an intimidating, hostile or offensive environment.

**Prevalence:** up to **52% of women and 43% of men** report sexual harassment behaviours within the past twelve months; up to **22% of women and 7% of men** report experiencing sexual harassment at work in their lifetime. Of nearly 41,000 EEOC charges over ten years, **over 25%** were in the customer-facing sector (accommodation, food services, retail). 140 nations have at least some protective policies, though implementation is questionable — **50% of Americans believe men getting away with harassment is a major problem**, and **46%** believe women not being believed is.

> [!important] Power is central
> Harassment is more likely where there are **large power differentials** — the supervisor–employee dyad being the paradigm case, since supervisors control performance reviews, salary increases and other scarce valued resources. It is **especially pronounced for employees with newly acquired power**. Where there are **no effective controls** to detect and prevent it — and no protection for whistleblowers from retaliation — abusers act and keep acting.
>
> **Five manager responsibilities:** ① an active policy defining harassment, stating that it is a firing offence, and setting complaint procedures ② reassurance that **no retaliation** follows a complaint ③ investigate **every** complaint and inform legal and HR ④ ensure offenders are **disciplined or terminated** ⑤ in-house training to raise awareness.
>
> Being unaware does not protect a manager or the organization: **if investigators believe a manager could have known, both the manager and the company can be held liable.**

## Politics: power in action

**Organizational politics** focuses on the use of power to affect decision making, sometimes for self-serving and organizationally unsanctioned ends. **Political behaviour** — activities not required as part of a person's formal role that influence, or try to influence, the **distribution of advantages and disadvantages** in the organization.

Examples span withholding key information from decision makers, joining a coalition, whistleblowing, spreading rumours, leaking to the media, exchanging favours, and lobbying for or against a person or decision. **Often negative, but not always.**

> [!note] Why politics is inevitable
> A politics-free organization is possible **only if** all members share the same goals and interests, resources are **not scarce**, and performance outcomes are **completely clear and objective**. That describes no real organization.
>
> But the most important driver is that **most of the "facts" used to allocate limited resources are open to interpretation**. What is good performance? What is an adequate improvement? *"It is in this large and ambiguous middle ground of organizational life — where the facts do not speak for themselves — that politics flourish."* One person's "selfless effort to benefit the organization" is another's "blatant attempt to further their own interests."

**Four ways people construe politics:** **reactive** (destructive and manipulative) · **reluctant** (a necessary evil) · **strategic** (a useful way of getting things done) · **integrated** (central to the reality of decision making). Most experienced managers see political behaviour as a major part of organizational life, ethical where it directly harms no one, and necessary — believing someone who never uses it **will have a hard time getting things done**.

### Gossip and the grapevine

The **grapevine** is an organization's informal communication network. Usually viewed negatively — and untrustworthy gossip does cause people to unfairly judge and dislike others — it is a **mixed blessing**: it raises performance through social pressure while **undermining well-being**.

Practical uses: grapevine information from peers affects whether applicants join, **over and above** Glassdoor ratings; managers can study gossip flows through social networks to see how information moves; and **"small talkers"** — people who regularly communicate about insignificant issues — tend to be **influencers** and are significantly more likely to **retain their jobs during layoffs**. But how you do it matters: **collaborating and helping**, rather than gossiping, is what earns a central network position.

### Causes of political behaviour

| Individual factors | Organizational factors |
|---|---|
| **High self-monitors** — more sensitive to social cues, more socially conforming, more skilled politically | **Reallocation of resources** — declining or changing resource patterns |
| **Internal locus of control** — believing they can control the environment, so more proactive in manipulating situations | **Promotion opportunities** |
| **High Machiavellian personality** — the will to manipulate and the desire for power | **Low trust** |
| **Organizational investment** — the more future benefit expected and the more to lose, the **less** likely to use illegitimate means | **Role ambiguity** — the less clear prescribed behaviour is, the more unnoticed political activity is possible |
| **Perceived job alternatives** — more alternatives (favourable market, scarce skills, reputation, outside contacts) → more likely to play politics | **Unclear performance evaluation system** |
| **Expectations of success** — high expectations belong to experienced, powerful, politically polished people **and to inexperienced employees who misjudge their chances** | **Zero-sum reward practices** · **democratic decision making** · **high performance pressure** · **self-serving senior managers** |

The **zero-sum approach** treats the reward "pie" as fixed, so any gain by one person comes at another's expense — which **encourages making others look bad** and inflating the visibility of your own work.

### Cialdini's seven principles of persuasion

The social forces behind why people acquiesce:

**Reciprocity** (giving back to those who gave to us) · **Consistency/commitment** (staying consistent with decisions already made) · **Social proof** (looking to others for validation) · **Liking** (people who like each other agree) · **Authority** (saying yes to power-holders) · **Scarcity** (wanting what is less available) · **Unity** (being most influenced by those with whom we identify).

### Responses to politics

For most people unwilling to play the game, outcomes are **predominantly negative**: politics leads to **decreased job satisfaction, increased anxiety and stress, increased turnover and reduced performance** — regardless of how necessary people believe politics to be, apparently because political environments are **perceived as unfair**, which demotivates.

> [!important] Understanding moderates the effect
> *"An individual who has a clear understanding of who is responsible for making decisions and why they were selected would have a better understanding of how and why things happen than someone who does not."* Where **politics and understanding are both high**, performance **increases** — those people see political activity as an **opportunity**. Where understanding is low, they see politics as a **threat**, and performance suffers.

**Defensive behaviours** — reactive and protective behaviours to avoid action, blame or change:

| Category | Behaviours |
|---|---|
| **Avoiding action** | **Overconforming** ("the rules clearly state…") · **buck passing** · **feigning ignorance** · **stretching** (turning a two-week task into four months) · **stalling** (publicly supportive, privately doing nothing) |
| **Avoiding blame** | **Bluffing** (documenting activity to project competence — "covering your rear") · **playing safe** (only high-probability projects, getting risky decisions approved above, staying neutral in conflicts) · **justifying** · **scapegoating** · **misrepresenting** (distortion, embellishment, deception, selective presentation, obfuscation) |
| **Avoiding change** | **Prevention** (stopping a threatening change) · **self-protection** (guarding information or resources during change) |

In the short run defensiveness protects self-interest; in the long run **it wears people down**. (The chapter distinguishes this from legitimate self-defence by people who have been bullied, harassed or victimized.)

## Voice and silence

| | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Voice** | **Discretionary** communication of suggestions, concerns or opinions about work-related issues **to people who might be able to take appropriate action** |
| **Silence** | **Discretionary withholding** of the same from those who might act on it |

**Voice matters** because it gives influence to people without formal leadership positions or substantial power bases — challenging the status quo, supporting others' viewpoints, or adding constructively. Predictors: self-evaluation, personal initiative, sense of responsibility and engagement, workplace climate, the supervisor's emotions and behaviour — and among the Big Five, **extroverted, open and conscientious** people voice more. Across 50,000+ employees, practices encouraging voice **raised job performance**, and voicing concerns has **positive emotional outcomes** for employees.

> [!warning] How managers suppress voice without meaning to
> Managers often find **"public" voice threatening** to their image or control and prefer it one-on-one. And **when voice is not acted on, employees stop speaking up** — so managers should explain **why** they did not follow through, to preserve psychological safety and justice perceptions.
>
> Culture shapes it: **power distance** norms signal whether speaking up is safe. And it is not only national culture — **organizational** beliefs (valorizing masculinity, minimizing the seriousness of harassment) and **network** characteristics (networks composed mostly of men in central positions) can silence voice about sexual harassment.

**Silence is itself a form of influence** — communicating noninterest, inability to deal with a topic, or a desire to cooperate without interruption. The decision to stay silent turns on perceived **target openness and trustworthiness**, a sense of **powerlessness**, and a tendency toward **negative emotions, especially fear**.

> [!note] The costs of silence
> Managers who speak up about wrongdoing face official sanctioning **plus bullying, reduced satisfaction and lost status**. Employees may get their voice "needs" met by **venting to coworkers** and then say nothing to management. Some silence is strategic — executives stop publicizing sustainability certifications during contradicting scandals for fear of looking hypocritical.
>
> And silence harms its recipients: in experiments, ostracized people felt **worse when not acknowledged at all** — even a **hostile** message improved satisfaction and recovery over being ignored. Recipients also attribute: they see people who withhold information as **unethical**, while the silent person believes they are acting ethically ("better to say nothing than say something I regret").

## Impression management

**Impression management (IM)** — the process by which individuals attempt to control the impressions others form of them. It can be conscious or unconscious, and perceived as authentic or disingenuous.

| Technique | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Conformity** | Agreeing with someone's opinion to gain approval (ingratiation) |
| **Favors** | Doing something nice to gain approval (ingratiation) |
| **Excuses** | Explaining a predicament to minimize its apparent severity (defensive) |
| **Apologies** | Admitting responsibility while seeking pardon (defensive) |
| **Self-promotion** | Highlighting your best qualities, downplaying deficits, calling attention to achievements (self-focused) |
| **Enhancement** | Claiming what you did is more valuable than others would think (self-focused) |
| **Flattery** | Complimenting others' virtues to appear perceptive and likeable (assertive) |
| **Exemplification** | Doing more than needed to show dedication — emailing late from a work computer so the timestamp shows (assertive) |

**When it helps and hurts.** Perceived as **modest, authentic and genuine** — a humorous self-disclosure of something negative — it improves how others see you and the relationship. Perceived as **inauthentic**, it produces performance decrements, anxiety and negative feelings.

> [!warning] IM costs the user too
> Executives who ingratiate themselves to the CEO become **resentful and plot to undermine them** — especially when the CEO is a woman or from an underrepresented ethnicity. Ingratiation also **exhausts and depletes personal resources**, leading to deviant behaviour. And leaders face multiple audiences at once (public, board, other executives, middle managers, employees), so IM that succeeds with one will **inevitably read badly to another**.

**In interviews:** IM is used by applicants **and interviewers**, and it works — it raises perceptions of **warmth and competence**, and interviewers are **surprisingly unable to detect it**, especially when it involves deception. Across thousands of recruiting interviews, IM (appearance-oriented, explicit tactics, verbal cues) strongly predicted outcomes — **but the effects were substantially weaker in highly structured interviews** with pre-written questions focused on qualifications. Manipulative behaviour works best in **ambiguous, unstructured** interviews.

**In performance evaluations, the picture reverses:**

- **Ingratiation** is positively related to performance ratings and reward allocations
- **Self-promotion backfires over time** as people recognise it — self-promoters may receive **lower** ratings

The qualifier: **political skill decides**. People **high** in political skill translate IM into higher appraisals; people **low** in it are more likely to be hurt by the attempt. Interns who ingratiated were usually **disliked — unless they had high political skill**, in which case ingratiation produced more liking and higher performance ratings.

**Cross-culturally:** US managers rated "gentle persuasion" tactics — consultation and inspirational appeal — as **more effective** than managers in China, Hong Kong and Taiwan did. Effective US leaders influence by focusing on **individual goals and the task** (analytical); influential East Asian leaders focus on **relationships among members and meeting the demands of those around them** (holistic). And Chinese subordinates were seen as more agreeable and conscientious when **self-effacing** — but only where it read as **modest rather than supplicating**.

## The ethics of behaving politically

Three questions to apply:

1. **What is the utility of the behaviour?** Sometimes we act politically for little reason, following rigid platitudes ("don't complain," "arrive early and stay late," "under-promise and over-deliver") that are not useful in every situation. **Is it worth the risk?**
2. **Does the utility balance out the harm to others?** Complimenting a supervisor to curry favour is far less harmful than **taking credit for a project others deserved**.
3. **Does it conform to justice and fairness standards?** Could anyone reasonably reject your behaviour?

> [!warning] Who is most at risk of an ethical lapse
> Powerful people become very good at explaining self-serving behaviour as the organization's best interest and arguing persuasively that unfair actions are fair. **Those who are powerful, articulate and persuasive are most vulnerable to ethical lapses because they are most likely to get away with them.** It is a lot easier for the powerless to act ethically — they have very little political discretion to exploit.

### Mapping your political career

Bud Bilanich: *"Working hard isn't going to be enough because your competition is also working hard and making contributions… hard work is kind of like the price of admission."*

The tool is a **political map**: identify whose decisions your ambitions depend on, then sketch your relationship to each — **close, loose, or none**. The obvious implication is to build closer relationships with those you have little influence over. And the best route is often **indirect**: influencing someone who is close to the decision maker often means influencing the decision maker.

## Key takeaways

- **Power = capacity to enforce your will; its essence is dependence.** Expand your options and you reduce others' power over you.
- **Five bases:** coercive, reward, legitimate (formal) · expert, referent (personal). **Referent is the strongest motivator; coercive and low-referent leaders draw deviance.**
- Dependence needs **importance, scarcity and nonsubstitutability** — the Steinway/Yamaha case being the loss of the third.
- **Sociograms show how resources really flow**, and **brokers hold power** — with stereotype threat penalising women in those positions.
- Nine **influence tactics**: sequence soft before hard, and **pressure backfires**. **Rational persuasion is the only tactic that works both upward and downward.**
- **Political skill** predicts income, position and career satisfaction through reputation and confidence — and can camouflage Machiavellian ends.
- Power corrupts by **liberating inward focus**; **sexual harassment is fundamentally a power differential** problem, and managers are liable for what they *could have* known.
- Politics is inevitable because **facts are open to interpretation** in the ambiguous middle ground. It usually hurts those who will not play — unless they **understand** it, in which case performance rises.
- **Voice needs to be acted on or it stops**; **silence is itself influence**, and being ignored is worse than being answered hostilely.
- **IM works in unstructured interviews; ingratiation helps appraisals and self-promotion backfires** — and political skill decides which.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Power / dependence** | The capacity, discretion and means to enforce one's will / the extent to which people rely on a powerful person |
