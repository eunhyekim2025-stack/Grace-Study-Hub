# Chapter 11 — Communication

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch10-understanding-work-teams|Ch 10]] · Next: [[ch12-leadership|Ch 12 — Leadership]]

---

## Core summary

Communication in all contexts is **imperfect and less than ideal**, with barriers systematically and inevitably curtailing effectiveness. The OB skill is **diagnosing communication breakdowns and fixing them**.

Five moves in the chapter:

1. **Interpersonal communication** in three modes — **oral** (synchronicity, listening, meetings, speeches), **written** (email, IM/text, NLP), **nonverbal** (body language, the senses, space and time).
2. **Choosing methods** — matching message to medium, and handling three barriers: **information overload, communication apprehension, crisis**.
3. **Virtual communication** — **channel richness**, videoconferencing, blogging/vlogging/podcasting, e-collaboration and e-learning, and the new "currency" of emojis, usernames and selfies.
4. **Smartphones, social media and cybersecurity** — including the autonomy paradox and the transformation of reputation management.
5. **Cross-cultural communication** — high- vs low-context cultures, five interaction approaches, and a practical guide built on Casmir's **"third culture."**

> [!important] The opening illustration
> A California hospital doctor told a 78-year-old man, surrounded by his family, that he was unlikely to survive. The family was outraged — because the doctor **sent a rolling medical assistant robot** to deliver the news. Certain modes are strongly preferred for certain messages, and getting the pairing wrong destroys the message.

## Oral communication

### Synchronicity

| | Definition | Advantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Synchronous** | Both sender and receiver present, aware and focused on the exchange | **Speed** (message and response in minimal time) · **feedback** (misunderstanding detected and corrected fast) · **exchange** (social, cultural and emotional — building trust, cooperation and agreement; partners develop rhythm and rapport, even in writing) |
| **Asynchronous** | Messages received **outside** a physically or psychologically present exchange | Voicemail; filmed leadership updates employees watch on their own terms (Verizon's CEO and HR leader during COVID) |

### Conversations, discussions and listening

We are usually **bad listeners**, prone to **listener burnout** — tuning out, planning what to say next, rushing to offer advice. Stress accelerates it: when preoccupied, it is hard to be fully present. People also actively avoid conversations they expect to produce conflict or intrude on privacy.

Conversation shapes not only immediate responses but **how people store and integrate knowledge** — influencing shared mental models and transactive memory systems ([[ch10-understanding-work-teams|Ch 10]]).

> [!warning] Conversation is a double-edged sword
> Three documented failures: people **recall less important material** than if thinking alone; they **ignore topics not raised** in the conversation, focusing too narrowly on what was discussed; and they engage in **audience tuning** — framing information for the specific partners, which pulls attention away from important content. (An R&D executive talking to a CEO produces a conversation "tuned toward" possibility and the cutting edge, quietly ignoring logistics and finance.)
>
> And the **Telephone game problem**: the more people a message passes through, the greater the distortion. Product-development engineers so far removed from customers that they build something excellent that **does not match customer expectations**.

**Two listening skills:**

| Skill | Definition | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Active listening** | Actively sensing and processing others' messages (subtle and overt) and responding in ways that show engagement | Training in a Fortune 500 customer service group produced better understanding of customer needs, **higher self-efficacy and confidence**, and reduced anxiety in difficult interactions |
| **Reflective listening** | Acknowledging, restating or reformulating others' messages to give nonjudgmental affirmation and encourage elaboration | Shows attention, builds trust, encourages confiding, reduces missed information. With people who disagree with you it **dampens conflict**, makes you seem a better collaborator and raises their intent to collaborate again |

> [!tip] But do not overdo reflective listening
> It is a skill and at times an art — sometimes as subtle as a nod, a "yes, I understand"; sometimes as direct as *"Here's what I think you are saying…"* Used too much or inappropriately in business it **backfires**. Once both parties clearly share an accurate understanding, **move the conversation to problem solving or action items**.
>
> The process runs **Sensing → Processing → Responding**, with active listening spanning all three and reflective listening operating in the responding phase. Asking **follow-up questions** makes listeners better liked and seen as more effective leaders — and for job seekers, maintaining quality **conversational flow** predicts networking success and stronger contact networks.

### Meetings

Millions take place daily in the US and people spend dozens of hours weekly in them. An Adobe study found "wasteful" meetings (58%) and unexpected phone meetings (32%) among the top three things that **get in the way of work** — while respondents simultaneously called meetings **the most effective form of communication available**.

Organizational experiments: **Amazon** begins meetings with 30 minutes of silent reading of memos and agendas; **Shopify and Asana** hold "no meeting days"; some organizations cap duration. Effective virtual teams **work in bursts** — meeting to coordinate, then long periods of uninterrupted **deep work**.

Findings worth knowing:

- **Meeting satisfaction is a facet of job satisfaction** contributing to how much people like their jobs overall.
- **Nonparticipation** (daydreaming) and **inappropriate interpersonal behaviour** (disrespecting, mocking) most damage satisfaction and perceived effectiveness.
- **Surface acting** in meetings — feigning enthusiasm you do not feel — causes emotional exhaustion and thoughts of quitting ([[ch04-emotions-and-moods|Ch 4]]).
- **Late starts** damage satisfaction, effectiveness perceptions and creativity, and can produce relational conflict. Individually late participants tend to be dissatisfied, thinking about quitting, or low in conscientiousness — and others get angry and want to punish them, **unless the excuse points to something beyond their control**.

Rogelberg's suggestions: **only meet when truly needed · gather input beforehand · provide an agenda · delegate roles · limit distractions · start and end on time.**

### Speeches

Leaders use speeches to communicate **vision** ([[ch12-leadership|Ch 12]]), conveying transformational and charismatic leadership. Influential speakers convey power through **figurative and abstract language**, relate personally to the audience, and foster mystery, discovery and excitement that prompts action.

But tone varies with purpose. Machine-learning analysis of 61 emerging-market CEOs' interviews and speeches found some reliably "excitable" and "dramatic," others "stern," "melancholy" or "rambling." **Stern or melancholy suits an organizational tragedy.** The "rambling" tone is typically an artifact of the power of leadership — **and typically for men**: studies of US senators find **women are less likely than men to ramble** when they have the floor, avoiding long speaking turns because they face **backlash** for them, a product of prejudice and stereotyping.

## Written communication

Even small, incidental written communication matters — a supervisor's **narrative comment** in a quarterly appraisal can drastically affect motivation and fairness perceptions. And distributed remote teams can be effective relying **almost exclusively on written communication**, because writing makes thoughts **concrete and specific** and promotes reflection.

### Email

**3.9 billion active users** worldwide (more than half the world's population) across 5.6 billion accounts, with employees averaging **three hours a day** checking email — roughly 37.5% of a 40-hour week. Time spent daily: 40% one hour or less, 22% 1–2 hours, 16% 2–4 hours, 9% 4–6 hours, **12% more than six hours**.

> [!note] Email's emotional paradox
> Interruptions **feel bad** — they increase time pressure and the sense of not meeting other goals. But being responsive **feels good**, giving a sense of task accomplishment. Whether answering email *is* accomplishment depends entirely on the email's importance.

Three ethical problems email has enabled: **deferent communication** (Enron employees yielding to superiors' preferences from fear of overstepping) · **more willingness to lie** than in person, since email feels less personal and restrained · **incivility**, active or passive. Email incivility produces withdrawal the following week — and **spills over to the recipient's partner**, who also withdraws from their own work. **Passive rudeness may do more damage**, because recipients try to "read into" what the sender meant, producing insomnia and negative affect at the start of the workday.

**Ten inbox rules:** turn off notifications · check at fixed intervals · move mail out of the inbox after reading · use search and filters · archive into a small number of categorized folders · handle important mail individually and less important mail in batches · use "reply all" thoughtfully · **do not treat email like an in-person dialogue** · do not skim or skip · **think before you send**.

### Instant and text messaging

**Text messaging** is tied to phone service and works without internet; **IM** goes through a third-party app (Messenger, Teams, Slack) or social network (DMs). Both were forecast to shift us toward asynchronous communication — instead they became **far more synchronous** than expected.

> [!warning] The immediacy norm
> Unlike email, IM and text carry a **strong immediacy norm**: recipients are expected to reply quickly, briefly and conversationally. This raises perceived support — instructors reachable by IM are seen as friendlier and more available — but Slack's internal research found users averaging **ten hours a weekday** on the app.
>
> Presence indicators and **read receipts** amplify it, producing the sting of being "left on read." The result is **super-charged email**: constant interruption on often trivial issues. And the brief informal format invites sloppiness, curtness, negativity and misunderstanding — people **low in agreeableness and emotional stability** use more negative-emotion words in texts, affecting recipients' moods.
>
> Lucas Miller's account of why it is hard to stop: each notification rewards us with **dopamine**, validating status as an informed member of the "work tribe" — while simultaneously leaving us **anxious about being out of the loop**.

### Natural language processing

Training algorithms on the actual words people use in email, IM, social media and hashtags to measure **emotions, moods, personality traits and stress**. Business applications: predicting product interest, real-time customer sentiment, fraud and compliance detection in accounting, patient experience and diagnostics, sensitive-information detection for cybersecurity, and digital assistants for task management. Market projected to exceed **\$6 billion by 2025**.

## Nonverbal communication

Includes **body language and movement**, information conveyed through **contact and the senses**, and the physical use of **space and time**.

> [!important] Nonverbal cues are believed over words
> Studies indicate people read much more about attitude and emotion from **nonverbal cues than from words** — and when the two conflict, the **cues are more likely to be believed**.

Nonverbal communication is often **unconscious and automatic**, which makes it a transmission vector for bias: merely watching videos of White individuals subtly treating Black individuals poorly prompted observers to **adopt negative racial stereotypes** and form negative impressions of the Black individuals. Body postures and gestures themselves carry stereotypes, with some poses read as masculine and others feminine.

Attributions from nonverbal behaviour are **strong and consistent even when wrong** — people read certain postures as dominant that do not match the person's actual dominant traits. Eye-tracking of project teams found naive observers gazed longer at members using **more dominant gestures and active facial expressions**, rating them more charismatic and as emergent leaders.

**Body language.** Every movement arguably has meaning — smiling to project trustworthiness, uncrossing arms to appear approachable, standing to signal authority. Even a downward head tilt reliably conveys traits and emotions, and people can "pose" the Big Five in photographs. We evaluate the **whole body as a unit**, not isolated gestures — and our attributions **update over time** as the person acts and reacts, rather than sticking from a first impression.

**The senses.**

| Sense | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Paralanguage** — sounds and vocal intonation | Communicates beyond the words; it **persuades and influences**, as when entrepreneurs speak with confidence and conviction despite private reservations |
| **Smell and odour** | Powerful and symbolic — a pleasant scent supports a positive, focused state. Its danger is that it is **one-dimensional**: people cannot distinguish scents well but can always tell **pleasant from unpleasant**. Fishy smells make people **suspicious** of the person — an impression with **nothing to do with actual intentions** |
| **Contact and touch** | Provides support in loss, communicates warmth, and raises team performance through cooperative intent. Handshaking shapes interview impressions. Required in some occupations (doctors, trainers, stylists), normatively fine in others (a tap on the shoulder). But **#MeToo** highlighted inappropriate touching and **COVID** the health risks — 65% of managers and professionals in one survey called hugging coworkers "somewhat common." The rule: **learn and respect boundaries, and keep contact light and brief** |

**Space and time.** Proximity signals liking and acceptance — teachers who took breaks at the same time and taught on the same floor formed more working relationships. But maintained distance depends on the other person's **mood and approachability** (you avoid the supervisor you have heard is in a bad mood). **Speed** is an expression cue: angry motion is jarring and fast, sad motion slow and sauntering — and quicker vocalizations and movements read as **confidence**, persuading more easily. The rapidity or delay of your replies signals what you are prioritizing.

> [!tip] Align the channels
> Watch for **contradictions**. Someone repeatedly glancing at their watch is signalling they want to end the conversation whatever they say. Expressing trust verbally while nonverbally reading "I do not have confidence in you" misinforms. Better to be direct: *"I do not have time to continue now — can we pick this up later?"*

## Choosing methods and handling barriers

### Matching message to method

| Method | Appropriate when… |
|---|---|
| **In-person or virtual meetings** | Setting structure, assumptions or expectations for the whole team · evaluating obstacles as a group · discussing anything involving **emotions** that could be misinterpreted · discussing conflict, performance goals or behavioural issues · collaborating with back-and-forth exchange · gauging receptivity to an idea and improving it |
| **Phone calls** | Needing something in the next thirty minutes · a question requiring a lot of **verbal explanation** · a message needing careful conveyance where in-person impression management is hard (e.g. under emotional labor) |
| **Texts / IM** | A thought on an already-started task · a quick noncritical question many people could answer · brief "FYI" information · sharing a document or link for real-time collaboration · **asking whether someone is available** for a call or meeting |
| **Email** | Relaying to multiple people · confirming expectations after a meeting · **confidential information or formal documentation** · giving official approval · outlining procedures, strategies or steps |

### Three barriers

**Information overload** — a condition in which information inflow exceeds an individual's processing capacity. People then **select, ignore, pass over or forget**, or defer processing until the overload ends. It makes negotiators **give up on deals faster** and jump to conclusions, and the pressure of **multicommunication** (interacting with several people at once) makes people inadvertently rude in a way that spirals.

> [!note] Most people say they *like* it
> Between 2006 and 2016 the share feeling overloaded **fell from 27% to 20%**, while those who like "so much information" **rose from 67% to 77%** — attributed to smart devices that let people keep up, judge trustworthiness faster and feel in control. Which means the genuinely overloaded may be defined by **socioeconomic status**: not everyone has smartphones, tablets, smart homes and broadband. Managers should account for that disparity in setting team communication norms.
>
> Fixes: build **situation awareness** (individual and collective understanding of what to prioritize) plus a plan for acting on it; reduce **ambiguity and complexity** and increase **relevance** — the three characteristics most strongly related to overload; and connect less frequently or at set intervals.

**Communication apprehension** — undue tension and anxiety about communication. People find it hard to talk to others, become anxious, or fall back on a preferred channel when a different one would be better. It often stems from reluctance to use **new media**. Consequences: it reduces electronic communication and therefore **the chance of emerging as a virtual team leader**; it distorts personality attributions and communication-skill scores in interviews requiring role-plays or presentations; and it spikes for new employees facing a new supervisor.

**Crisis.** Hurricane Katrina is the standard case — agencies at all levels surprised, struggling to coordinate and cope with uncertainty and ambiguity. Effective crisis communication has **three stages**: ① communicating in **anticipation and preparation** ② managing responses **while the crisis occurs** ③ communicating with stakeholders **afterward** and catalyzing shared learning.

> [!warning] Motivations shift during crises
> People become driven to **take control and dominate** in wartime crises, and toward **self-criticism and fear of negative consequences** in peacetime crises. Neither aligns with the reassurance and grace under pressure that crisis communication requires.
>
> What helps: messaging that shares **facts, interpretations of the facts, and projections of consequences** at every stage · **working together rather than independently** (people solving a problem separately become overcome by confusion and cannot learn from each other's failures) · technology for rapid accurate distribution. And a caution: a 2018 false-alarm ballistic missile Twitter alert left anxiety **heightened for a week** across 1.2 million analyzed tweets.

## Virtual communication

**Channel richness** — the amount of information transmitted during a communication episode. Rich channels ① handle **multiple cues simultaneously** ② facilitate **rapid feedback** ③ are **personal**.

**Lean → rich:** formal reports and bulletins → memos and letters → email → voicemail → online discussion groups and groupware → prerecorded speeches → telephone conversations → live speeches → videoconferences → **in-person conversations**.

### Videoconferencing

March 2020 saw a record **62 million downloads** of platforms like Google Meet, Zoom and Teams, up 90% from the pre-COVID average — though a 2017 survey already found **80–84%** calling videoconferencing the norm for internal and customer meetings.

**Six practices:** ① mind your **nonverbal behaviour** — look into the camera, lean in, nod; avoid yawning, stretching, eating ② **set the stage** — background, camera position, warm soft lighting ③ **be aware of others** — leave space, resist filling silence, watch how long you have spoken ④ **leverage and test the technology** — raise-hand, reactions, chat, screen share ⑤ **manage Zoom fatigue** — minimize the window, and **hide self-view**, which is draining and pulls attention from others ⑥ use it **innovatively** — virtual coffee breaks, virtual coworking, virtual office hours.

### Blogging, vlogging, podcasting

| Medium | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Blog** | A site used by a person, team or company to share primarily **written** content and engage an audience — the key is **engagement and searchability** | Intuit QuickBooks' organized blog of free resources for running a business, which attracts prospective customers |
| **Vlog** | The same function via **video** — especially useful where products are used in projects, need step-by-step instruction, or require proficiency | *Bon Appétit*'s test-kitchen videos, watched "like catching up with old friends" |
| **Podcast** | Primarily **audio**, with more emphasis on in-depth discussion and storytelling | John Deere's *On Life and Land*; Trader Joe's *Inside Trader Joe's*; Girlboss/Sephora's *#LIPSTORIES* |

### E-collaboration and e-learning

Platforms through which employees **do their work or train to do it** — Microsoft Teams, Google Workspace, Facebook Workplace; Blackboard, Moodle, Google Classroom, MOOCs.

For **e-collaboration**: leadership becomes **more salient** online because physical cues are missing, so **LMX** matters more for empowering followers. Members need specific qualifications — motivation, knowledge, attentiveness, composure. Systems should be monitored for **frequency and quality** of exchange.

For **e-learning**: effectiveness depends on design, and with the right conditions — **autonomy, collaboration, feedback** — it can be equally effective. **Blended formats** work well, with learners **outperforming** in-person learners when given feedback and collaboration opportunities. Autonomy matters most in blended formats, where people often have little control over which parts are online.

### The new currency

People prefer to communicate in **pictures rather than words** when separated by time and distance. Across 85,000+ Facebook users, **90% regularly used emojis**, with young, women and extroverted users using them more.

> [!warning] Three cautions
> **Emojis in a first impression** make you look **less competent** and make others less willing to share information — test the waters and follow local norms.
> **Usernames** are read consistently for Big Five traits but are **highly inaccurate** windows into actual personality.
> **Selfies** are read fairly accurately (less so for agreeableness and self-esteem), and extroverts post more — but Instagram users who post selfies **more often are rated more negatively** (less successful) than judicious posters.

## Smartphones, social media and cybersecurity

**Scale:** ~3.5 billion social media users (about 45% of the world), 68% of US adults on Facebook, an average of **three hours a day** on social media — combined with email, **six hours daily**. Over 2.5 billion people own smartphones, ~81% of Americans.

### Smartphones

> [!important] The autonomy paradox
> Increased connection gives people a **deeper sense of autonomy** — they can control when and where they work. So they use it **everywhere and all the time**, which **strips them of that autonomy**.

**Technoference** — the ways technology interferes with rather than assists life. Constant connectedness makes people **enjoy in-person interaction less**. Tracking over a million high school students across 25 years found happiness and well-being **declining steadily after smartphones rose in 2012**, not attributable to the Great Recession. One argument is evolutionary incompatibility — phones pull attention from meaningful attachment. The counter-argument: **digital natives adapt**, and smartphone use has led many to think more abstractly about work and integrate the technology into their roles.

**Across the workday:** late-night use interferes with sleep so employees **start already depleted and less engaged**; work engagement during the day **reduces** bringing work home; and evening usage inhibits the **recovery activities** needed to reduce strain. The organizational answer found by one large footwear manufacturer: let employees communicate in the ways they want and build **work–life-balance-friendly norms**, avoiding "the trap of constant connectivity."

### Social media

Businesses use it to discover trends, connect with and reach audiences, and build brands; leaders use it to capture attention, convey information, evangelize a vision, dialogue with stakeholders, mobilize people and direct information flow; employees use it to connect and collaborate — and to **voice concerns and stand up to perceived unfairness**. Many organizations also build **enterprise social software** internally.

**Social media as a context, not a technology** — a virtual world affecting emotions, thoughts and behaviours, which is why personality manifests there. People accurately detect **agreeableness, extroversion and emotional stability** from posts, and do so **spontaneously**. Narcissism links to update frequency, especially for self-presentation, social comparison and gaining followers — and observers detect **narcissism and psychopathy** accurately, though **Machiavellianism is harder to spot**.

**Personal and relational outcomes.** The **shallowing hypothesis** holds that social media produces less reflective, more rapid thought and shallow judgments. The effects are genuinely double-edged: good for well-being when it makes **meaningful connections**, bad when it drives **comparison or alienation**; it reduces loneliness when used to forge and enhance relationships but **not when used for escapism or as a substitute for deeper in-person engagement**. It produces constructive dialogue with managers only where people feel **autonomy and psychological safety** online and identify with the organization. And hundreds of studies show it damaging self-worth and self-esteem — as researchers put it, on social media *"every day is a high school reunion."*

**Organizational outcomes.** Management cannot control what employees post, and even well-intentioned people post damaging or sensitive content — PepsiCo's "lady Doritos" episode; the *New York Daily News* social media team firing back with disgruntled gifs after being laid off. This is not trivial: corporate and customer Twitter messaging **predicted stock analyst recommendations** in S&P 500 companies.

> [!note] Reputation management has been inverted
> Previously, news outlets obtained and broadcast information, and organizations shaped reputation by cultivating media relationships, threatening lawsuits or funding withdrawal, and buying advertising. **Social media put distribution in the hands of people** — millions can post factual or fake content that diffuses through networks. Legal threat and control are impractical at that scale, which is why many organizations adopt **restrictive social media policies** limiting employees' freedom to speak — creating a standing tension between employee authenticity and organizational brand protection.

### Cybersecurity

Employee data is as valuable a target as customer data — enough for identity theft and insurance fraud. Employees, doubting that networks safeguard their information, respond by **sharing less and staying vigilant**. Employers should ① **understand the threats** in order to mitigate them ② build a **culture that values cybersecurity** ③ **adapt to changing threats**.

## Cross-cultural communication

> [!important] The framing
> A West African saying: **strangers see only what they know.** Everyone enters interactions carrying assumptions, histories, experiences, traditions, practices and languages — mosaics of different tiles ([[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]]), meeting as strangers with imperfect understanding.
>
> The L'Oréal case makes it concrete. A manager: *"The more we debate openly and the more strongly we disagree in meetings, the closer we get to excellence."* An Indonesian employee: *"To an Indonesian person, confrontation in a group setting is extremely negative, because it makes the other person lose face."* A Mexican employee: *"In Mexican culture, open disagreement is considered rude, disrespectful, and too aggressive."*

### High- and low-context cultures

| | **High-context** | **Low-context** |
|---|---|---|
| **Rely on** | Nonverbal and **subtle cues**; official status, place in society and reputation carry weight. **What is not said may matter more than what is said** | **Spoken and written words**; body language and formal titles are secondary |
| **Examples** | Japan, Kenya, Saudi Arabia, India (then Russia, France) | United States, Australia, Germany, Denmark (then Poland) |
| **Agreements** | **Oral agreements imply strong commitments**; casual conversation is relationship- and trust-building | **Enforceable written contracts**, precisely worded and highly legal |
| **Credibility from** | Age, experience, rank | Directness — managers expected to be explicit and precise |

Two consequences: high-context approaches may produce **better negotiation outcomes**, by directing attention to subtle unspoken cues; and **lean channels (email, text) disadvantage high-context communicators** who depend on nonverbal context. Multicultural individuals can be **"ambidextrous,"** switching modes and channels to match the partner.

**Situations modulate culture.** Physical (opportunity for personal contact), affective (risk or insecurity) and cognitive (ambiguity) features make cultural aspects more or less salient — and extremity heats things up, as with interpreters deployed in Iraq being **ten times more likely to die in combat** than other soldiers. In routine contexts, **norms** matter most; in ethical or morally charged situations, **values** begin to matter more than norms — which is why people sometimes act against their cultural proclivities.

### Five interaction approaches

Positioned by how much you adopt **your own** culture versus **the other's**:

| Approach | Own / Other | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **Avoiding** | Low / Low | Putting aside cultural preferences, values, practices and customs, often for tactical or strategic reasons |
| **Imposing** | High / Low | Asserting your own without acknowledging the other's |
| **Embracing** | Low / High | Putting aside your own to acknowledge or embrace the other's |
| **Synergizing** | High / High | Celebrating **both** partners' cultures, often improvisationally and flexibly |
| **Compromising** | Mid / Mid | Give-and-take — recognizing conflicts, embracing some and setting aside others to reduce friction |

A **third dimension** overlays these: how extreme or morally charged the situation is. Where the consequences of cultural expression become great, people **strategically abstain** from expressing or from calling out violations.

> [!tip] The frame that matters
> This applies to **both** partners. Do not ask what can be done to manage "the other person" — ask how both partners can **come to an agreement on the cultural approach** given the situation.

### Five aspects that differ

1. **Semantics** — words mean different things across cultures, and some do not translate: Spanish *pena ajena* and German *fremdschämen* ("to be embarrassed for someone"); Thai *greng-jai* (fear of imposing on someone by asking for help). Some cultures have vocalizations and accents conveying emotions **virtually uninterpretable** to outsiders.
2. **Word connotations** — Japanese *hai* translates as "yes" but connotes **"Yes, I'm listening"** rather than "Yes, I agree." Note the asymmetry: negative perceptions of a **word-usage error** are softened once people learn you are from another culture, but **etiquette violations are not** — so norm and etiquette mistakes do far more damage than wording mistakes.
3. **Tone differences** — formal versus informal, and context-dependent. Mexican tire-industry employees are relatively formal, using *usted* rather than *tú* at work. In email between Chinese and US employees of one multinational, Chinese employees preferred **"facework"** ("I hope all is well") and put the request **at the end** — behaviours US employees disliked. Even gratitude differs: Americans expressed it through **touch and contact**, Taiwanese through **self-improvement**; verbal thanks read as emotionally negative and uncomfortable to Chinese compared with Canadian participants.
4. **Tolerance for and handling of conflict** — individualists are comfortable with **direct conflict** and make disagreements overt; collectivists acknowledge conflict **only implicitly**, avoid emotionally charged disputes, and attribute conflict to the **situation**, so they may not need explicit apologies, where individualists prefer public acceptance of responsibility.
5. **What information gets communicated** — Japanese storytelling used more collectivist language and values than American; Korean Facebook users treated the network more relationally than Americans; Spanish-speaking Latinx participants preferred **metaphor and analogy**, found them more convincing, and liked people who used them more.

Aggression standards also differ: Israel, Pakistan and Japan draw a sharper line between **verbal and physical** aggression; the US and Israel treat infringement on **personal resources** as aggressive; Pakistan differentiates **degrees of threat**.

### Casmir's guide — building a "third culture"

We communicate poorly across cultures because **we generalize from cultural origin alone** — stereotyping. In Jamaican–Indian software teams this appeared as **"culturizing"**: negatively stereotyping culturally different members and reinforcing group boundaries.

Casmir's answer: there are far too many cultures for anyone to understand completely, and individuals interpret their own cultures differently — so intercultural communication must rest on **sensitivity and pursuit of common goals**. The ideal is an ad hoc **"third culture"** a group forms by incorporating aspects of each member's communication preferences, whose norms create common ground.

| Stage | Practices |
|---|---|
| **Before** | ① **Know yourself** — recognise your own cultural identity and biases ② **Foster a climate of mutual respect, fairness and democracy** — the "third culture" context that transcends each person's norms; decide how you will stay open-minded and willing to modify stereotypes |
| **During** | ③ **Consider the other's viewpoint** before speaking — is this insensitive? does it assume something about their background? is it appropriate to just ask? Take a collaborative problem-solving approach to conflict ④ **Learn from misunderstandings** — acknowledge them, correct the assumption, and move on collectively in a way that **strengthens the relationship** |
| **After** | ⑤ **Proactively maintain the group's identity and culture** — reinforce common goals, mutual respect and adaptation to individual preferences, and fold new insights into the group's permanent understanding |

## Key takeaways

- Communication is **always imperfect**; the skill is diagnosing the breakdown. Match the **message to the method** — the robot delivering a terminal prognosis is the failure case.
- We are bad listeners by default. **Active** and **reflective** listening are trainable — and reflective listening should stop once understanding is shared, moving to problem solving.
- Meetings are simultaneously the biggest obstacle to work and the most effective communication form; **only meet when needed, with an agenda, starting and ending on time**.
- Email carries an emotional paradox and enables new incivility; **IM/text carry an immediacy norm** that turns them into super-charged interruptions.
- **Nonverbal cues are believed over words** when the two conflict — and they transmit bias unconsciously.
- **Channel richness** ranks in-person highest and formal reports lowest; the barriers are **information overload, communication apprehension and crisis**.
- Smartphones create the **autonomy paradox**; social media is a **context** where personality manifests, and it inverted how reputation is built.
- Cross-culturally, the split is **high- vs low-context**, the five approaches run **avoiding → imposing → embracing → synergizing → compromising**, and the practical goal is Casmir's **third culture**.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Synchronous / asynchronous communication** | Both parties present, aware and focused / messages received outside a present exchange |
| **Active listening** | Actively sensing and processing messages and responding to show engagement |
| **Reflective listening** | Acknowledging, restating or reformulating a message to affirm and invite elaboration |
| **Channel richness** | The amount of information transmitted during a communication episode |
| **Information overload** | Information inflow exceeding an individual's processing capacity |
| **Communication apprehension** | Undue tension and anxiety about communication |
| **Technoference** | The ways technology interferes with rather than assists our lives |
| **Shallowing hypothesis** | That social media use produces less reflective, more rapid thought and shallow judgments |
| **Enterprise social software** | An organization's in-house social-networking application |
| **High-context / low-context cultures** | Cultures relying heavily on nonverbal and subtle cues / heavily on words to convey meaning |
| **Avoiding / imposing / embracing / synergizing / compromising** | The five cross-cultural interaction approaches |
| **Third culture** | An ad hoc common-ground culture a group forms by incorporating each member's communication preferences |
