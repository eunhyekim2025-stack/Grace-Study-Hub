# Chapter 4 — Emotions and Moods

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch03-job-attitudes|Ch 3]] · Next: [[ch05-personality-and-individual-differences|Ch 5 — Personality]]

---

## Core summary

Historically OB assumed emotions were **detrimental to performance**, so managers tried to build emotion-free organizations and researchers studied mainly strong negative emotions such as anger. The **Affective Revolution** reversed that: some emotions hinder performance, some are neutral, some are constructive — and employees bring them to work every day regardless.

The chapter's arc:

1. **Distinguish affect, emotions and moods**, map them on the **affective circumplex**, and note the special case of **moral emotions**.
2. **Trace where feelings come from** — personality, time of day, day of week, weather (mostly a myth), stress, social interaction, sleep, exercise, gender identity (mostly stereotype).
3. **Emotional labor**: the gap between **felt** and **displayed** emotions, managed through **surface** or **deep acting**, with **emotional dissonance** as the cost.
4. **Affective events theory (AET)** as the causal model, **emotional intelligence (EI)** as the capability, and **emotion regulation** as the technique.
5. **Apply all of it** to selection, decision making, creativity, motivation, leadership, negotiation, customer service, work–life conflict, deviance and safety.

> [!important] The framing that matters
> Emotions do **not** make thinking irrational — they supply essential information about how we understand the world, and they guide behaviour. Cold rationality fails because it refuses to acknowledge that people are, by nature, emotional. Good management is **not** creating an emotion-free environment.

## What emotions and moods are

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Affect** | A generic term covering a broad range of feelings, including emotions and moods |
| **Emotions** | Intense, discrete, short-lived feeling experiences, often caused by a specific event |
| **Moods** | Feelings that are longer-lived and less intense than emotions and that lack a contextual stimulus |
| **Positive affect (PA)** | An affective dimension with excitement, enthusiasm and elation at the high end |
| **Negative affect (NA)** | An affective dimension with nervousness, stress and anxiety at the high end |

| | Emotions | Moods |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause** | A specific event | Often general and unclear |
| **Duration** | Seconds or minutes | Hours or days |
| **Specificity** | Many distinct kinds (anger, fear, sadness, happiness, disgust, surprise…) | Broad |
| **Expression** | Usually accompanied by distinct facial expressions | Generally not |

**Affect varies by valence** — how positive or negative the feeling is.

### The affective circumplex

Emotions can be arranged by their degree of positivity and negativity: *elated* is high PA, *bored* is low PA; *nervous* is high NA, *relaxed* is low NA. Some sit on both axes at once — *upset* and *sad* are simultaneously low PA **and** high NA; *happy* and *content* are high PA **and** low NA. Not everything fits: **surprise** is not clearly positive or negative.

### The basic emotions

Dozens exist, and researchers have long tried to reduce them to a fundamental set. Many agree on **six universal emotions**: anger, fear, sadness, happiness, disgust and surprise. Critics argue that thinking in "basic" emotions loses the bigger picture, because emotions mean different things in different contexts and cultures.

Identifying them by facial expression is harder than it looks: some emotions are too complex to show on a face, and although people recognize emotions across cultures at better-than-chance rates, accuracy **falls for cultural groups with less exposure to one another**. Machine-learning work on speeches by 100 professional actors across English-speaking countries found people better at recognizing emotions from actors of their **own country** — there are dialect differences even in vocal emotional expression.

### Moral emotions

**Moral emotions** have moral implications because of our instant judgment of the situation that evokes them — disgust at a coworker's slur, sympathy for suffering, guilt about our own wrongdoing, anger at injustice, contempt for the unethical.

> [!important] Ethics is more affective than it looks
> People long believed ethical decision making ran on thought and cognition. Research on moral emotions increasingly says otherwise: **ethical decisions are primarily based on feelings**, even though we experience moral issues as logical and reasonable.
>
> Two dangers follow. Shared group beliefs can lead us to **justify purely emotional reactions as rationally "ethical"** simply because others share them. And we judge and punish **outgroup** members more harshly than ingroup members for the same transgression, even when trying to be objective — an ethics double standard ([[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]]).

Moral emotions also behave differently: **moral anger** makes us more likely to confront the situation than ordinary anger does. Emergency doctors observing patients' suffering felt sympathetic moral emotions and advocated for the patient; observing colleagues' unethical behaviour made them angry and led them to sanction.

### Experiencing moods

The **positivity offset**: at zero input — when nothing in particular is going on — most individuals experience a **mildly positive mood**. It holds across job settings; British call centre representatives reported positive moods **58% of the time** despite a stressful environment.

But negative emotions loom larger: people think about events that created strong negative emotions **five times as long** as events that created strong positive ones.

> [!note] Culture shapes the experience, not the emotion
> People across cultures experience and interpret emotions similarly — everyone views hate, terror and rage as dangerous and desires joy, love and happiness. What differs is **which emotions a culture values**. Mexico and Brazil emphasize positive emotion and expression; Japan and Russia treat negative emotions as useful and constructive.
>
> This has consequences. Negative emotions are **less damaging to health** in Eastern (negative-emotion-valuing) cultures — valuing negative affect lets people accept circumstances and cope, and may let managers think more critically and fairly. And it has a hiring consequence: European Americans conveyed more PA in job applications and remote interviews than Hong Kong Chinese, and European American hiring managers rated the ideal applicant as displaying **more** PA and were more likely to hire those who did.

## Sources of emotions and moods

| Source | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| **Personality** | Affect has a trait component — some people are built to feel certain moods more often. **Affect intensity** is the individual difference in *strength*: affectively intense people feel both positives and negatives more deeply. Across 100+ studies, the tendency to experience positive emotions is the **strongest predictor of engagement** |
| **Time of day** | PA rises after sunrise, peaks **late morning (10am–noon)**, declines until early evening, rises again until midnight, then falls until sunrise. NA fluctuates less, but generally **increases across the day** — lowest early morning, highest late evening |
| **Day of the week** | US adults have highest PA on Fri/Sat/Sun and lowest on **Monday**; **Monday is the highest-NA day across most cultures**. Japan is the exception — PA is *higher* on Monday than Friday or Saturday. Sunday can pair high PA (day off) with rising NA (dread of the week) |
| **Weather** | **Little effect on mood** for most people. The belief persists because of **illusory correlation** — associating two events with no real connection. Japanese and US data found people *more* productive on bad-weather days, but because the weather removed distractions, not because of mood |
| **Stress** | Stressful events worsen mood. "A constant diet of even low-level stressful events has the potential to cause workers to experience gradually increasing levels of strain over time." Under heavy emotional charge, the natural response is to disengage — literally to look away |
| **Social interactions** | Interactions shape emotions, and emotions then strengthen or dissolve the relationship. Negative interactions at work **spill over** into family relationships. Lukewarm interactions produce boredom; "emotional roller coaster" relationships produce bittersweet, conflicted feelings |
| **Sleep** | ~62% of adults across twelve countries report not sleeping well or long enough, and sleep length and quality have declined. Poor sleep impairs mood, decision making and emotional control, and raises risk of disease, injury and depression. One estimate: for the under-slept, **a one-hour increase in long-run average sleep raises wages by 16%** — more than a year of schooling |
| **Exercise** | "Sweat therapy" works — exercise reliably enhances positive mood, reduces perceived fatigue, protects against persistent negative moods and improves recovery from negative experiences |
| **Gender identity** | Differences in emotional *display* exist but are **trivial or small**, and likely reflect upbringing, stereotype and measurement rather than real difference. The stereotypes persist anyway: a Denver police commander filed a civil rights complaint after being called "emotional" and "immature" and denied promotion. And people attribute women's emotional expressions to **disposition** ("she has an emotional personality") while attributing men's to **situation** ("he had a bad day") |

## Emotional labor

**Emotional labor** — an employee's expression of organizationally desired emotions during interpersonal transactions at work. It is a key component of effective job performance: we expect flight attendants cheerful, funeral directors sad, doctors emotionally neutral.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Felt emotions** | An individual's actual emotions |
| **Displayed emotions** | Emotions that are organizationally required and considered appropriate in a given job |
| **Surface acting** | Hiding one's feelings and forgoing emotional expression in response to display rules |
| **Deep acting** | Trying to modify one's true feelings based on display rules |
| **Emotional dissonance** | Inconsistency between the emotions people feel and those they project |

Surface acting operates on **displayed** emotions; deep acting operates on **felt** emotions.

| | Surface acting | Deep acting |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcomes** | Increased stress, decreased job satisfaction. Daily surface acting → emotional exhaustion at home, work–family conflict, absenteeism, insomnia | Positive relationship with job satisfaction (especially when work is challenging), job performance, better customer treatment and **higher tips** |

> [!warning] Display rules can be biased and unequally costly
> **Moderate display rules work best.** Employees under very high *or* very low display rules perform worse in sales than those with moderate rules and high discretion.
>
> **Introverts pay more.** Emotional labor is generally more taxing for introverts, and surface acting relates more strongly to their exhaustion and negative affect.
>
> **Stereotype adds an unpaid tax.** Black grocery and service workers were perceived as less warm than White workers, so they had to perform **more** emotional labor to be viewed favourably — held to a higher customer-service standard because of a stereotype.

Long-term emotional dissonance predicts **burnout**, declines in performance, lower satisfaction, and more serious harms including alcohol abuse and lashing out at coworkers. Buffers: high EI, high self-control, good sleep, strong customer relationships, being empowered to help others. Amplifiers: fear of isolation and a strong desire to please and belong.

## Affective events theory (AET)

**AET** proposes that employees react emotionally to things that happen at work, and those reactions influence job performance and satisfaction.

$$\text{Work event} \;\rightarrow\; \text{emotional reaction} \;\rightarrow\; \text{attitudes and behaviour}$$

Personality and mood **predispose** people to respond with greater or lesser intensity. Not all events are equally frequent: interpersonal mistreatment by customers accounted for nearly **50% of negative affective work events** for part-time workers. For remote workers, a productive Zoom call is a positive affective event and an unfixable connection problem a negative one.

> [!tip] AET's two messages
> ① Emotions provide **valuable insight** into how workplace events influence performance and satisfaction.
> ② Managers should **not ignore minor events**, because emotional reactions **accumulate**.

## Emotional intelligence (EI)

**EI** is the ability to detect and manage emotional cues and information — a cascade of three abilities:

1. **Perceive** emotions in oneself and others
2. **Understand** the meaning of these emotions
3. **Regulate** one's own emotions accordingly

Employers value it. In a CareerBuilder survey of 2,500+ hiring managers and HR professionals, **59% would not hire someone with high cognitive ability but low EI**, and **75% would rather promote a high-EI candidate over a high-IQ one**. A study tracking students from graduation to 10+ years into work found EI predicted salary at ten years and whether they had a mentor.

Findings: EI relates to job attitudes, academic and job performance, teamwork effectiveness, OCB and career decision making, and to reduced CWB. South Korean managers high in EI had better sales through more cohesive stores. Across eleven US presidents evaluated on six qualities, **EI was the quality that separated the successful from the unsuccessful**. And it is **EI, not narcissism**, that predicts long-term popularity.

> [!warning] The case against over-trusting EI
> - **Measurement is contested** — no commonly accepted test (MSCEIT, TEIQue, SJT of EI, ECI-2), and EI tests may need to be **culturally specific** since emotional display varies by culture.
> - **Construct overlap** — its survey items are strikingly similar to personality, intelligence and self-perception measures, so it may not be measuring something new.
> - **Costly and limited** — Dutch research found self-focused EI reduced stress but at **physiological cost**, while other-focused EI only weakly explained task performance.
> - **It may help people fake** — high-EI candidates understand what a test wants, so they may be **better at faking personality tests** used to screen them.
> - **Applicant reactions** — low scorers often see the tests as unfair and view the hiring organization unfavourably.
>
> **Can EI be learned?** Reviews of dozens of studies say yes, it can be trained as a skill — but, paradoxically, **the people who need it most are the least likely to seek it out**.

## Emotion regulation

**Emotion regulation** — the process of identifying and modifying felt emotions. It predicts task performance in some jobs and predicts OCB.

**Who regulates, and when.** People high in **neuroticism** have more trouble; people with **low self-esteem** are less likely to try to lift a sad mood, perhaps feeling they do not deserve a good one. **Diversity increases regulation** — younger employees regulate more around older colleagues, and underrepresented employees regulate to fit in. This is offered as a *beneficial* by-product of diversity: it makes regulation more conscious and effective.

### The techniques

| Technique | What it is | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Emotional suppression** | Suppressing the initial emotional response | Facilitates practical thinking short-term but is **generally ineffective** versus expressing. Genuinely useful only in a **crisis** — a portfolio manager suppressing panic at a sudden price drop. As an everyday strategy it damages mental and emotional ability, health and relationships |
| **Cognitive reappraisal** | Reframing your outlook on an emotional situation | The most effective general technique, **especially where you cannot control the stressor**. Reframing job loss as a chance at a dreamed-of career. Israeli participants primed to reappraise anger-inducing information about the Israeli–Palestinian conflict showed more inclination toward conciliation and less toward aggression — **up to five months later** |
| **Social sharing (venting)** | Openly expressing rather than bottling up | Can reduce anger — but **depends entirely on the listener**. No response leaves the venter feeling *worse*; support or validation leaves them feeling better. Venting **to the perceived offender** rarely improves things and usually heightens negative emotion |

Two cautions on reappraisal: it can serve unethical aims — reappraising the shame and guilt you would anticipate for a CWB can make you go through with it — and it can produce **mental fatigue**.

Under abusive supervision, employees using **suppression** suffered greater emotional exhaustion and work withdrawal than those using **reappraisal**.

> [!note] The ethics of regulating
> One view: controlling your emotions is unethical because it requires acting. The opposite view: all emotion should be controlled to stay dispassionate. The useful question is **why** — "am I regulating so I do not react inappropriately, or so nobody knows what I am thinking?" And "fake it till you make it" has some support: participants asked to *act happy* with a Starbucks barista later reported being in **much better moods** than those asked only to be efficient.

## OB applications

| Domain | What emotions do |
|---|---|
| **Selection** | Consider EI for socially demanding jobs — US Air Force recruiters selected on EI were **2.6× more successful**. Note also that applicants' emotional displays are used in hiring decisions, knowingly or not, and that charismatic leaders transfer positive emotion to followers by **contagion** — so choosing positive team members seeds a positive environment |
| **Decision making** | Positive emotions help produce sound decisions and enhance problem solving. Negative moods are contested: sad people may decide as before, angry people make **stronger (not necessarily better)** choices, negative moods may increase originality — and may increase **risk-taking** |
| **Creativity** | Two schools. Mostly, good moods → more ideas, more options, more flexible and open thinking. But some argue positive mood breeds relaxation ("things must be fine, no need for new ideas") and that worriers do better on creative tasks. The reconciliation: **activating** moods (anger, fear, elation) increase creativity; **deactivating** moods (sorrow, depression, serenity) reduce it. One study even found students performed best on creative problem-solving when **fatigued** |
| **Motivation** | A group shown a funny clip before word puzzles expected to do better, worked harder, and **solved more**. Insurance agents in good moods were more helpful and performed better. Feedback shifts mood, mood shifts motivation, and a **virtuous spiral** can form: good mood → creativity → positive feedback → better mood |
| **Leadership** | Inspirational goals generate optimism, cooperation and enthusiasm. Transformational leaders inspire positive emotions that raise task performance. **Leader sadness increased followers' analytic performance** (they attended more closely to help). Across 300+ basketball halftime speeches, sharing negative emotion could improve performance — but **only at the right intensity**; too intense and it backfires. **Leader anger** paradoxically raises effort while producing unethical retaliation, silence and exit |
| **Negotiation** | Anger can work — the opponent may conclude you have conceded all you can, or may try to diagnose why you are angry and offer joint concessions. But use it selectively: **angry negotiators with less information or less power do significantly worse**, as do those who refuse to take the opponent's perspective. Feeling angry, regretful or proud about your performance **impairs future negotiations** |
| **Customer service** | **Emotional contagion** — the "catching" of emotions from others — creates a matching effect. Customers who catch employees' positive moods (if believed **authentic**) become more satisfied and loyal, trust more and shop longer. In the other direction, an employee treated unfairly by a customer struggles to display the required positive emotion, producing emotional dissonance |
| **Work–life conflict** | A good day at work means a better mood at home, and vice versa — but the spillover usually **clears by the next day**. The bad news runs both ways: one partner's workday negative mood spills over to the spouse at night |
| **Unethical behaviour** | Negative emotions predict deviance. **Envy** — resenting someone for something you strongly desire — leads to undermining colleagues and stealing credit. **Anger** correlated with aggressive CWB (abuse of others, production deviance) while **sadness did not**; neither predicted withdrawal, meaning angry employees may **stay and keep behaving aggressively**. CWB can also produce a negative emotional spiral in the perpetrator |
| **Safety and injury** | Bad moods contribute to injury: anxiety impairs hazard coping, pessimism undercuts belief in safety precautions, fear can cause freezing, and negative mood increases distractibility. Homesickness among migrant workers and expatriate military personnel predicted both performance and safety behaviour |

> [!warning] A privacy limit on all of this
> Many companies now track employees' emotions through surveys and by analyzing daily email and chat data. **Many employees experience this as an invasion of privacy** — the same tension raised by big data in [[ch01-what-is-organizational-behavior|Ch 1]].

## Key takeaways

- **Affect** is the umbrella; **emotions** are short, intense and event-caused; **moods** are longer, milder and diffuse. Emotions are never neutral, and most people carry a **positivity offset**.
- Emotions do not make thinking irrational — they supply information. The managerial goal is **not** an emotion-free workplace.
- Feelings come from **personality (affect intensity), time of day, day of week, stress, social interaction, sleep and exercise** — and largely *not* from the weather (**illusory correlation**).
- **Emotional labor** costs: **surface acting** damages (stress, dissatisfaction, exhaustion, insomnia); **deep acting** helps (satisfaction, performance, tips). Sustained **emotional dissonance** → burnout.
- **AET**: work events → emotional reactions → attitudes and performance. Minor events matter because they **accumulate**.
- **EI** (perceive → understand → regulate) predicts real outcomes and is trainable, but its measurement and distinctiveness are genuinely contested.
- In regulation, **cognitive reappraisal beats suppression**; venting works only when the listener responds supportively.
- **Emotional contagion** makes leaders' and service employees' moods a business variable — but authenticity is the condition.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Affect** | The broad range of feelings people experience, covering emotions and moods |
| **Emotions / moods** | Intense, discrete, short-lived, event-caused / longer-lived, less intense, no clear stimulus |
| **Positive affect / negative affect** | Dimensions with excitement and enthusiasm / nervousness and anxiety at the high end |
| **Moral emotions** | Emotions with moral implications arising from instant judgment of the evoking situation |
| **Positivity offset** | Most people's tendency toward a mildly positive mood at zero input |
| **Affect intensity** | Individual differences in the strength with which emotions are experienced |
| **Illusory correlation** | Associating two events that in reality have no connection |
| **Emotional labor** | Expression of organizationally desired emotions during workplace interactions |
| **Felt / displayed emotions** | Actual emotions / those organizationally required and appropriate |
| **Surface acting / deep acting** | Hiding feelings to satisfy display rules / modifying true feelings to satisfy them |
| **Emotional dissonance** | Inconsistency between felt and projected emotions |
| **Affective events theory (AET)** | Workplace events cause emotional reactions that influence attitudes and behaviours |
| **Emotional intelligence (EI)** | The ability to detect and manage emotional cues and information |
| **Emotion regulation** | The process of identifying and modifying felt emotions |
| **Emotional suppression / cognitive reappraisal / social sharing** | Blocking the response / reframing the situation / venting to someone |
| **Emotional contagion** | The process by which people's emotions are caused by others' emotions |
