# Chapter 10 — Understanding Work Teams

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch09-foundations-of-group-behavior|Ch 9]] · Next: [[ch11-communication|Ch 11 — Communication]]

---

## Core summary

Teams can achieve what individuals cannot, and they are **flexible and responsive** — quick to assemble, deploy, refocus and disband. But:

> [!important] The warning that runs through the chapter
> Extensive use of teams creates the **potential** for greater output with no increase in head count. *Potential.* **There is nothing magical that ensures positive synergy, and merely calling a group a team does not improve its performance.**

The chapter's four moves: distinguish **groups from teams** → catalogue **five team arrangements** → build the **team effectiveness model** (context, composition, processes/states) → explain how organizations **select, train and reward** for teamwork, and when **not to use teams at all**.

---

## Groups versus teams

| | **Work group** | **Work team** |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Share information | **Collective performance** |
| **Synergy** | Neutral, sometimes negative | **Positive** |
| **Accountability** | Individual | **Individual and mutual** |
| **Skills** | Random and varied | **Complementary** |

A **work group** interacts primarily to share information and make decisions helping each member perform in their own area of responsibility. There is no joint effort, so **performance is the summation of individual contributions**.

A **work team** generates positive synergy through coordination, so individual efforts produce performance **greater than the sum of the inputs**. Teams are constantly changing and adapting rather than static — which is why many scholars now focus on **"teaming" as a verb**, on the processes of engaging as a team rather than on the team as an entity.

## Five team arrangements

| Type | What it is | Evidence and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Problem-solving team** | Employees from the same department meeting a few hours a week to discuss improving quality, efficiency and the work environment | Long-standing (quality-control teams), now spread to fields like medicine. **Rarely have authority to implement** their own suggestions — real improvement requires pairing recommendations with implementation processes |
| **Self-managed work team** | Employees with highly related or interdependent jobs who **autonomously implement solutions and own the outcomes**, taking on supervisory responsibilities | Findings are **not uniformly positive**. Effectiveness depends on rewarding team-promoting behaviour. Conflict is corrosive — members stop cooperating and power struggles follow — **unless psychological safety exists**, in which case conflict can boost performance. Members report higher satisfaction but sometimes **higher absenteeism and turnover**, and one large study found **no evidence** they outperform traditional teams. Without controlled supervision, ineffective leaders may **"over-emerge"** |
| **Cross-functional team** | Employees from about the same hierarchical level but **different work areas**, coming together for a task | Powerful for exchanging information, developing ideas and coordinating complex projects — but hard to form and manage. Four difficulties: **leadership ambiguity** (everyone is peer-level, so trust must precede leadership emergence) · long early development while members learn to handle diversity and complexity · time to build trust across different experiences · **flat structures derive the least benefit**, since power dispersion lengthens conflict resolution and coordination processes already exist. One study of ~100 cross-functional teams across 25 major corporations found a **success rate of only 25%** |
| **Virtual team** | Uses technology to unite physically dispersed members toward a common goal | Manage differently: virtual environments convey **fewer social cues** and create psychological distance. Virtual teams may share more unique information but be **less open to sharing** it. **Trust is critical** — one inflammatory email can undermine it. Three musts: establish trust · monitor progress closely so no member "disappears" · publicize the team's work so it does not become invisible. Also **select for virtual competencies**, which differ |
| **Multiteam system** | Two or more **interdependent teams sharing a superordinate goal** — a "team of teams" | NASA's Mars mission: researchers, scientists, engineers, operations, ground crews and psychologists, technically independent but interdependent in practice. What helps small teams can **hinder** here. **Boundary spanners** who coordinate with all constituents reduce communication and coordination demands; **boundary spoilers** communicate inaccurate perspectives and damage coordination. Identification is double-edged — strong own-team identification breeds intersystem conflict, but identifying with the whole system creates **uncertainty about which norms and role expectations apply**, depleting members. Leadership must **both coordinate between teams and lead them** |

## Creating effective teams

**Team effectiveness** in this model means objective productivity, managers' ratings of performance, and aggregate member satisfaction.

> [!note] Teams sometimes evolve organically — and that is a risk
> In Noam Wasserman's research on 10,000+ tech startups, nearly **40% of founders were friends before going into business** — and being friends **increases the likelihood of failure**, with each additional friend on the founding team raising the probability of founders leaving by nearly 30%.

### Context — five factors

| Factor | What matters |
|---|---|
| **Adequate resources** | Every team depends on outside resources. After examining thirteen factors, one study concluded "perhaps one of the most important characteristics of an effective work group is **the support the group receives from the organization**" — timely information, proper equipment, adequate staffing, encouragement, administrative assistance |
| **Leadership and structure** | Teams cannot function if they disagree on who does what or fail to share the workload. Well-performing teams have **transformational and empowering** leaders. Notably, the **leader–member relationship matters more** for job attitudes, performance and turnover intentions than relationships *between* members — and leaders who **play favourites** damage group processes |
| **Culture and climate** | Teams have their own. The most important climate factors: a **shared sense of vision**, ability to share and collaborate **non-threateningly**, concern for performance quality, encouragement of creative solutions, and **regular frequent interaction**. Strong team climates raise financial performance and innovation performance. **Justice climate** — fair policies, practices and procedures — strongly affects team attitudes, conflict and performance |
| **Performance evaluation and reward system** | Purely individual evaluation interferes with high-performance teams. Use **hybrid systems** — individual rewards for individual contributions *plus* group rewards for team outcomes: group-based appraisals, profit sharing, small-group incentives. Watch for bias: teams composed primarily of **Black employees tend to be stigmatized more and paid less** than those composed of White employees |
| **Crises and extreme contexts** | A crucible that either unlocks the team's potential or unravels it |

> [!warning] Mann Gulch, 1949
> Foreman "Wag" Dodge saw a wildfire advancing at **610 feet per minute with 30-foot flames**. His crew turned uphill on a 76% grade and was losing ground. Dodge told everyone to **drop their tools**, lit an escape fire, and told them to lie in the burned area. **No one listened** — they all ran for the ridge. Dodge survived by lying in the ashes.
>
> The research pattern: in disasters, multiteam systems **default to gathering information rather than acting**, and when they do act, they do so **without considering context, options, or forming a plan**. What helps: leaders who support problem solving, initiate structure and planning, delegate, coordinate and **help members make sense of the situation**; **team scaffolds** (fluid underlying structures establishing role types, shared responsibilities and boundaries) that create accountability, shared understanding and belonging; **adaptability** — high performers show few, short, simple interaction patterns; and **positive affectivity**, which buffers the strain.

### Composition — seven variables

**Abilities of members.** Performance depends partly on members' knowledge, skills and abilities — abilities set limits on what members can do. The team-relevant ability clusters: **conflict resolution, collaborative problem solving, communication, goal setting and planning**. **Complementary backgrounds** (education, expertise area, abilities) relate weakly to performance but more strongly to **innovation and creativity**. And experience compounds — members improve problem solving as they learn to work together.

**Personality of members.** Big Five effects at team level:

| Trait | Team effect |
|---|---|
| **Conscientiousness** | **Especially important** — conscientious people back up teammates and sense when support is truly needed |
| **Agreeableness** | More agreeable teams perform better. **Disagreeable members** make teams worse and less cohesive, and a **wide span** in agreeableness lowers productivity |
| **Openness** | Open members share more ideas → **more creative and innovative** teams |
| **Emotional stability** | Teams facing **task conflict** perform better when composed of emotionally stable members — not because conflict helps, but because they can **handle and leverage** it |
| **Extroversion** | Unclear, but a high team mean can increase **helping behaviours**, especially in a cooperative climate |
| **Proactive personality** | Important for **team innovation** |

**Allocation of roles.** Across 778 major league baseball teams over 21 years, teams with more experienced and skilled members performed better — but the experience and skill of those in **core roles** handling more of the workflow (pitchers and catchers) was **especially vital**.

Thirteen team roles exist, of which nine are constructive — **leader, motivator, coordinator, shepherd, evaluator, problem-solver, socializer, cooperator, producer** — and **four are generally negative**: the **dominator** (domineers, aggravates, defies), **critic** (antagonizes and criticizes), **shirker** (avoids responsibility, seeks empathy) and **detractor** (withdraws, complains, downplays). Most people play multiple roles. Managers should understand each person's strengths, select with those in mind, and allocate assignments to fit — **especially hard for founding entrepreneurs**, whose teams often share similar characteristics and strengths.

**Diversity of members.** **Demography** is the degree to which members share a common demographic attribute — age, gender identity, race, educational level, organizational tenure. The logic is that dissimilar experience makes communication harder, raising conflict, and conflict losers leave or are pushed out. But the premature conclusion is wrong: as in [[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]], diversity produces **negative outcomes initially and positive ones over time**. Teams that stay together reach desirable performance, are **less likely to conform**, share more information, make fewer errors, and may be more creative.

**Cultural differences** interfere with team processes **in the short term** — culturally heterogeneous teams have more difficulty learning to work together and solving problems, though the difficulty dissipates over time. A finer finding: cultural differences **hurt performance for cultural majority members and help it for cultural minority members** — as teams diversify, minority members benefit more.

**Team size.** Keep it small. Jeff Bezos's **"two-pizza rule"**: *"If it takes more than two pizzas to feed the team, the team is too big."* George Miller's "magical number seven, plus or minus two." Potential connections grow **exponentially** with size, complicating communication and reducing perceived support. Managers systematically err toward **too large**, believing more people means more benefit — while underestimating the hours required and the losses from adding people. With excess members, **cohesiveness and mutual accountability decline, social loafing increases, and people communicate less**. Use the smallest number who can do the task; for a large natural working unit, **break it into subteams**.

**Member preferences.** Not everyone works well in teams, and given the option many will opt out. Forcing people who prefer to work alone into a team is a **direct threat to team morale and individual satisfaction**. High-performing teams are composed of people who **prefer** group work.

### Processes and states

$$\text{Actual team effectiveness} = \text{Potential team effectiveness} + \text{Process gains} - \text{Process losses}$$

**Common plan and purpose.** Effective teams analyze their mission, develop goals, and create strategies for reaching them. The common purpose, once accepted, "becomes what a GPS is to a ship captain." Teams should agree on whether the purpose is to **learn and master** the task or simply to **perform** it — differing perspectives on learning versus performance **lower overall performance**.

**Reflexivity** — reflecting on and adjusting the master plan when necessary. It improves psychological well-being in manufacturing teams, helps startup teams learn from setbacks, helps R&D teams innovate, and improves performance in extreme environments like emergency rooms. It matters **most for teams that have performed poorly in the past**.

**Mental models** — team members' **shared knowledge about the key elements in their task environment**. Teams with shared mental models interact more frequently, are more motivated, have more positive attitudes and higher objectively rated performance. Wrong mental models — particularly likely **under acute stress** — cause frequent misunderstanding, overlooked task aspects, and argument about methods instead of work.

**Transactive memory systems** — how members collect, integrate, generate and distribute knowledge, so the team knows **"who knows what and is best at what."** Critical in collectivist and high-power-distance cultures, for top management teams, and in volatile environments. Without them, **the turnover of one critical member is devastating**.

> [!tip] The anesthetic team finding
> Swiss anesthetic teams used two message types: **vocally monitoring** each other's performance (keeping a spoken record, not criticizing) and **"talking to the room"** ("Patient's blood pressure is dropping"). High- and low-performing teams used both **equally often** — what separated them was the **sequencing**: high performers followed monitoring dialogue with **assistance and instructions**, and talking-to-the-room with **further team dialogue**.
>
> The lesson: **communicate about what is happening while the team is in operation.**

**Team conflict** — three types beyond role conflict:

| Type | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Relationship conflict** — interpersonal incompatibility, tension, animosity | **Almost always dysfunctional**, especially among friends |
| **Task conflict** — disagreement about task content | On **nonroutine** activities it stimulates discussion, promotes critical assessment and can improve decisions |
| **Ethical conflict** — disagreement about ethical issues | e.g. the "right" way to handle a customer's coupon dispute |

Task conflict helps when members are **open to experience and emotionally stable**. **Moderate** levels early in a creative task raise team creativity; **both very low and very high** levels hurt performance. Resolution style separates effective from ineffective teams: effective teams **explicitly discuss the task disagreements**, while ineffective ones leave conflicts unresolved and focused on **personalities and the way things were said**. Teams hit by early relationship conflict can rebound and become more cohesive through **cognitive reappraisal** ([[ch04-emotions-and-moods|Ch 4]]).

**Social loafing** — effective teams undermine it by making members **individually and jointly accountable** for the team's purpose, goals and approach. Members must be clear on what they are individually and jointly responsible for.

**Motivation.** Translate the common purpose into **specific, measurable, difficult but realistic** performance goals aligned with team objectives. Goals for quantity raise quantity; goals for accuracy raise accuracy. Critically, goals aimed at improving **individual member performance rather than team performance tend to undermine group performance** — individual goals must link to the group's actual goals.

**Team efficacy** — a team's collective belief that they can succeed. Success raises efficacy, which motivates harder work. It strongly predicts performance, **especially where members are interdependent**. Two ways to build it: help the team achieve **small successes** that build confidence, and **train** technical and interpersonal skills.

**Team identity** — a member's affinity for and sense of belonging to the team. Dutch soldiers who felt **included and respected** became more willing to work hard for their teams. Where team identity is strong, performance-motivated members direct effort toward **team** rather than individual goals. Build it by recognizing individuals' specific skills and creating a climate of **respect and inclusion** — and pay special attention in **virtual teams**, where identity and therefore effort tend to be lower.

> [!warning] Team identity without organizational identity
> Individuals with strong team identity but weak organizational identity become **fixed to their teams and unwilling to coordinate with other teams**. Positive organizational identification tends to *produce* positive team identification — so the two should be built together.

**Team cohesion** — the shared bond driving members to work together and stay. It runs both ways: cohesive teams perform better, **and better-performing teams become more cohesive**. Astronaut analogue studies in isolated, confined, extreme environments find cohesion **breaks down several months into missions**. Builders: shared leadership responsibilities, open information sharing, interdependence.

**Team trust** — a mutual, positive state of positive expectations between members: believing in their reliability and dependability and genuinely caring about their welfare. It evolves as members share, put in effort and monitor one another. It has a **sizeable effect on performance, especially in virtual teams**, and among professional firefighting teams it **buffered the effect of job demands on stress and strain**. Broken trust **can be repaired**, and leadership is key to both building and repairing it.

## Turning groups of employees into teams

| Lever | Practice |
|---|---|
| **Selecting** | Hire for **team roles as well as technical requirements**. Place people in teams where they will work well with the specific others. **Cluster hiring** — selecting an already-existing team for a new role — skips the growing pains but is a significant investment and risk. For virtual teams, select for leadership, decision-making, interpersonal and analytical skills |
| **Training** | Team training reliably improves member attitudes, team processes and cognitive aspects such as **shared mental models** — and the findings generalize to critical industries like medical services. Prioritize training that builds **shared mental models and transactive memory systems**, including **virtual** ones now that people meet in person less. Training needs **evolve over time**; it is not a one-time activity |
| **Rewarding** | Rework reward systems to encourage **cooperation rather than competition**. Team rewards give a common goal and let every member be recognized. Team-based rewards raise performance **especially when distributed by each individual's level of contribution rather than equally**. Do not ignore intrinsic rewards — camaraderie, the excitement of a successful team, personal development |

> [!warning] Set the cooperative tone early
> Teams that **switch from competitive to cooperative** motivations do not immediately share information and make **rushed, poor-quality decisions**. Managers who create "friendly competitions" between teams to raise motivation should not be surprised when the climate turns **competitive and conflict-ridden**.

## Teams are not always the answer

Teamwork takes **more time and more resources** than individual work — more communication demands, more conflict to manage, more meetings. **The benefits must exceed the costs, and that is not always possible.** Employees over-loaded with team demands report problems with conflict while collaborating, frustration in planning, ensuring plans get executed, rapidly changing environments, leadership transitions and motivating others.

> [!important] The three tests
> ① **Can the work be done better by more than one person?** Indicators: complexity, and the need for different perspectives. Simple tasks not requiring diverse input are better left to individuals.
> ② **Does the work create a common purpose or set of goals that is more than the aggregate of individual goals?**
> ③ **Are the members interdependent** — does the success of the whole depend on each, and each on the others?
>
> Soccer passes all three. **Bowling teams are not really teams** — individuals performing individually, whose total is merely the sum of their scores.

## Key takeaways

- A **work team** produces positive synergy; a **work group** produces a sum. Teams differ on **goal, synergy, accountability and skills** — and naming a group a team changes nothing.
- Five arrangements: **problem-solving** (recommends only), **self-managed** (mixed evidence, needs psychological safety), **cross-functional** (25% success rate; leadership ambiguity is the core problem), **virtual** (trust, monitoring, visibility), **multiteam systems** (boundary spanners vs boundary spoilers).
- Effectiveness = **context + composition + processes/states**, and actual effectiveness is potential **plus process gains minus process losses**.
- **Small teams win** — the two-pizza rule; excess members bring loafing, less accountability and worse communication.
- Processes worth engineering: **shared mental models**, **transactive memory systems**, **reflexivity**, **task conflict at moderate levels**, **individual + joint accountability**.
- **Team goals must be team goals** — goals aimed at individual member performance undermine group performance.
- **Team identity needs organizational identity beside it**, or teams refuse to coordinate with other teams.
- Apply the **three tests** before forming a team at all.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Work group / work team** | Interacts to share information, performance is the sum / individual efforts produce more than the sum |
| **Problem-solving team** | Same-department employees meeting to discuss improving quality, efficiency and environment |
| **Self-managed work team** | Employees who autonomously implement solutions and take responsibility for outcomes |
| **Cross-functional team** | Same-level employees from different work areas working on a task |
| **Virtual team** | Uses technology to unite physically dispersed members toward a common goal |
| **Multiteam system** | Two or more interdependent teams sharing a superordinate goal |
| **Demography** | The degree to which work-unit members share a common demographic attribute |
| **Reflexivity** | Reflecting on and adjusting the master plan when necessary |
| **Mental model** | Team members' shared knowledge about the key elements in their task environment |
| **Transactive memory system** | How a team collects, integrates, generates and distributes knowledge — "who knows what" |
| **Relationship / task / ethical conflict** | Interpersonal incompatibility / disagreement about task content / disagreement about ethical issues |
| **Team efficacy** | A team's collective belief that it can succeed at its tasks |
| **Team identity** | A member's affinity for and sense of belonging to their team |
