> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- Motivation blends extrinsic rewards with intrinsic purpose; mismatch lowers satisfaction.  
- Expectancy Theory links effort → performance → reward, with “valence” aligning rewards to personal goals.  
- Real‑world decisions rarely meet rational‑model assumptions; bounded rationality (satisficing) dominates.  
- Selection, survivorship, and framing biases arise from incomplete or skewed data.  
- Case analyses (race team, Challenger) show how missing data and bias can flip the optimal choice.

## Motivation
- **Extrinsic motivators**: pay, benefits, status, recognition.  
- **Intrinsic motivators**: mastery, curiosity, sense of purpose.  
- Combining both yields higher commitment; removing meaning drops motivation sharply.  

> [!info] **Value‑fulfillment model** – satisfaction rises when job attributes match personal values; larger mismatches → lower satisfaction.  

| Source | Example | Typical Impact |
|--------|---------|----------------|
| Material | Salary, bonuses | Direct performance incentive |
| Social | Recognition, community | Boosts belonging, status |
| Meaning | Purposeful work | Sustains long‑term drive |

## Expectancy Theory
> [!info] **Three components**  
1. **Expectancy** – belief effort → better performance.  
2. **Instrumentality** – belief performance → reward.  
3. **Valence** – reward value aligns with personal goals.  

> [!warning] Perceived inequity (e.g., a colleague gets higher rewards) erodes motivation (fish‑monkey studies).

## Decision‑making Models
| Model | Assumptions | Strength | Weakness |
|-------|-------------|----------|----------|
| Rational | Complete info, unlimited computation, utility maximization | Ideal benchmark | Unrealistic; ignores cognitive limits |
| Bounded Rationality (Satisficing) | Limited info, time pressure, “good enough” | Energy‑efficient | Systematic biases, sub‑optimal outcomes |

- Constraints: incomplete data, unclear goals, changing environment, brain capacity, individual intelligence.  
- Benefits of satisficing: lower mental load.  
- Drawbacks: bias, framing effects.

## Cognitive Biases & Missing Data
- **Selection bias** – analyzing only pre‑selected cases (e.g., only races with gasket failures).  
- **Survivorship bias** – ignoring units that never “survive” (WWII aircraft, CEOs).  
- **Framing effect** – wording (“happy?” vs “unhappy?”) changes responses.  
- Incomplete datasets skew expected‑value calculations; include both failure and non‑failure observations for reliable inference.

## Race Team Decision Example
- **Financials**: $500 k guaranteed sponsor; potential $1 M contract if top‑5 finish; $20 k repair risk; entry cost $25 k if withdraw.  
- **Probabilities**: ~80 % top‑5 finish historically; colder temps (≈4 °C) raise gasket‑failure risk dramatically.  
- **Expected value** (including temperature‑adjusted failure data) → negative profit for racing; withdrawal yields net ≈ $57.5 k.  
- **Decision**: consensus to withdraw (risk‑averse) after accounting for missing temperature‑success data and bias from limited failure points.

> [!example] **Missing data impact** – original graph showed only 7 failure points out of 25 races; adding the 18 non‑failure points revealed a strong cold‑temperature failure trend, flipping the EV from modest gain to ≈ ‑45 loss.

## Challenger Disaster Lessons
- **Key facts**: Launch scheduled at ≈40 °F; O‑ring erosion risk rises sharply in cold. Engineers warned; management framed decision positively, ignored adverse data.  
- **Biases observed**: selection (only successful launches considered), framing (“safe enough”), time pressure, CYA documentation.  
- **Outcome**: launch proceeded, all crew lost – a stark illustration of bias‑driven decision failure.

> [!warning] **Framing vs. proof** – decision should be based on risk evidence, not on the absence of proof of failure.

## Practical Takeaways
- Always audit data for completeness before calculating expected values.  
- Explicitly state assumptions; test how alternative framings affect conclusions.  
- Use satisficing when information is costly, but guard against systematic biases.  
- In high‑stakes contexts, elevate dissenting voices and document all risk evidence.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Extrinsic motivator | External reward such as pay or status |
| Intrinsic motivator | Internal drive like mastery or purpose |
| Expectancy Theory | Model linking effort, performance, and reward valence |
| Bounded rationality | Decision making with limited information and cognitive capacity |
| Selection bias | Distortion from analyzing non‑random, pre‑selected samples |
| Survivorship bias | Ignoring failures that never appear in the data |
| Framing effect | Influence of wording on judgment and choice |
