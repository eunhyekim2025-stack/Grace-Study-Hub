# Chapter 3 — Job Attitudes

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch02-diversity-equity-inclusion|Ch 2]] · Next: [[ch04-emotions-and-moods|Ch 4 — Emotions and Moods]]

---

## Core summary

An **attitude** is an evaluative statement — favourable or unfavourable — about an object, person or event. It has three components (cognitive, affective, behavioural), and OB cares about it because the **behavioural component** is where turnover, effort and citizenship come from.

The chapter's five moves:

1. **Attitudes have an ABC structure**, and cognition and affect are intertwined rather than sequential.
2. **Attitudes generally predict behaviour** — but five moderators decide how strongly. Sometimes the arrow runs backwards, which is **cognitive dissonance**.
3. **OB narrows to a handful of job attitudes**: organizational identification, job satisfaction, job involvement, psychological empowerment, organizational commitment, POS and employee engagement — which turn out to be **highly correlated** with one another.
4. **Job satisfaction** is measured two ways, caused mainly by job conditions and personality (**not** pay beyond a comfortable level), and produces performance, OCB, customer satisfaction and life satisfaction.
5. **Dissatisfaction** shows up along the **EVLN** grid and as **counterproductive work behaviour**, absenteeism and turnover — and managers systematically overestimate how satisfied their people are.

> [!important] The one line to keep
> Job satisfaction is the **best single predictor** of employee behaviour among the job attitudes — and, notably, **high pay alone does not create it**.

## What an attitude is

**Attitudes** are judgments or evaluative statements — favourable or unfavourable — about objects, people or events.

| Component | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cognitive** | The opinion or belief segment | "My supervisor gave a promotion to someone who deserved it less. My supervisor is unfair." |
| **Affective** | The emotional or feeling segment | "I dislike my supervisor!" |
| **Behavioural** | An intention to behave a certain way | "I'm looking for other work; I've complained to anyone who would listen." |

> [!note] Cognition and affect are not sequential
> When you realise someone treated you unfairly, the feeling arrives almost simultaneously with the realisation. The three-part model is an analytic convenience, not a claim that belief comes first and feeling second.

In organizations the **behavioural component** is what matters. Whether an employee believes they are unattached to the firm and could do better elsewhere is what eventually decides whether they stay — so understanding how commitment forms and changes is the practical point of studying attitudes. Dutch research suggests the **cognitive component** best predicts who becomes committed (newcomers in onboarding) or uncommitted (people weighing better offers).

## Attitudes and behaviour

Research generally supports that **attitudes predict future behaviour**. Five characteristics strengthen the link:

1. **Importance** — attitudes reflecting fundamental values, self-interest, or identification with valued people and groups
2. **Correspondence to behaviour** — the more specifically the attitude matches the behaviour, the better it predicts
3. **Accessibility** — attitudes you express frequently are remembered and predict behaviour
4. **Absence of social pressure** — discrepancies appear where social pressure to behave a certain way is powerful, **as in most organizations**
5. **Direct experience** — the relationship is much stronger when the attitude concerns something you have personally experienced

### Cognitive dissonance — when behaviour drives attitude

Sometimes the arrow runs backwards: people change what they *say* so it does not contradict what they *do*.

**Cognitive dissonance** is any incompatibility between two or more attitudes, or between behaviour and attitudes. People seek consistency; inconsistency is uncomfortable, so they alter the attitude, alter the behaviour, or **build a rationalization**.

Example from the text: university faculty on strike found it difficult to accept their own union's recommendation to take the university's offer, and instead **sought out extra information** to justify the belief that the offer was unfair.

> [!tip] Three factors decide how hard you work to reduce dissonance
> ① **Importance** of the elements creating the dissonance ② the **control** you believe you have over them ③ the **rewards** attached — dissonance is less distressing when accompanied by something good, such as a larger-than-expected raise. You know texting while driving is unsafe, do it anyway, and convince yourself nothing will happen: low importance, high perceived control, small immediate reward.

## The major job attitudes

### Organizational identification

The extent to which employees define themselves by the **same characteristics that define their organization**. A review of hundreds of studies found it strongly predicts job attitude formation. One proposed mechanism borrows from AI: employees accumulate events (reduced pay → downsizing → cancelled bonuses) and "learn," like a model updating on incoming data, that the organization does not value paying people well — then form negative attitudes and **disidentify**.

> [!warning] Identification is not uniformly good
> It can lead employees to behave **unethically on the organization's behalf** (fudging numbers to make it look better), reduce performance and perpetuate conflict. In one sample of thousands of White male corporate executives, organizational identification **decreased following the appointment of a racial or gender minority CEO** — and those executives then provided less help to colleagues.

**In the Gig Economy**, you might expect no identification from short-term contractors. But if the work itself is personally fulfilling — providing autonomy and a chance to relate to others — gig workers **do** identify with their contracting organizations.

### Job satisfaction, job involvement, psychological empowerment

| Attitude | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Job satisfaction** | A positive feeling about one's job resulting from an evaluation of its characteristics |
| **Job involvement** | The degree to which a person identifies with a job, actively participates in it, and considers performance important to **self-worth** |
| **Psychological empowerment** | Employees' belief in the degree to which they affect their work environment, their **competence**, the **meaningfulness** of their job, and their **autonomy** |

Empowerment strongly predicts job attitudes and strain and moderately predicts performance behaviours. A meta-analysis of 43 studies and 15,000+ employees found it more predictive when **all four beliefs are considered together** (impact, competence, meaningfulness, self-determination) than separately — though **meaningfulness** carries substantial effect on attitudes and strain even after accounting for the others.

### Organizational commitment

Identifying with an organization and its goals and wishing to remain a member. The traditional three components:

| Component | What it is | Feels like |
|---|---|---|
| **Affective** | Emotional attachment to and involvement in the organization | "I *want* to stay" |
| **Normative** | The sense of obligation felt toward the organization | "I *ought* to stay" |
| **Continuance** | Weighing the costs of leaving | "I *have* to stay" |

Emotional attachment and belief in the organization's values are the **gold standard**. Committed employees are less likely to withdraw even when dissatisfied, and **affective** commitment matters most for outcomes beyond turnover — attendance, performance, OCB.

A crisis illustrates how the components diverge: during a recession, job insecurity **lowers affective commitment** while making the job market hostile, which **raises continuance commitment**. People stay while caring less. Some scholars criticise the model on exactly this ground — normative and continuance commitment look more like attitudes toward *turnover* than toward the *organization*.

### Perceived organizational support (POS)

The degree to which employees believe the organization **values their contribution and cares about their well-being**. It builds when people are treated fairly, have a high-quality relationship with the organization, and see its practices as supportive, developmental and fair.

The illustration: a Salesforce R&D engineer diagnosed with leukemia had his medical expenses covered by the CEO and 350 colleagues, who stayed in touch throughout his recovery.

> [!note] POS is culturally conditioned
> POS matters more in **low power distance** countries, where people view work as an **exchange** rather than a moral obligation and therefore look for reasons to feel supported. In high-power-distance countries such as China, POS perceptions rest less on demonstrations of fairness and encouragement. US POS has been rising steadily across a 30-year study of hundreds of thousands of employees.

### Employee engagement

The degree of **enthusiasm** an employee feels for the job — roughly a combination of satisfaction and commitment, but exceeding both: "devotion," giving your "heart and soul." Disengaged employees have checked out, putting in time but not energy or attention.

Findings: a study of ~8,000 business units across 36 companies found high-engagement teams achieved **higher customer satisfaction, higher productivity, higher profits, lower turnover and fewer accidents**. Molson Coors found engaged employees five times less likely to have a safety incident, and incidents that did occur cost **\$63 versus \$392**. Caterpillar recorded an 80% drop in grievances and a 34% rise in highly satisfied customers. Disengagement is estimated to cost organizations up to **\$550 billion annually** in lost productivity.

Engagement can be raised: interventions that increase **autonomy and resilience**, reduce or ease job demands, or contribute to development (feedback, learning). Organizational levers include training, role clarity, autonomy and supervisor support.

> [!warning] The construct-validity problem
> Engagement has been used to mean psychological states, personality traits and behaviours at different times — one reviewer called it "an umbrella term for whatever one wants it to be." Its survey items resemble those measuring satisfaction, commitment and involvement, and meta-analytic correlations with job attitudes are extremely strong. It predicts important outcomes; whether it is genuinely **distinct** is unsettled.

### Are these attitudes distinct at all?

They are reliably differentiable — but heavily overlapping. If work is central to your being (job involvement) and you identify strongly with your employer (identification), you probably also like it (satisfaction); if you think the organization is supportive (POS), you are probably committed to it. Part of the overlap is the employee's own personality.

> [!tip] The practical shortcut
> **If you know someone's job satisfaction, you know most of what you need to know about how they see the organization.**

### Job attitudes in the Gig Economy

Gig workers' job satisfaction is **only slightly lower** than permanent workers' according to a meta-analysis of hundreds of thousands of workers — but **type matters**. Temporary *agency* workers show substantial decrements in satisfaction relative to other workers including other gig workers, and are less committed to their organization, occupation and form of employment than permanent or self-employed people.

What raises gig-worker attitudes: personally fulfilling work with socioemotional relationships to clients · perceived **stability** of employment and confidence they could find work elsewhere · **POS from the agency** (critical for training success, client-rated performance and turnover) · and perceptions of **fairness** — that organizations fulfil their obligations and do not treat them as lesser in status.

## Job satisfaction in depth

### Measuring it

| Method | How | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Single global rating** | One question — "All things considered, how satisfied are you with your job?" on a 1–5 scale | Fast, not time-consuming |
| **Summation of job facets** | Rate key elements — type of work, skills needed, supervision, pay, promotion opportunities, culture, coworker relationships — then add | Helps managers **locate the problem** and act faster |

Intuitively the facet method should be more accurate. Research **does not support that**: this is a rare case where simplicity works as well as complexity, and the two are essentially equally valid. Choose by purpose, not by precision.

### How satisfied are people?

US satisfaction was consistently high 1972–2006, then economic conditions bit: the Great Recession drove it down to a low of **42.6% in 2010**, recovering to about **51% in 2017** — still far below the 1987 level of 61.1%. By facet, people are typically more satisfied with **the work itself, supervisors and coworkers** than with **pay and promotion opportunities**.

### What causes it

**Job conditions.** Interesting jobs providing training, variety, independence and control satisfy most employees. Interdependence, feedback, social support and interaction with coworkers outside work are also strongly related — even after accounting for the work's characteristics. Toxic environments do the reverse; experiencing workplace racial discrimination produces dissatisfaction and may even bring declines in physical and psychological health.

> [!note] Fit is culturally patterned
> Across ~70,000 employees in 23 countries, leader–member exchange quality relates more strongly to satisfaction in **individualistic** cultures than collectivistic ones. And across 100+ studies, fitting in with your **organization and job** matters more in North America, while fitting in with your **team or supervisor** matters more in East Asia.

**Personality and individual differences.** People with positive **core self-evaluations (CSEs)** — believing in their inner worth and basic competence — are more satisfied; the effect can be especially strong in collectivist cultures. Intelligent people tend to be more satisfied, largely because they seek complex jobs that satisfy intellectual curiosity. And a meta-analysis spanning 65 years shows people are more satisfied when their **interests match their job**.

**Pay — much weaker than expected.** Income does correlate with satisfaction and happiness, and people are dissatisfied by discrepancies with peers. But the effect of both level and discrepancy shrinks once someone reaches a **comfortable standard of living**, and plotting average pay per job against average satisfaction per job shows **little relationship**.

> [!important] The distinction worth memorising
> **What motivates us is not necessarily the same as what makes us happy.** Money motivates ([[ch08-motivation-applications|Ch 8]]); it does not reliably satisfy.

### Outcomes of job satisfaction

| Outcome | The evidence |
|---|---|
| **Job performance** | Once thought a myth; a review of **300 studies** shows the correlation is robust. Satisfied individuals perform better, and organizations with more satisfied employees are more effective |
| **OCB** | Moderately correlated. The mechanism is partly **trust** — across 18 countries, managers reciprocate employees' OCB with trusting behaviours of their own; and people who feel supported by coworkers help more |
| **Customer satisfaction** | Yes, for employees with regular customer contact. Zappos offers a **\$2,000 payment to quit after training** so the least satisfied self-select out; 75% of its 24m+ customers are repeat buyers |
| **Life satisfaction** | Job and life satisfaction are more related than they look. German research shows life satisfaction falls on becoming unemployed **not just because of lost income** — work is where many people derive meaning |

## The impact of job dissatisfaction

### The EVLN framework

Four responses along two dimensions — **constructive/destructive** and **active/passive**:

| | **Constructive** | **Destructive** |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** | **VOICE** — actively and constructively trying to improve conditions: suggesting improvements, discussing problems with superiors, union activity | **EXIT** — directing behaviour toward leaving: job-hunting, resigning |
| **Passive** | **LOYALTY** — passively but optimistically waiting for improvement; speaking up for the organization against outside criticism; trusting management to do the right thing | **NEGLECT** — passively letting conditions worsen: chronic lateness or absence, reduced effort, higher error rate |

Exit and neglect map onto productivity, absenteeism and turnover. The model's value is adding **voice and loyalty** — constructive responses that let people tolerate or improve an unpleasant situation instead of leaving. Union grievance procedures are voice institutionalized: members act to improve the situation while continuing in their jobs.

### Counterproductive work behaviour (CWB)

Actions that actively damage the organization — stealing, aggression toward coworkers, substance abuse, endless scrolling on the clock, gossip, lateness, absence. Also called **deviant behaviour** or **employee withdrawal**.

Job dissatisfaction generally predicts CWB: dissatisfaction → frustration → lower performance and higher CWB. Contributing factors: the relationship may be stronger for men; **workplace norms** nudge behaviour (German research finds people in high-absenteeism teams are themselves more likely to be absent); and CWB can be a response to **abusive supervision**, which then increases the abuse — a vicious cycle.

> [!warning] Why policing individual behaviours fails
> Dissatisfied employees "get even" in **idiosyncratic** ways — one quits, one browses social media, one takes supplies home. Because the responses are creative and various, **controlling one behaviour with policy and punishment leaves the root cause untouched**. Correct the dissatisfaction, not the symptom.

CWB is sometimes an emotional reaction to **perceived unfairness** — an attempt to restore equity. This makes its ethics genuinely complicated: is taking a box of markers home for your children stealing, or partial repayment for uncompensated extra effort? Mitigations: poll attitudes, identify areas for improvement, measure CWB (self-assessments are as useful as coworker or supervisor reports, partly because of differences in observability), build strong teams with integrated supervisors, formal team policies and team-based incentives to lower CWB "contagion."

### Absenteeism and turnover

**Absenteeism** — dissatisfied employees are absent more, but the relationship is **not strong** and is conditional: where alternative jobs are plentiful, dissatisfied employees have high absence rates; where alternatives are few, their absence matches satisfied employees'. Guilt-proneness lowers absence.

**Turnover** — a stronger relationship, and a **pattern of declining job satisfaction is the best predictor**. It too is conditional:

- **Push vs pull.** An unsolicited job offer weakens the link: the person left for the lure of the other job, not the unattractiveness of this one.
- **Alternatives.** Dissatisfaction converts to turnover more readily when opportunities are plentiful — and **high human capital** (education, ability) has the same effect, because those people have or perceive more options.
- **Job insecurity.** Even satisfied employees are more likely to leave when insecurity is high.
- **Contagion.** If coworkers are also dissatisfied, turnover spreads — so managers should consider satisfaction and turnover patterns of coworkers when assigning people to an area.
- **Embeddedness** — connections to the job and community — lowers turnover probability, particularly in collectivist cultures, because embedded employees protect the resources their attachment has built. But embeddedness in a **toxic** environment means feeling **stuck**, with adverse physical and psychological consequences.

### Managers often "don't get it"

The bottom-line evidence: firms with **high morale** (>70% expressing overall satisfaction) saw stock prices grow **19.4%** versus **10%** for medium/low-morale firms, and "100 Best Companies to Work for in America" generated **2.3–3.8% higher annual stock returns** than other firms across 1984–2011.

Yet many managers are unconcerned — and, worse, **overestimate satisfaction**, so they see no problem where one exists:

- Of 262 large employers, **86% of senior managers** believed their organizations treated employees well; only **55% of employees** agreed.
- **55% of managers** thought morale was good; only **38% of employees** did.

Regular surveys narrow the gap. A KFC manager in Houston surveyed employees quarterly and changed policy in response (more say over which days off) — but valued the process itself as much as the findings: *"That's the most important part of it — that they have a voice and that they're heard."*

## Key takeaways

- An attitude is **cognition + affect + behavioural intention**, and OB cares because the behavioural component becomes turnover, effort and citizenship.
- Attitudes predict behaviour more strongly when **important, specific, accessible, directly experienced** and not swamped by social pressure. **Cognitive dissonance** is the reverse arrow — behaviour reshaping attitude.
- The job attitudes are highly intercorrelated; **job satisfaction is the best single predictor** of behaviour. **Affective** commitment matters most beyond turnover.
- Satisfaction comes mostly from **job conditions** (interest, variety, autonomy, feedback, social support, supervision) and **personality** (CSE, interest–job fit) — **not from pay** beyond a comfortable level.
- Satisfaction produces **performance, OCB, customer satisfaction and life satisfaction**; dissatisfaction produces **EVLN** responses, **CWB**, absence and turnover.
- The turnover link is conditional on **alternatives, push vs pull, insecurity, contagion and embeddedness** — and embeddedness in a toxic workplace is a trap, not a benefit.
- **Managers systematically overestimate how satisfied their people are.** Measure it regularly.
