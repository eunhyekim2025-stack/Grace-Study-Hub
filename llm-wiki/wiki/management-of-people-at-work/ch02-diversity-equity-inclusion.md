# Chapter 2 — Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion in Organizations

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch01-what-is-organizational-behavior|Ch 1]] · Next: [[ch03-job-attitudes|Ch 3 — Job Attitudes]]

---

## Core summary

The chapter runs a single argument in six steps:

1. **Diversity has two levels.** Surface-level (visible, stereotype-activating) and deep-level (values, personality, work style — what actually matters once people know each other).
2. **Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination is the behaviour it predicts.** Both come in explicit and implicit, overt and subtle forms — and the subtle forms are now the more common and no less damaging.
3. **Four theories explain why difference turns into disadvantage** — social categorization, stereotyping/threat/stigma, system justification and social dominance, and intersectionality/cultural mosaic.
4. **Inside groups**, diversity is genuinely hard in the short run: surface-level diversity raises early conflict, and **fault lines** fracture teams into competing subgroups. Functional diversity, and time, are what make it pay.
5. **Across countries**, Hofstede's dimensions and the GLOBE framework describe how work-related values vary — plus cultural tightness–looseness, religion, expatriate adjustment and **cultural intelligence (CQ)**.
6. **Management is three goals, not one**: **diversity** (representation), **equity** (access), **inclusion** (belonging) — implemented through leadership, recruitment, training and, above all, a genuine diversity **culture and climate**.

> [!important] The thesis in one line
> Representation alone does nothing. Diversity produces value only when paired with equity and inclusion, and only when the prejudice mechanisms underneath are actually addressed rather than declared away.

## Understanding diversity

### Levels of diversity

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Surface-level diversity** | Differences in easily perceived characteristics — gender, race, ethnicity, age — that do not necessarily reflect how people think or feel, but that **activate stereotypes** |
| **Deep-level diversity** | Differences in values, personality and work preferences that become **progressively more important** for determining similarity as people get to know one another |
| **Biographical characteristics** | Personal characteristics that are objective and easily obtained from personnel records — the raw material of surface-level diversity |

Evidence shows people are **less concerned about demographic differences** once they see themselves sharing more important characteristics such as personality and values. Two coworkers with visibly different race, age and national background who discover a shared approach to time management and work problems will get along and experience less conflict — the deep-level similarity overshadows the surface-level difference.

This is exactly the distinction developed in [[management-of-people-at-work/mpw-2-2|MPW 2-2]].

### Biographical characteristics at work

**Race and ethnicity.** Race is the heritage people use to identify themselves; ethnicity the additional set of cultural characteristics that often overlaps with it. Conventionally race is associated with biology and ethnicity with culture, but both have a history of self-identification. Due to systemic racism, minorities report higher workplace discrimination. In the US context, Black employees receive lower interview ratings, lower performance ratings, less pay and fewer promotions. In one controlled study of low-wage jobs, **Black applicants with no criminal history received fewer offers than White applicants with criminal records**.

**Age.** The workforce is ageing across developed countries and mandatory retirement has effectively been outlawed in the US; over 40 countries prohibit age discrimination. Managers increasingly value older workers' experience, judgment, work ethic and commitment to quality — Singapore's Public Utilities Board reports 27% of its workforce is over 55 and credits them with workforce stability. Stereotypes persist that older workers are less adaptable and less motivated to learn new technology.

> [!note] The age–performance finding
> The majority of studies show **virtually no relationship between age and job performance**. On satisfaction the picture is more nuanced: a review of 800+ studies found older workers more satisfied with better coworker relationships, while a 40-year, 20,000-participant study found satisfaction *falling* the longer someone stays at one organization. Ageing raises satisfaction; tenure at one place lowers it.

**Gender identity and sexual orientation.** Neither appears to affect job performance or leadership.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Gender identity** | People's deeply held sense of or identification with their own gender — does not necessarily match sex at birth, is not visible to others, and cannot be neatly categorized |
| **Sexual orientation** | People's patterns of enduring physical, emotional and/or romantic attraction toward others |

Yet gaps in pay, benefits and rewards are strong and substantial — recent estimates put them at **up to fourteen times the size of performance differences**. Biases produce the **glass ceiling** (impeded advancement) and the **glass cliff** (women disproportionately selected for crisis-handling leadership roles where they are set up to fail — found in a study of twenty Spanish organizations). As of April 2020 women held only 6% of S&P 500 CEO positions, despite hundreds of studies rating women effective leaders and evidence that women's representation in leadership **predicts financial performance**.

On sexual orientation, a Harvard study sent near-identical résumés to 1,700 real entry-level openings; those mentioning involvement in gay organizations received **60% fewer callbacks**. Research indicates disclosure is associated with reduced work–family conflict and improved partner satisfaction, well-being and job satisfaction — but the decision is personal and, in practice, made dozens of times a day.

## Prejudice and discrimination

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Prejudice** | An attitude representing broad, generalized feelings toward a group or its members that **maintains the hierarchy** between that group and others |
| **Implicit bias** | Prejudice that may be hidden outside one's conscious awareness |
| **Discrimination** | Actions or behaviours that create, maintain or reinforce some groups' advantages over others |
| **Disparate impact** | Employment practices with an **unintentional** discriminatory effect on a legally protected group |
| **Disparate treatment** | Employment practices with an **intentional** discriminatory effect on a legally protected group |
| **Microaggressions** | Automatic, subtle, stunning exchanges that negatively affect those from minority or marginalized backgrounds |

> [!note] Benevolent prejudice
> Prejudice is not always negative in feeling. Viewing Asians as a "model minority" who excel academically and professionally, or viewing women as kind, gentle and deserving of protection, are **benevolent prejudice** — positively valenced, and still prejudice, because they still maintain a hierarchy.

### The forms discrimination takes

| Form | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Discriminatory policies or practices** | Denying equal opportunity or unequal reward — e.g. older workers targeted for layoffs because they are highly paid |
| **Sexual harassment** | Unwanted advances and conduct creating a hostile or offensive environment |
| **Intimidation** | Overt threats or bullying directed at members of specific groups |
| **Mockery and insults** | Jokes and negative stereotypes; "jokes taken too far" |
| **Exclusion** | Being left out of opportunities, social events, discussions or informal mentoring — often unintentional |
| **Incivility** | Disrespectful treatment — interrupting, ignoring opinions, aggressive manner |

Microaggressions come as **microinsults** (rudeness or insensitivity) and **microinvalidations** (language that excludes, nullifies or negates others' thoughts, feelings or experience).

**Disparate impact is the harder problem for HR.** The best predictors of job performance — structured interviews and cognitive tests — also tend to be the tools most likely to discriminate against protected groups. A newer route is social media screening: even a structured, systematically administered rating of candidates' profiles can produce disparate impact, because the profiles reveal demographics.

**Consequences.** For employees: higher stress and perceived injustice, negative job attitudes, worse physical and psychological health. For employers: reduced productivity and OCB, more conflict, increased turnover, increased risk-taking, and qualified candidates lost at hiring and promotion. Even with no lawsuit ever filed, there is a **business case** for eliminating unfair discrimination.

## Four theoretical perspectives

### 1 · Social categorization

**Social categorization** is the process through which people make sense of others by constructing social categories — groups sharing similar characteristics. Minds run on **heuristics** because the information load is too high to process otherwise.

Noticing difference is not itself bad — noticing that one candidate is more qualified is necessary to hire. **Unfair** social categorization assumes everyone in a group is the same instead of looking at individuals within it.

The categorization is two-sided: perceivers categorize others, and people categorize *themselves* and identify with those they share characteristics with. The result is the **ingroup/outgroup** split, producing **ingroup favouritism** and **outgroup derogation**, and — inside organizations — the fragmentation of work groups into subgroups, lowering team performance.

### 2 · Stereotyping, stereotype threat, stigma

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Stereotyping** | Judging someone based on one's perception of the group they belong to |
| **Stereotype threat** | The degree to which someone is concerned with being judged by, or treated negatively because of, a certain stereotype |
| **Stigma** | Attributes that cannot be readily seen, are concealable, and convey an identity devalued in certain social contexts |

Stereotypes are **the fuel that powers the discrimination engine**. What creates stereotype threat is not whether the stereotype is true of you but whether you believe you will be **judged by it**.

> [!warning] The four self-defeating responses to stereotype threat
> ① **Unconsciously exaggerating** the stereotype. ② **Self-handicapping** — withholding effort so failure can be attributed elsewhere ("bad day"). ③ **Overcompensating** to avoid confirming it. ④ **Brain drain** — working memory is depleted, so test and training performance actually falls.
>
> Countermeasures: treat employees as individuals rather than highlighting group differences; increase awareness of how stereotypes get built into policy; reduce differential treatment through objective assessment; confront microaggressions; adopt transparent practices signalling that all employees are valued.

**Stigma** applies to concealable attributes — and to jobs themselves. "Dirty work" studies show garbage collectors carrying a *physical* stigma and debt collectors a *moral* one. Research across 108 studies of ~10,000 employees suggests disclosure tends to produce better interpersonal, work, health and family outcomes for certain stigmas; concealment carries immediate emotional costs while identity expression carries emotional benefits.

### 3 · System justification and social dominance

These two shift the focus from how people think about others to **the context** — why prejudicial arrangements are accepted and defended.

| Theory | Claim |
|---|---|
| **System justification theory** | Group members often **accept, rationalize, legitimate or justify** their own experiences of inequality, prejudice and discrimination. Over time, "the ideas of the dominant tend to become the ideas of the dominated" |
| **Social dominance theory** | Prejudice and discrimination rest on a **hierarchy** in which one group dominates another and enjoys privilege the subordinate group is denied |
| **Social dominance orientation (SDO)** | The individual difference: how much a person *desires and supports* that hierarchy and believes some groups are inferior |

In one study, people who learned about **systemic** barriers to women's executive success were *more* likely to view those barriers as legitimate than people who learned nothing. System justification thrives where people believe they have low personal control, the situation is inescapable, and they depend on or feel grateful to the system. It can be broken by directing attention at the **restrictive nature** of the inequality itself.

**SDO** is one of the strongest predictors of prejudicial belief and discriminatory behaviour. Its organizational consequences run both ways: high-SDO applicants are attracted to high-status, similar-to-themselves organizations and repelled by diverse ones; high-SDO managers are less likely to hire from low-status groups and judge their performance more harshly — especially when they perceive special treatment.

### 4 · Intersectionality and the cultural mosaic

**Intersectionality** — the idea that identities interact to form different meanings and experiences. We are not the *sum* of our characteristics but a **multiplicative combination** of them. Adam Grant's formulation of the 2018 US Open incident: *"When a man argues with an umpire, it's passion. When a woman does it, it's a meltdown. When a Black woman does it, it's a penalty."*

**Double jeopardy** is the negative case: people with multiple stigmatized characteristics face compounded effects. Minority women experience more workplace harassment, more hiring discrimination and heavier penalties as leaders than minority workers and women workers considered separately.

**Cultural mosaic theory** generalizes it. A person is made of many **tiles** — biographical/demographic (age, race, ethnicity, gender identity), geographical (climate, location) and associative (employer, religion, political affiliation, hobbies). Theorists outline fifteen sample tiles, giving at least 105 unique pairwise intersections. People emphasize different tiles in different settings (home versus work), and two people may **share tiles** — a shared employer, for instance.

A review of nearly 300 studies found this multicultural approach had **uniformly positive effects**: reduced prejudice, discrimination and stereotyping, and increased support for diversity policies.

## Diversity dynamics inside groups

### Group composition

Research is genuinely inconclusive and depends on how diversity is indexed. What has emerged:

| Type | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Surface-level diversity** | **Increases conflict**, especially early in a group's tenure — lowering morale and raising turnover. Members arrive with different understandings of task, norms and goals, so they are "not on the same page" and coordinate poorly at first |
| **Deep-level diversity** | Differing values or opinions produce more conflict — but leaders who focus the group on the task and encourage group learning can reduce it and improve discussion |
| **Functional diversity** | Differences in skills, abilities and experience **may improve performance and innovation** — contingent on several factors. It boosts creativity by facilitating knowledge sharing, and functionally diverse founding teams do better in competitive environments |

A telling experiment: culturally diverse and homogeneous groups performed **equally well** on a virtual wilderness survival test, but members of diverse groups were **less satisfied, less cohesive and had more conflict**.

> [!important] The honest summary on diverse teams
> In the short term diverse groups are hard: fragile, prone to fragmentation, tending to homogenize over time. But diverse groups are also **less likely to fall into conformity**, and more likely to deliberate longer, share more information and make fewer factual errors. If members can weather their differences, diversity makes them more open-minded, more creative and better performing. The management job is getting them through the early period.

### Fault lines

**Fault lines** are perceived divisions that split a group into two or more subgroups based on individual differences such as gender, race, age, work experience, language and education.

They form when **multiple attributes align**. A group of three Black employees with finance backgrounds and three Hispanic employees with marketing backgrounds has a fault line, because two dimensions of difference reinforce each other.

Effects are generally bad: subgroups compete, taking time from core tasks; groups learn more slowly, make riskier decisions, are less creative and have more conflict; subgroups distrust each other and cohesion falls. Note the trap — **satisfaction within subgroups is generally high while the overall group's satisfaction is lower**, so the problem is easy to miss from inside.

**Fixes:** crosscut the roles · give the group a common superordinate goal · recruit similar pairs within a diverse group · build pro-diversity beliefs and open-mindedness norms (trainable). One study of German Ministry of Foreign Affairs diplomats found pro-diversity beliefs reduced the negative effect of fault lines on performance. And fault lines around **skill, knowledge and expertise** may actually help in a **results-driven culture**, because attention stays on what matters to the company rather than on subgroup problems.

This is the same material as [[management-of-people-at-work/mpw-2-2|MPW 2-2]], which adds the "wisdom of crowds" framing from the lecture.

## Cross-cultural OB

### Hofstede's framework

Hofstede surveyed **116,000+ IBM employees in 40 countries** on work-related values, and found five dimensions of national culture:

| Dimension | High end |
|---|---|
| **Power distance** | Large inequalities of power and wealth exist and are **tolerated** — a class or caste logic discouraging upward mobility. Low = stress on equality and opportunity |
| **Individualism vs collectivism** | Individualism: people prefer to act as individuals and believe in individual rights above all. Collectivism: a tight social framework where people expect their groups to look after and protect them |
| **Masculinity vs femininity** | Masculinity: the culture favours traditional masculine roles — achievement, power, control — with separate roles for men and women. Femininity: little differentiation; women treated as equals in all respects |
| **Uncertainty avoidance** | High: anxiety about ambiguity, laws and controls used to reduce uncertainty. Low: accepting of ambiguity, less rule-oriented, more risk-taking, readier to accept change |
| **Long- vs short-term orientation** | Long-term: the world is constantly changing, so prepare — thrift, persistence, pragmatism. Short-term: maintain the link to the past — traditions, social obligations, protecting image |

Hofstede later added **indulgence versus restraint** — enjoying life and having fun versus regulating conduct through strict social norms.

Selected extremes: power distance highest in **Malaysia**; the US is the **most individualistic** nation (then Australia, Great Britain), Guatemala the most collectivistic; Japan highest on masculinity, Sweden highest on femininity; Greece highest on uncertainty avoidance and **Singapore the lowest**; Hong Kong among the longest-term oriented, Pakistan the shortest.

> [!warning] How to use Hofstede honestly
> Research across **598 studies and 200,000+ respondents** found the five original dimensions equally strong predictors of relevant outcomes. But the framework describes **national averages** — do not assume all people from a country share the same values. This is the [[ch01-what-is-organizational-behavior|Ch 1]] "few absolutes" rule applied to culture.

### The GLOBE framework

The **Global Leadership and Organizational Behavior Effectiveness** programme (founded 1993) used data from **951 organizations in 62 countries** and identified **nine** dimensions. Five resemble Hofstede's (power distance, uncertainty avoidance, gender differentiation, future orientation, and collectivism — which GLOBE splits into **institutional collectivism** and **ingroup collectivism**). It adds:

- **Humane orientation** — how much a society rewards being altruistic, generous, fair and kind
- **Performance orientation** — how much a society encourages and rewards performance improvement and excellence
- **Assertiveness** — how much a society emphasizes confidence and advocating for what one wants
- **Gender egalitarianism** — how much a society de-emphasizes traditional gender roles

### Cultural tightness–looseness

**Tight** cultures have clear, strong, heavily enforced norms; **loose** cultures are more ambivalent about restrictions on autonomy. Gelfand's account: tight norms emerge from historical threat — Japan, among the most disaster-prone countries in the world, needs tight norms to coordinate and survive; the US, being loose, produced a "conflicted, unstandardized, uncoordinated" COVID response.

Practical consequence: **high-performance HR systems work better in tight cultures**, because workers are motivated to actually implement rigorous hiring, training, reward and participative-decision practices. And **expatriates find tight countries hardest to adapt to**, because the norms are strongly observed and heavily enforced.

### Religion, expatriate adjustment, and CQ

**Religion** is an employment issue almost everywhere, and discrimination on it is prohibited in many countries with a **reasonable accommodation** requirement. Religious discrimination claims are a growing category, partly because the issues are complex.

**Expatriate adjustment** improves with: feelings of empowerment and motivation to interact with other cultures · previous culture-specific work experience · self-esteem and self-efficacy · language ability, relational skills, role clarity and autonomy, organizational support and familial support. Adjustment rises over time for everyone.

**Cultural intelligence (CQ)** — a worker's ability to function effectively in culturally diverse settings. The illustration: a Brazilian executive stands close and touches an Australian executive's shoulder; the Australian keeps backing away, and it takes half an hour for the Brazilian to notice. High CQ means both **understanding** the difference and **perceiving and adapting quickly** in the moment.

CQ is associated with expatriate career aspirations and adjustment, job performance, psychological health, cross-cultural leadership, negotiation performance and OCB. Two development notes: **cultural generalists beat cultural specialists** (broad, general international experience predicts ability to manage international affairs; specific experiences do not), and an "us versus them" mentality makes people more sensitive to cultural rejection abroad, which damages adjustment and CQ development.

## Implementing diversity management

**Diversity management** — the use of **evidence-based strategies** to manage and leverage the workforce's inherent diversity. A focus on diversity alone is not enough, which is why organizations aim at three goals:

| Goal | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Diversity** | Celebrating rather than denigrating difference; enhancing **representation** of diverse, marginalized people |
| **Equity** | Providing access to the **same opportunities** for all, recognising that some are afforded privilege and others confront barriers. Strategies: engaging diverse communities, critiquing unjust structures, building coalitions, education on inequity |
| **Inclusion** | Creating an environment where all people feel **valued, welcomed and included** — able to contribute, involved in decisions affecting them, their unique "voice" able to resonate |

> [!note] Affirmative action, correctly stated
> In the US, affirmative action **does not involve quotas** for minority groups — those have been ruled unconstitutional. It is a good-faith effort to recruit, select and train qualified minorities to enhance representation and fair treatment. The "reverse discrimination" framing treats equity and merit as opposed; research on **multicultural meritocracy** — emphasising both — shows it can reduce stereotyping and discrimination while increasing engagement for everyone.

### Two evidence-based mechanisms

| Model | The idea | The catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Common ingroup identity** | Use the same social categorization machinery that divides people to unite them — shift focus from what divides ("us"/"them") to what unites ("we"). Demonstrated to reduce racism toward immigrants, increase support for inclusion, and even lower IAT bias scores | Can inadvertently **overshadow people's diverse identities**, and may undermine equity efforts by making workers less likely to notice and address inequality |
| **Contact hypothesis** | The more people from diverse backgrounds interact, the more prejudice declines over time. Contact need not be physical — **virtual, imagined and indirect** contact all reduce prejudice, and prejudice reduction is contagious through your own group members | Negative early encounters can **reinforce** prejudice, especially among high-SDO individuals. Less effective in high-power-distance countries, and dampened by stress and low mental resources |

Two illustrations of contact working: **imagined contact** led hiring managers to rate transgender applicants as more likeable and hirable; and across NBA players and coaches 1955–2000, players initially got more playing time from same-race coaches, but **the longer they were on the team together, the more playing time coaches gave players of different races**.

### Practices

**Leading for diversity.** Four leadership roles: ① advocate for diversity as a resource ② promote positive intergroup interactions ③ stimulate cross-background discussion to manage organizational knowledge ④ encourage continuous reflection on diversity practices and goals. To promote inclusion, leaders start with humility and self-understanding, then **facilitate belongingness** (support all workers as team members, include them in decisions) and **convey that uniqueness is valued**.

> [!tip] Oppositional courage
> Confronting prejudice as it happens, and removing hidden barriers to promotion, carries real risk for a leader in a toxic culture. But a study of transgender employees found that **observing** such acts sent a powerful message to the organization — improving self-esteem, job attitudes and well-being while reducing emotional exhaustion.

**Recruitment and staffing.** Target recruitment messages to underrepresented groups; partner with relevant institutions and associations. Women and minorities show greater interest in employers highlighting a DEI commitment — but diversity advertisements that **fail to show women and minorities in leadership positions send a negative message**. In hiring, use a well-defined protocol for assessing talent: when the protocol is clear and non-discrimination is prioritized, qualifications dominate demographics in who gets hired.

**Training and development.** Most programmes have three components: the legal framework for equal employment opportunity · how a diverse workforce better serves a diverse market · personal development that brings out everyone's skills. Reviews of hundreds of studies find diversity training **does work** — improving diversity knowledge and representation and reducing discrimination.

But two conditions matter. First, a **needs analysis**: a programme may successfully raise awareness of discriminatory practice while doing nothing to help employees recognise their own biases. Second, **opportunity to apply it** — one study found workers applied interracial perspective-taking skills only if they had close cross-race friendships, which is why **goal setting** to build trainee motivation matters.

**Culture and climate.** "Establishing a diversity program is not enough."

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Diversity culture** | The shared diversity values, prioritization of diversity, and belief that it should be fostered by members of an organization |
| **Diversity climate** | The shared **perceptions** of diversity-enhancing policies, practices and procedures — workers are aware that supportive norms are actually enforced |

A positive diversity climate is linked to financial performance, customer satisfaction, commitment and retention, and can even **reduce the negative effect of fault lines**. It helps refugees and expatriates adapt faster. In effect, a diversity culture and climate is the common ingroup identity model put into organizational practice.

### Four reasons diversity management fails

1. **Authenticity.** Leaders who appear to pursue DEI for self-interested reasons, or who are "all talk," lose all credibility. Even attempts to seem more relatable (through humour, say) can backfire into diminished inclusion perceptions.
2. **Tokenism** — a perfunctory effort to enhance representation so the company *seems* to value diversity. An S&P 1500 board analysis found more boards than chance would predict with **exactly two women** — "twokenism" — conforming to peers and then stopping.
3. **Paradoxical effects.** Appointing someone from an underrepresented group to leadership can legitimize diversity — or backfire if that leader is tokenized, placed on a **glass cliff**, or has their diversity "put on trial" (a stereotype-threat variant where they are pressured to excel).
4. **Context.** Organizations do not exist in a vacuum. Regional culture, gender-role expectations, patriarchal institutions and government policy can undermine even well-designed internal practices.

## Key takeaways

- **Surface-level diversity activates stereotypes; deep-level diversity is what actually determines whether people work well together.** Similarity in values and work style overrides visible difference once people know each other.
- **Prejudice (attitude) → discrimination (behaviour).** Both can be implicit; as overt forms became illegal, **subtle** forms — exclusion, incivility, microaggressions — took their place and do comparable damage.
- Four lenses: **social categorization** (us/them), **stereotype threat and stigma** (the target's own response is part of the harm), **system justification and SDO** (why inequality is accepted and defended), **intersectionality and cultural mosaic** (identities multiply).
- **Diverse groups start worse and can end better.** Surface diversity raises early conflict; **fault lines** fracture teams — and subgroup satisfaction stays high while group satisfaction falls, so the damage hides. Common goals, crosscut roles and pro-diversity norms are the fixes.
- **Hofstede (5 dimensions, 40 countries) and GLOBE (9 dimensions, 62 countries)** describe national value differences; **CQ** is the individual capability to operate across them, and generalist experience builds it better than specialist experience.
- **DEI is three distinct goals**, and representation without equity and inclusion is tokenism. Diversity **climate** — enforced practices people actually perceive — is what makes programmes work.
