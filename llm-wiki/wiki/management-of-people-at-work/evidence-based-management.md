> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - **Evidence-based management (EBM)** = the conscientious, explicit, judicious use of the best current evidence to make organizational decisions — borrowed from evidence-based *medicine*.
> - Managers routinely substitute **six weaker things** for evidence: obsolete knowledge, personal experience, specialist skill, hype, dogma, and mindless benchmarking.
> - EBM is a **mind-set**, not a technique: put aside "dangerous half-truths" and demand facts before acting.
> - Four everyday practices build it: **demand evidence, examine logic, treat the organization as an unfinished prototype, embrace the attitude of wisdom.**
> - It shifts **power** from authority/reputation/intuition toward data — which is exactly why senior leaders often resist it.

## What EBM is

> [!info] Definition
> Adapting David Sackett's definition of evidence-based *medicine*: **the conscientious, explicit, and judicious use of the current best evidence in making decisions** — here, decisions about how to run an organization.

- Management is a **craft** learned through practice — but craft improves when it is *routinely guided by the best logic and evidence*, updated relentlessly from inside and outside the firm.
- Harder than in medicine: the evidence is weaker, "experts" are everywhere, and companies vary so wildly in size/form/age that a "cure" proven in one place is **risky to assume** elsewhere.

## The problem: six substitutes for evidence

Managers (like doctors) reach for these instead of the best evidence:

| # | Substitute | The trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Obsolete knowledge** | What was learned in school, never re-checked |
| 2 | **Personal experience** | Vivid firsthand memory *feels* richer than data, but the sample size is tiny (e.g. importing a past firm's pay policy into a very different company) |
| 3 | **Specialist skill** | You default to the fix you're best at — "to a hammer, everything looks like a nail" |
| 4 | **Hype & marketing** | Vendors exaggerate benefits; genuinely good practices often have no loud advocate |
| 5 | **Dogma & belief** | Ideology makes a practice feel right, so its effectiveness is never questioned (e.g. faith that stock options lift performance; the "first-mover advantage" myth) |
| 6 | **Casual benchmarking** | Copying top performers *without* understanding why it works there → at best a perfect imitation of a moving target |

> [!example] Casual benchmarking gone wrong
> **Shuttle by United** (1994) copied Southwest's *surface* features — 737s, casual dress, no meals, faster turns — but not the essence: Southwest's culture and priority on employees. Southwest ended with *higher* California market share; the Shuttle was shut down.

## Worked example: "Should we adopt forced ranking?"

Forced-curve ranking (GE's "rank-and-yank") sorts staff onto a bell curve — reward the top ~20% (A), develop the middle ~70% (B), counsel out or fire the bottom ~10% (C). An evidence-based process would have exposed the flaws:

- **Broken causality.** *The War for Talent* rated firms as high/average performers first, then measured their talent practices — treating a 1997 practice as the "cause" of 1987–1997 performance. The proposed cause must come *before* the effect.
- **Cooperation suffers from big pay gaps.** Where work needs coordination, wider top-to-bottom pay dispersion correlates with **lower product quality** (Cowherd & Levine, 102 business units) and worse team outcomes — even in baseball, the least coordinated major sport (Bloom, 1,500+ players).
- **Systems beat stars.** MacDuffie's 15+ years on auto plants: lean/flexible systems (teams, training, job rotation, de-emphasized status) build **better cars at lower cost** than individual talent does. Average players can be highly productive, and A-players can founder, depending on the system.
- **Practitioners dislike it.** In one firm's poll of ~100 top executives, a "stacking system" was voted the *worst* obstacle to turning knowledge into action; an HR survey of 200+ pros linked forced ranking to lower productivity, inequity, weaker engagement, and mistrust in leadership.

## Four practices to build EBM

<div class="dc-cols">
<div class="dc-card"><b>1 · Demand evidence</b> Ask "what's the evidence this will work?" for every proposed change. DaVita's Kent Thiry — <b>"No brag, just facts"</b> — backs claims with quantitative comparisons; reports even flag important metrics as "not available" to prompt gathering them.</div>
<div class="dc-card"><b>2 · Examine logic</b> Asking for backup research isn't enough — probe gaps in exposition, logic, and inference. Most management studies are correlational, so control for alternative explanations. Ask: "What would have to be true about people for this to work? Does that feel true?"</div>
<div class="dc-card"><b>3 · Treat the org as an unfinished prototype</b> Run pilots and controlled experiments. Harrah's, eBay, and Yahoo constantly A/B-test; a small experimental promo at Harrah's beat a costlier one, and employee-selection experiments cut turnover ~50%.</div>
<div class="dc-card"><b>4 · Embrace the attitude of wisdom</b> The best managers "profoundly appreciate how much they do not know" — they act decisively on current knowledge while still questioning it. Humility + decisiveness, not know-it-all certainty.</div>
</div>

> [!example] EBM is often about the mundane
> A University of Missouri study: groups that **stood up** in meetings took 34% less time to decide, with no loss in quality. Scale it: if Chevron's 50,000+ employees each swapped one 20-minute sit-down meeting a year for a stand-up, that's ~6,000 hours saved annually.

## Why it's hard — and the power cost

- EBM is a mind-set that **clashes** with how many managers operate: it asks them to drop cherished beliefs and conventional wisdom (the "dangerous half-truths").
- It **redistributes power.** As one executive put it: if decisions are made on *facts*, everyone's relevant facts are equal; if on *opinions*, the senior person's opinion counts for more. EBM replaces authority, reputation, and intuition with data — so leaders may lose stature.
- The core choice for a leader: **do you want to be told you're always right, or do you want to lead an organization that actually performs well?**

## Why this matters for Management of People at Work

- **Pay & reward design:** the evidence on pay *dispersion* and forced ranking directly informs how you structure incentives for cooperative work — a central people-management decision.
- **Engagement & trust:** practices that "feel" motivating (stacking, dispersed pay) can quietly erode morale and trust — so test them, don't assume.
- **Manager mind-set:** EBM is a transferable habit for every HR/people decision — demand the evidence, unpack the logic, pilot before rolling out firm-wide, stay humble.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Evidence-based management (EBM)** | Using the best current logic + evidence to make organizational decisions |
| **Six substitutes** | Obsolete knowledge, personal experience, specialist skill, hype, dogma, mindless benchmarking |
| **Casual benchmarking** | Copying top performers without understanding *why* their practice works |
| **Forced ranking** | Rating staff on a bell curve and rewarding/removing by tier ("rank-and-yank") |
| **Pay dispersion** | The gap between highest- and lowest-paid; wide gaps can hurt cooperative performance |
| **Attitude of wisdom** | Acting on current knowledge while actively questioning it (humility + decisiveness) |
| **Unfinished prototype** | Treating the organization as something to keep testing and improving via pilots |
