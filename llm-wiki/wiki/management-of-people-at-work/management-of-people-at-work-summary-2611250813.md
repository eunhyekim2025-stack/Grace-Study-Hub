## AI Adoption & Human‑Centered Design  
*How psychology shapes AI success and what to do about it.*  

- **Failure risk** – >70 % of AI initiatives fail; treat AI as a socio‑technical program, not just engineering.  
> [!info] **AI adoption reality** – Billions spent, but failure exceeds that of traditional digital transformation; continuous iteration & oversight are required.  

- **Core psychological barriers**  
  - **Algorithm aversion** – Trust drops after a few errors, even if the model outperforms humans overall.  
  - **Fairness concerns (uniqueness neglect)** – Uniform treatment triggers resistance in high‑stakes domains (hiring, medicine).  
  - **Black‑box problem** – Model opacity lowers confidence despite human cognition also being opaque.  
  - **Pro‑human bias** – Preference for human decisions masks equal or greater human bias from training data.  
  - **Processing fluency** – Concrete human credentials (years of experience) are trusted more than abstract algorithmic provenance.  
  - **Dual perception** – AI is seen simultaneously as a magical savior and a dangerous monster.  
> [!warning] **Over‑reliance & awe** – “Magical” framing can cause users to over‑trust AI, assuming supernatural capabilities.  

- **Anti‑AI bias in the workplace** – Systematic penalties for AI‑augmented workers.  
  - Lower wage offers, reduced leadership‑potential ratings, higher likelihood of headcount cuts.  
  - Freelancers suffer most; formal contracts soften the bias.  
  - Many conceal AI use (“secret cyborg”) to protect reputation, stifling knowledge sharing.  

| Bias effect | Typical outcome |
|------------|-----------------|
| Pay & compensation | Lower wage offers for AI‑assisted workers |
| Leadership perception | Reduced rating of leadership potential |
| Job security | Higher likelihood of headcount cuts |
| Reputation | Seen as lazy or replaceable |
| Transparency | Concealment of AI use (“secret cyborg”) |

> [!example] **Pay experiment** – Participants valued “Steve + AI” less than “Steve alone,” yielding a 46 % win‑win score, illustrating anti‑AI bias in compensation decisions.  

- **Human‑centered AI design**  
  - Design with creator **and** user psychology in mind (behavioral human‑centered AI).  
  - Counter **inventor’s bias** – creators over‑estimate benefits and under‑estimate risks.  
  - Treat adoption as an ongoing socio‑technical program: monitor, iterate, align with evolving context.  
> [!warning] Ignoring behavioral factors leads to project failure despite technically superior models.  

- **Preparing for an AI‑augmented future**  
  1. **Foresight exercise** – Teams predict a plausible AI‑driven transformation for a profession by 2035.  
  2. **Second‑order consequences** – Identify resources that become abundant/scarce and the resulting value shifts.  
  3. **Reverse‑engineer preparation** – Choose today the skills that will make you an early adopter of the predicted shift.  

  *Sample prediction*: AI‑augmented design tools enable rapid, innovative creation → face‑to‑face collaboration declines → communication & empathy become premium skills.  

  **Skill focus** – communication, empathy, relationship building; stay current on emerging tech; study AI security, ethics, auditability.  

> [!info] **“Stay where the puck is going”** – Prioritize future‑oriented skill development over defending current roles.  

## Decision‑Making Biases & Team Dynamics  
*How defaults, framing, and group structure shape outcomes, and how to build effective teams.*  

- **Decision‑making biases**  
  - **Default effect** – Opt‑out organ‑donation in Singapore lifts consent from ~20‑30 % to ~80‑90 %.  
  - **Framing effect** – Wording (“happy” vs. “unhappy”) flips responses.  
  - **Bounded rationality** – Choices made with limited information and cognitive capacity.  
  - **Selection & survivorship bias** – Must use representative samples; WWII plane‑repair case missed damage on planes that never returned.  
  - **Sunk‑cost fallacy** – Ignore past investments; evaluate only future expected outcomes.  
  - **Overconfidence / better‑than‑average** – People routinely rate themselves above the median.  
  - **Power distance** – Low distance aids rapid crisis coordination; high distance hampers information flow.  
> [!warning] Avoid basing decisions on non‑representative data or on past sunk costs; these introduce systematic errors.  

- **Group vs. Team**  

| Criterion            | Group / Work group | Team / Work team |
|----------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| Interdependence      | Low / none        | High            |
| Shared objective     | Weak / optional   | Strong, explicit|
| Synergy              | Neutral/negative  | Positive (> sum)|
| Accountability       | Individual        | Collective      |

> [!info] **Group** – ≥2 individuals who interact, share a common objective, but work largely independently.  
> [!info] **Team** – Interdependent members, shared goal, positive synergy; output > sum of parts.  

- **Team types & effectiveness**  
  - **Four common team types**: problem‑solving, self‑managed, cross‑functional, virtual.  
  - **Three pillars of effectiveness**:  
    1. **Composition** – diversity (personality, culture, function), clear roles, optimal size.  
    2. **Processes & states** – conflict management, cohesion, motivation, psychological safety, transactive memory.  
    3. **Context** – resources, leadership, structure, culture, incentives, technology, crisis conditions.  
> [!info] Assess any team by asking: *Composition? Processes/States? Context?*  

- **Bias‑mitigation interventions**  
  - **Financial incentives / nudges** – steer healthier or safer choices.  
  - **System design** – prompts, training modules, serious‑games.  
  - **Pre‑mortem** – imagine future failure, work backward to preventive actions.  

- **Desert‑survival ranking exercise (worked example)**  
  1. **Individual ranking** – each member orders 15 items (1 = most essential).  
  2. **Team ranking** – 50 minutes to reach consensus; no edits to personal lists after start.  
  3. **Submission** – unified team list submitted; instructor gives final 5‑minute warning.  
  - **Key debate** – whether rain is needed only for shelter or essential for survival.  
> [!warning] Once the team ranking phase begins, **no edits** to individual lists are allowed; the team must submit a single unified ranking.
