# Chapter 8 — Motivation: From Concepts to Applications

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch07-motivation-concepts|Ch 7]] · Next: [[ch09-foundations-of-group-behavior|Ch 9 — Foundations of Group Behavior]]

---

## Core summary

Chapter 7 explained *why* people are motivated. This chapter is the toolkit, in four families:

1. **Job design** — the **Job Characteristics Model (JCM)**, and the redesign options: rotation, enrichment, relational job design and job crafting.
2. **Alternative work arrangements** — flextime, job sharing, telecommuting.
3. **Employee involvement (EIP)** — participative management and representative participation, with heavy cultural caveats.
4. **Rewards** — establishing a pay structure, variable-pay programmes, flexible benefits, and intrinsic recognition.

> [!important] The pattern across all four
> Every lever works **conditionally**. Flextime helps absenteeism much more than performance; telecommuting raises satisfaction and creates isolation; pay-for-performance motivates and can corrupt. The evidence-based move is always to ask **for whom, under what conditions, and does the expectancy chain actually hold** ([[ch07-motivation-concepts|Ch 7]]).

## Motivating by job design: the JCM

**Job design** — the way the elements in a job are organized. The **job characteristics model (JCM)** describes any job on **five core dimensions**:

| Dimension | Definition | High | Low |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Skill variety** | Requires a variety of activities using different skills or talents | Garage owner-operator doing electrical repairs, engine rebuilds, bodywork and customers | Spraying paint eight hours a day |
| **Task identity** | Requires completion of a **whole and identifiable** piece of work | Cabinetmaker who designs, selects wood, builds and finishes | Operating a lathe to make table legs only |
| **Task significance** | Substantially affects the lives or work of other people | Nurse in an intensive care unit | Sweeping hospital floors |
| **Autonomy** | Freedom, independence and discretion in scheduling and choosing procedures | Sales manager who schedules their own work and tailors the approach | Account rep following a standardized script |
| **Feedback** | Carrying out the work generates **direct and clear information** about your performance | Testing and inspecting iPads | Installing components on a moving assembly line |

### The causal chain

$$\underbrace{\text{variety} + \text{identity} + \text{significance}}_{\text{experienced meaningfulness}} \;\;\underbrace{\text{autonomy}}_{\text{experienced responsibility}} \;\;\underbrace{\text{feedback}}_{\text{knowledge of results}} \;\rightarrow\; \text{outcomes}$$

The three **critical psychological states** — meaningfulness, responsibility, knowledge of results — produce **high internal work motivation, high-quality performance, high satisfaction, and low absenteeism and turnover**. The relationship is moderated by **employee growth-need strength**: people with a high growth need are more likely to experience the states and to respond positively.

### Motivating potential score (MPS)

$$\text{MPS} = \frac{\text{skill variety} + \text{task identity} + \text{task significance}}{3} \times \text{autonomy} \times \text{feedback}$$

> [!important] Why the formula is shaped that way
> The three meaningfulness dimensions are **averaged** — a job needs to be high on **at least one** of them. But autonomy and feedback are **multiplied**, so a job scoring near zero on either has near-zero motivating potential **no matter how varied and significant the work is**.

**Efficacy.** Evidence supports the link between these characteristics and higher satisfaction, commitment and experienced meaningfulness. Notably, although jobs have become more autonomous, more interdependent and more skill-demanding since the 1970s, **the relationship with satisfaction has not changed** — employees still value enriched work. Moderators: the link is strongest for people with **trait positive affect**; and in **virtual work**, meaningfulness, responsibility and knowledge of results all suffer — which managers can offset by deliberately building personal relationships and raising task significance, autonomy and feedback.

## Job redesign

The chapter's illustration: an assembly worker at \$24/hour dropping instrument panels into Jeeps for eight hours a day quit for a print shop paying under \$15 — *"My brain was turning to JELL-O."* The failure to redesign produced turnover.

| Approach | What it is | Evidence and cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Job rotation** *(cross-training)* | Periodic shifting of an employee from one task to another with **similar skill requirements at the same level** | ~70% of millennial managers rate it important. Manufacturers use it for flexibility and to reduce boredom; Singapore Airlines rotates ticket agents through baggage handling. **Costs:** repeated training rounds, reduced productivity in the new role, disruption for the work group, more supervisor time answering questions |
| **Job enrichment** | Adding **high-level responsibilities** to increase purpose, direction, meaning and intrinsic motivation | Distinct from **enlarging** (just adding tasks) — enrichment adds a *layer of responsibility and meaning*. Roots in Herzberg. Nearly **twice as effective at reducing turnover** as a realistic job preview. Across 20,000+ British employees, it related to financial performance, labour productivity, absenteeism and output quality through satisfaction. Managers high in **openness** and in autonomous jobs themselves are likelier to design enriching jobs — everyone else must **work against the urge to micromanage** |
| **Relational job design** | Constructing jobs so employees **see the positive difference they make in others' lives** | Shifts the spotlight from the employee to the **beneficiary**. Medtronic shares patient stories at annual meetings; university fundraisers who briefly met a scholarship recipient **persisted 42% longer and raised nearly twice as much** — the effect appeared after meeting a *single* recipient. Mechanism: beneficiaries become memorable and emotionally vivid, prompting perspective-taking and commitment |
| **Job crafting** | Employees **customizing their own jobs** by altering tasks or interactions to capitalize on their strengths | Produces engagement, satisfaction, better performance and resilience. The school administrator who reframes late slips and absence calls as being a **liaison** between school and home. **But:** *avoidance demands crafting* — taking on congenial extra work while dodging pressing official demands — leads to missed deadlines, work intensification, health problems and turnover. Crafting also creates stress if you change your work without understanding your manager's goals |

## Alternative work arrangements

Especially important for a workforce of dual-earner couples, single parents and people caring for sick or aging relatives — and flextime and telework help women continue advancing after childbirth.

Post-COVID context: before the pandemic only 15–27% of US workers were fully remote; during it **67% of employers** were shifting employees toward remote work. Roughly **75 million US employees (56% of the workforce)** are *capable* of working from home; about two-thirds wanted to keep doing so; **98%** of 2,000+ remote workers wanted it for the rest of their careers; and **74% of surveyed executives** planned to keep some of the workforce remote.

### Flextime

Employees work a set number of hours per week but vary when, usually around a **common core** (e.g. 9am–3pm) inside a wider flexibility band. **57% of employers** use the core-hours approach; 29% set no common core. Some allow accumulating extra hours into days off; compressed workweeks are a variant. In **Finland**, most full-time workers have a **legal right** to decide the location and timing of at least half their schedule.

Why it matters to employees: **50%** cite flexible arrangements as very important to job satisfaction, **34%** say it is why they stay, **15%** would leave to get more of it.

> [!warning] What flextime actually delivers
> A review of 40+ studies finds flextime relates to positive outcomes **only weakly** overall. The effects are **much stronger for reducing absenteeism**, weaker for productivity and schedule satisfaction. Across 100,000+ employees, flextime weakly reduces **work interfering with family** but **does not affect family interfering with work**.
>
> Three further catches: German studies found flextime helps people set work–life boundaries — **but the boundaries do not hold unless daily work goals are met**, so overuse can undermine goal accomplishment. It is **not applicable to every job or worker** (young healthy and older unhealthy employees benefit most; people who want to *separate* work and family use it less). And **those who ask for it are often stigmatized** — which is avoided when the organization's own leaders visibly adopt flexible hours.

### Job sharing

Two or more individuals split a traditional full-time job. Only about **10% of US organizations** offered it in 2015, held back by the difficulty of finding compatible partners and historically negative perceptions of commitment.

The case for it: it draws on **more than one person's talents** for one job and opens access to skilled workers — retirees, parents of young children — unavailable full-time. Two part-timers may cost less in salary and benefits than one full-timer, **though training, coordination and administration costs can erase the saving**.

### Telecommuting

Working at home or anywhere else outside the physical workplace.

**Which jobs?** McKinsey studied 2,000+ tasks across 800 jobs in nine countries: most industries could average **at least 29% remote without productivity loss** — much more for finance, insurance, management and IT knowledge work, much less for manufacturing, construction, retail and food service. At task level, **no job is fully remote**: even the most remote-suitable tasks occasionally require presence (learning surgery, installing hardware, building a physical prototype). The education gradient is stark: **46.5%** of people with a bachelor's degree or higher work from home versus **3.2%** of those without a high school diploma.

| Benefits | Costs |
|---|---|
| Increases **performance and job satisfaction**; to a lesser degree reduces role stress and turnover intentions | **Social loafing** risk in team-focused workplaces, especially where the remote worker has family responsibilities their teammates lack |
| Work–family conflict reduction is felt more intensely by those remote **more than 2.5 days a week** | A **remote manager** hurts your performance — less available for guidance |
| Societal gain: US half-time telecommuting was estimated to cut carbon emissions by ~51 metric tons per year through office energy, traffic and road repair | **Demotivated office workers** who feel unfairly denied the same freedom — sharply visible for "essential workers" during COVID |
| A way to "get away from them all" if the work situation is emotionally exhausting | **Isolation**, lower satisfaction and worse coworker relationship quality |
| | **"Out of sight, out of mind"** — missing informal daily interaction disadvantages you for raises and promotions, unless remote work is the norm, contact with the supervisor is maintained, and productivity is visible |

**Making it work.** Success depends on communication quality. Successful telecommuters **set their own goals, rewarded themselves for good work, and thrived on autonomy** — telecommuters carry much of the motivational burden themselves. Organizationally: smart collaboration tools, frequent communication, clear transparent policies, trust-building. The **assess–create–support** framework: assess telecommuting needs → create practices empowering work–home boundaries → support them through adjustment, encouragement, role-modelling and monitoring.

## Employee involvement

**Employee involvement and participation (EIP)** — a participative process that uses employees' input to increase commitment to organizational success. Engaging workers in decisions that increase autonomy and control makes them more motivated, more committed, more productive and more satisfied — and the benefit extends to **teams** given more control.

| Form | Definition | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Participative management** | Subordinates share a significant degree of **decision-making power** with immediate superiors — formally (briefings, surveys) or informally (daily consultation) | Reduces the negative effects of **job insecurity** on satisfaction and turnover intentions. Requires followers to **trust** their leaders and be prepared for the change; leaders must avoid coercion, explain organizational consequences, and review progress. Effects on organizational performance are **mixed and typically not large** — higher stock returns, lower turnover, higher labour productivity |
| **Representative participation** | Workers participate through a **small group of representatives** — **works councils** (consulted on employee decisions) and **board representatives** (sitting on the board) | Required by law in many countries. It **redistributes power**, putting labour's interests nearer parity with management and shareholders. Influence on individual employees is **mixed** — surpassed as a motivational tool by more direct participation |

> [!warning] EIP must be tailored to national culture
> US employees readily accepted EIP; managers in **India** who tried to empower employees were rated **low** by those employees — consistent with India's high power distance, which expects authority differences. In **China** the picture is transitional: workers highly accepting of traditional cultural values showed **few benefits** from participative decision making, while **less traditional** workers were more satisfied and better rated. Urban Chinese research found the components favouring **consultation and expression** — but not participation in the decision itself — produced higher satisfaction.

## Extrinsic rewards

Pay is not the only driver of satisfaction ([[ch03-job-attitudes|Ch 3]]) but it matters more than companies assume: in an SHRM survey **47%** stayed with their employer because of pay and **62%** were looking elsewhere because of pay — **the number one reason on both sides**, out of thirteen. And over half of organizations surveyed by PayScale admit to having **no compensation strategy at all**.

### What to pay: the pay structure

Balancing two equities:

- **Internal equity** — the worth of the job to the organization (established by **job evaluation**)
- **External equity** — competitiveness relative to the industry (established by **benchmarking surveys**)

Pay more and you may get better-qualified, more motivated employees who stay longer and innovate more; across 126 large organizations, employees who believed their pay was competitive had **higher morale, higher productivity and more satisfied customers**. But pay is often the **single highest operating cost**, so paying too much prices the product out. And money's salience can breed self-serving behaviour — NHL and NBA players engage in **more self-serving play in their final contract year**.

The Costco/Walmart contrast makes the trade-off concrete: Costco pays ~200,000 people a minimum of \$15/hour with a laser focus on store operations and customer experience — *it will get more if it pays more*. Walmart runs a vastly larger, costlier store network and pays ~2 million people about \$11/hour base. Different strategies, different constraints — with the predictable result that **Costco employees are likely happier and less likely to leave**.

### How to pay: variable-pay programmes

A **variable-pay programme** (pay for performance) bases part of pay on an individual and/or organizational performance measure. Around **85% of companies globally** offer some form; about **70%** included it in total compensation in 2020. Organizations typically split awards, with over half of the short-term incentive from **organizational** performance and the rest from individual and unit performance.

| Plan | How it works | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Piece-rate** | Fixed sum per unit of production completed; a *pure* plan has no base salary | Produces higher effort, productivity and wages — one Chinese university raised piece-rate pay for articles and saw **research productivity rise 50%**. **68% of risk-averse individuals prefer individual** piece-rate; lower performers prefer **team** piece-rate (fearing others will slack). Not motivating when pace is set by **uncontrollable factors** like customer arrivals. Watch the incentive to **cut quality for speed**. And it is simply infeasible for many jobs — paying ER doctors only when patients recover would produce **ERs turning away terminal and life-threatening cases** |
| **Merit-based pay** | Pay for individual performance based on **performance appraisal ratings** | Common for salaried employees, but merit increases are typically **0–4% of the salary budget**. Used **together with bonuses**, merit raises are most effective at raising performance and preventing turnover. Limitations: only as valid as the ratings, which are **often subjective** — research shows **Black employees receive lower performance ratings** and demographic differences in salary increases. Unions typically resist it. Also, managers unknowingly pay for **projected future** performance: NBA players on an upward trend were paid above what their average performance predicted |
| **Bonuses** | Reward **recent** rather than historical performance | Now the **most common variable-pay incentive** (~53% of organizations). Incentive effects should exceed merit pay, since merit is cumulative but smaller. Firms can cut bonuses when times are bad — which is exactly the downside, since **"people have begun to live as if bonuses were not bonuses at all but part of their expected annual income."** Two design notes: **splitting rewards into categories** raises motivation (people feel they missed out if they fail to earn one from each); and in a high-tech factory, cash, family meal vouchers and recognition each raised performance 5% — with **non-money bonuses more effective** |
| **Profit sharing** | Distributes compensation by a formula built on company profitability — cash or stock options | Organizations with profit sharing show **higher profitability**; it is linked to higher commitment (especially in small organizations) and greater **psychological ownership**. It motivates individual performance best **in combination with other pay-for-performance plans**. Does not work where there is no profit — nonprofits, much of the public sector |
| **Employee stock ownership plan (ESOP)** | Employees acquire stock, often below market, as part of benefits | Increases firm performance, satisfaction and innovation — but **only when employees psychologically experience ownership**. May not reduce absenteeism or raise motivation, since the monetary benefit arrives only on cashing out later. Weaker where employees have **no control rights**; across ~2,000 European firms it predicted ROI **only where employees trusted managers and were low on uncertainty avoidance**. For top management it can **reduce unethical behaviour** — CEOs with an ownership share manipulate earnings reports less |

> [!warning] Where pay-for-performance backfires
> The effects are **small and positive** overall — and conditional. It works in **stable economic environments and organizations with fair policies**, and is weaker where **guanxi** shapes HR practice. Two failure cases worth memorising:
> - **Teacher merit pay on student test scores** — across thousands of US teachers, no positive impact on motivation or teaching practice, plus **higher stress and CWBs including cheating and bullying students** into performing.
> - **R&D invention rewards** — reduced motivation, **less collaboration between scientists**, and **lower-quality inventions**.
>
> Both are **expectancy/instrumentality breakdowns** ([[ch07-motivation-concepts|Ch 7]]): teachers do not control test scores; scientists do not control invention outcomes.
>
> It also strains relationships: managers on pay-for-performance show employer–employee strain and higher turnover; employees **compete and may actively try to harm one another**. Star employees respond well to pay dispersion while average and underperforming employees feel alienated — and when managers quietly make **"special deals"** for those falling behind, discovery makes everyone else perform **worse than before they knew**.

**Pay secrecy.** Most US organizations require or encourage it, and secrecy initially appears **detrimental to performance**. Individual amounts need not be broadcast — publishing **general pay categories** while making the performance link objective retains the motivational effect. But **transparency has its own cost**: it facilitates comparison, breeding jealousy and reducing cooperation and helping. What decides the outcome either way is **justice perceptions**.

## Benefits and intrinsic rewards

### Flexible benefits

Benefits now make up roughly **a third of total compensation costs**. **Flexible benefits** let each employee assemble a package suited to their own needs and situation — accommodating age, marital status, a partner's benefits, and the number and age of dependents. (Would a recent graduate and someone who paid off their loans a decade ago value student-loan repayment assistance equally?)

Flexibility does **not** mean everything is optional: managers rate **health care, retirement and leave** as essential and to be offered in some form, while housing assistance and travel are only slightly important and are better treated flexibly.

> [!note] Two limits on flexible benefits
> **Diminishing distinctiveness** — as more organizations adopt them, the individual motivation they produce falls, because they come to be seen as a **standard work provision**. And flexible benefits **may not substitute for higher salaries** as motivators.
> **Norms beat policy.** Offering a benefit is different from employees feeling free to use it. In high-pressure cultures people avoid taking vacation days for fear of falling behind or appearing undedicated — modelling coworkers' behaviour rather than deciding what is right for them ([[ch07-motivation-concepts|social learning]]).
>
> There is also an equity dimension: members of underrepresented groups often cannot attain full-time employment with benefits at the same rate, leaving them without medical, family and medical leave coverage.

### Employee recognition programmes

An **employee recognition programme** is a plan to encourage specific behaviours by **formally appreciating specific employee contributions** — from a spontaneous private thank-you to widely publicized formal awards with clear criteria.

Uptake: **59%** of organizations integrate recognition into talent management, **68%** say it positively influences retention and **56%** recruitment. Walt Disney World publicly recognises employees with over a hundred award types; LinkedIn reports a **95% new-employee retention rate** attributed to its recognition programme.

> [!important] The timing finding
> **Financial incentives may be more motivating in the short term, but in the long run non-financial incentives work best.** Recognition programmes are associated with self-esteem, self-efficacy and job satisfaction — and their obvious advantage under tight budgets is that they are inexpensive.

## Key takeaways

- The **JCM** is the core job-design tool: five dimensions → three psychological states → motivation, performance, satisfaction, retention, moderated by **growth-need strength**. In **MPS**, autonomy and feedback are **multiplicative** — either one at zero kills the job's motivating potential.
- Four redesign moves: **rotation** (breadth, at a training cost), **enrichment** (responsibility, not just more tasks), **relational design** (show the beneficiary — the fundraisers raised nearly twice as much), **job crafting** (powerful, unless it becomes avoidance crafting).
- **Flextime** mainly reduces **absenteeism**; **job sharing** is rare and hard to staff; **telecommuting** raises performance and satisfaction but produces isolation and an "out of sight, out of mind" career penalty.
- **EIP** raises intrinsic motivation and procedural justice — but must be tailored to national culture; empowerment attempts were rated **low** in high-power-distance India.
- Pay structure balances **internal** and **external** equity. **Variable pay** works modestly and conditionally; it fails hardest where the **expectancy or instrumentality link is broken**, and it can produce cheating, competition and sabotage.
- **Flexible benefits** individualize rewards but lose distinctiveness as they spread, and **norms decide whether benefits are actually used**.
- **Recognition** is cheap and, over the long run, more motivating than money.
