> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- Power = capacity to influence; it can exist without a formal title.  
- Five bases: coercive, reward, legitimate, expert, referent; formal vs personal power differ in durability.  
- Dependence (importance + scarcity + non‑substitutable) magnifies power; excess power reduces empathy and raises risk‑taking.  
- Personal power grows through expertise, visibility, confidence, and strategic alliances.  
- Beware of overconfidence, ethical drift, and “glass ceiling/cliff” traps.

## Power fundamentals
- > [!info] **Power**: capacity to influence others’ actions; may be held without being exercised.  
- Power does **not** require a leadership title – a junior with critical knowledge can wield it.

## Types of power
| Type | Basis | Typical source |
|------|-------|----------------|
| Coercive | Ability to punish | Authority to sanction |
| Reward | Ability to give incentives | Control of resources |
| Legitimate | Formal/legal authority | Position, rule of law |
| Expert | Superior skill/knowledge | Specialized expertise |
| Referent | Prestige, kindness, generosity | Personal charisma |

## Formal vs. personal power
> [!info] **Formal power** is attached to a role and grants rights to issue directives, rewards, or punishments.  
> [!info] **Personal power** stems from individual attributes (expertise, referent status) and yields stronger, longer‑lasting commitment.

## Dependence as a source of power
- Create dependence by offering **important**, **scarce**, and **non‑substitutable** resources.  
- Example: a sales rep highlights a product’s unique feature (importance), limited stock (scarcity), and lack of alternatives (non‑substitutable) to increase leverage.

## Effects of power
| Effect | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Disinhibition (Inhibition‑approach theory) | Heightened approach behavior, impulsivity, reduced listening. |
| Empathy loss | Lower compassion and perspective‑taking at higher status. |
| Risk appetite | Power holders perceive risky actions as less risky. |
| Responsibility | Greater power → higher ethical stakes. |

> [!info] **Inhibition‑approach theory**: power raises approach‑oriented actions and lowers inhibition, leading to impulsivity and reduced empathy.  

> [!warning] **Corruption risk**: “absolute power corrupts absolutely”; unchecked power can erode ethics.

## Empirical evidence
> [!example] **UC Berkeley Monopoly study** – Randomly assigned “rich” players (double salary, extra dice) behaved more dominantly, consumed more snacks, became ruder, and attributed success to personal skill, illustrating power’s impact on behavior and self‑attribution.

## Building personal power
- **Speak first** and take initiative to signal competence.  
- Use a **confident voice & posture**; they boost perceived expertise.  
- **Share specialized knowledge** across teams; visibility = power.  
- **Volunteer for challenging tasks** (avoid “setup‑to‑fail” assignments).  
- **Ingratiate strategically** – genuine or not, flattering a superior can improve standing.  
- **Form coalitions** – collective action (e.g., unions) multiplies influence; average union wages ≈ 27 % higher.  
- **Network as a connector** – being known for linking people raises your “connector” power.  
- **Self‑promote judiciously** – highlight achievements without excessive bragging.

> [!warning] **Glass ceiling & cliff** – systemic barriers may limit advancement; “glass cliff” places women in high‑risk roles. Assess role risk before accepting.

## Political tactics in organizations
- Identify **informal power structures** and the truly respected influencers.  
- **Align** with influential individuals; opposing them unnecessarily harms your influence.  
- **Build consensus** quietly with like‑minded colleagues before presenting ideas.  
- **Pre‑sell proposals** – gather support before decisions are made.  
- **Manage reputation** – actions (quality work) outweigh words; avoid tasks designed to fail.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Power | Capacity to influence others’ actions |
| Formal power | Authority derived from a position |
| Personal power | Influence from expertise, referent status, or charisma |
| Dependence | Others’ reliance on resources/knowledge you control |
| Inhibition‑approach theory | Power increases approach behavior, lowers inhibition |
| Glass ceiling | Invisible barrier limiting advancement of certain groups |
| Glass cliff | Placement of under‑represented leaders in precarious roles |
