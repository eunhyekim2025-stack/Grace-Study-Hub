# Chapter 17 — Human Resource Systems and Practices

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch16-organizational-culture-and-change|Ch 16]] · Next: [[ch18-stress-and-health|Ch 18 — Stress and Health]]

---

## Core summary

**HR systems and practices** — recruitment, selection, training, performance management — **influence people's behaviour in organizations and ultimately organizational effectiveness**. HR and OB are inextricably linked: HR practices shape manager and employee behaviour, and vice versa, and the cycle never ends. As one former HR consultant put it: *"HR problems never, ever end. You will never have a day when you can say, 'I'm finished.'"*

The chapter walks the employee lifecycle: **recruitment** (attracting the right applicants) → **selection** (three-stage funnel) → **training and development** (making it transfer) → **performance management** (what, who, how, and how to improve it) → **accessible workplaces** (reasonable accommodations) → **HR leadership** (high-performance work systems, communication, policy).

> [!note] Open hiring — the chapter's opening case
> Greyston Bakery in Yonkers has used **open hiring** since 1982: individuals are offered a position when they are next on the interest list. **No background check, drug test, interview or résumé.** Greyston therefore has essentially **no hiring costs** — instead it invests roughly **\$1,900 per new employee in hard and soft skills training**, plus connections to health insurance, housing, childcare and transportation.
>
> The reported local economic impact is **\$7 million annually** through public assistance savings, tax revenue and reduced incarceration costs. Standards are still strictly enforced — as the general manager notes, **performance problems usually come from what is happening in an employee's life today, not from their past**.

## Recruitment

### Applicant attraction

**Applicant attraction** — the degree to which an individual is **drawn toward an organization, intends to apply, and would accept an offer if given one**. The best recruitment systems attract the most qualified candidates, who then fit better and are more satisfied in the jobs they take.

Some personality traits (extroversion, conscientiousness) predispose people toward attraction generally, but attraction is mostly driven by:

- **position and organization characteristics** — pay, reputation
- **perceived fit** with the organization
- **impressions of the recruiter**
- **fairness of the process** (e.g. timely responses)
- **self-efficacy** — whether they believe they will succeed if they apply and get the job

**Perceived fit and impressions of the recruiter matter most early on.** **Material symbols** (logo, imagery, social media presence — see [[ch16-organizational-culture-and-change|Ch 16]]) signal the organization's values, which may or may not resonate.

> [!warning] The dark side of attraction
> **Vague job requirements** let applicants draw wrong conclusions about the job and can make job seekers feel **entitled to being hired or deserving of a higher salary**. Be clear and specific about who you are looking for.

### Referral hiring

**Referral hiring** — when a hiring manager hires a job seeker based on **their own previous experience with that person or a recommendation from a referrer**. **61%** of hiring managers source applicants through their professional networks. People refer for bonuses, reputation, reduced risk of hiring "someone no one knows", or to strengthen network ties, and referred candidates get stronger social ties, privileged information, and easier onboarding.

Is it a good idea? Evidence cuts both ways. Call center employees who refer friends **stay longer and perform better**. But when the **referrer holds a lot of power** (see [[ch13-power-and-politics|Ch 13]]), other employees view them as self-interested and power-seeking, and **judge both referrer and hire more harshly**, supporting the decision less than for a lower-power referral.

### Recruiters

- **49%** of organizations use external recruiters and **28%** in-house recruiters for **executive/managerial** talent — but for **non-executive** talent the numbers reverse: **89%** use in-house.
- **Dedicated recruiters handle 2.7× more requisitions** than HR professionals with other duties — worth creating dedicated positions where applicant flow is large.
- The most effective recruiters are **well informed about the job, efficient in communicating, and treat recruits with consideration and respect**; internal recruiters must use fair and just practices, because **fairness perceptions predict job offer acceptance**.

> [!warning] Bias filters into recruiters too
> Recruiters give **job-seeking women less information on leadership positions** than men and feel more anxious interacting with them. Job seekers from **unrepresented racial groups report receiving less recruitment source information overall**, which reduces their attraction to positions and worsens their performance in the hiring process.
>
> It is a **two-way street**: negative recruitment experiences send underrepresented job seekers to **competitors**, and they "spread the word" to others.

### Realistic job previews

**Realistic job previews** (job tryouts) — given to demonstrate to job seekers what they would actually be doing on the job. They **clarify and manage applicant expectations** and **decrease turnover**, because both sides know what they are getting into ahead of time. Beyond expectation management there is a human element: as one talent acquisition VP put it, previews help ensure **"every candidate walks away from their experience feeling like they matter, whether they got the job or not."**

## Selection

**Human capital resources** — the capacities available to an organization through its employees: specialized skills, collective knowledge, abilities and other workforce resources. They are **activated by leaders' behaviours** (see [[ch12-leadership|Ch 12]]) and **change over time** as people develop and roles shift.

The **selection** process matches individual characteristics (ability, experience) with job requirements. A failed match hurts performance and satisfaction — and the **cost of turnover is up to twice that employee's annual salary**, plus the loss of their contribution to human capital, **possibly to a competitor**.

### Stage 1 · Initial selection

**Initial selection** — methods used for **preliminary rough cuts**, deciding whether an applicant meets the **basic qualifications**.

| Method | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Application forms** | By itself not a useful predictor of performance, but a **good initial screen** where the information is genuinely required (no point interviewing an unlicensed nurse). Never ask about **protected class membership, marital status, dependents or family obligations** — legally risky, and applicants who meet unnecessary or illegal questions **feel alienated and abandon the application** |
| **Applicant tracking systems (ATS)** | ~85% of larger corporations have a careers page. An ATS creates unique applicant records, scans for keywords, and can even use **machine learning** to predict success or turnover. **Do not attach a photo** — it invites discrimination and unconscious bias, may be read as unprofessional, and can break your ATS record |
| **Résumés and cover letters** | Not standardized, so **independent raters of the same résumé rarely agree**, and applicants are motivated toward impression management. Not immune to **disparate impact** — hiring managers subconsciously judge them differently based on assumed ethnicity from names and education. **Video résumés**: only 3% of hiring managers prefer them vs **78% for written**, they make impression management easier to see through, and they carry the same discrimination risk as photos |
| **Background checks** | At least **96%** of employers run at least one. **86%** do it primarily **to protect employees and customers**; 56% report more consistent safety and security, and 71% uncover issues they would otherwise have missed |

**Four kinds of background check:**

- **Reference checks** — a **paradox**: most employers want reference information but few will give it out, for fear of being sued (for saying something bad — or even something good about a poor performer). As many as **30% of hiring managers regularly discover false or misleading references**. Reference-checking software sending 10-minute surveys may produce more objective information.
- **Letters of recommendation** — **only weakly predictive** of graduate and medical school performance. Applicants pick referees who will praise them, so almost all are positive; readers either ignore them or read between the lines. When they *do* raise doubt, that happens **disproportionately for underrepresented groups**. And a classic study found letters written by **different** referees about the **same** person were **less similar** than letters written by the **same** referee about **different** people.
- **Informal social media checks** — recruiters' ratings from them **did not predict job performance or turnover** beyond normal measures, profiles often contain information that could **illegally** affect hiring (political affiliation, marital status), and research suggests they lead to **biased decisions regarding women and White candidates**.
- **Credit history checks** — credit scores did predict task performance, OCB and conscientiousness, but consistency is questioned and **Black job seekers can be adversely impacted**. Employers must establish a genuine need and comply with the **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)**.

> [!important] Criminal background checks — the narrow legal path
> **73.5 million US adults — roughly one in three — have criminal records.** The **EEOC** holds that candidates **cannot be denied employment based only on background check findings** (no blanket "no convictions" policy), and the checks are often inaccurate anyway.
>
> A conviction history can legally justify rejection only if ① the violation **relates to the job** or is **federally mandated** (an embezzler can be disqualified from finance or a TSA role, but not marketing) ② **convictions, not arrests**, are considered ③ the employer **considered less adverse alternatives**.
>
> Best practice: **give applicants a chance to explain** — many redeem themselves in recruiters' eyes. Some companies deliberately hire applicants who would not pass, valuing second chances, and report they become valuable contributors.

### Stage 2 · Substantive selection

**Substantive selection** — methods determining the **most qualified** applicants from among those meeting basic qualifications. Up to **one-third** of surveyed organizations use written tests, though applicants view them as **less valid and fair** than interviews or performance tests.

**Written tests — three types:**

| Test | Strength | Catch |
|---|---|---|
| **Intelligence / cognitive ability** | Long-proven valid predictors across skilled, semiskilled and unskilled jobs; **particularly good for cognitively complex jobs**; many experts call them the **single best selection measure across jobs**, as valid in the EU as in the US. Newer work suggests separating **mathematical, verbal and technical** abilities predicts better than one overall score | Only **16–18%** of organizations use them, because of **disparate impact** — of all substantive methods, cognitive ability tests produce **the most** |
| **Personality** | Inexpensive and simple to administer; use has grown; conscientiousness and extroversion predict performance. Most often given to executives and middle managers | **Faking** — easy to claim to be hard-working and dependable, and applicants **are not always aware they are faking**. **Observer-rated** personality predicts performance and behaviour, so consider asking people who know the applicant well |
| **Integrity** | Assess propensity to behave (un)ethically; **powerful alternatives to polygraphs** for predicting theft, discipline problems and excessive absenteeism, with **little disparate impact** | Available tests **do not all predict equally well**, may be easily faked, and applicants react negatively — especially when misclassified or stigmatized. Use only where there is **business necessity** tied to job requirements |

**Performance-simulation tests** — the logic is simple: *what better way to determine whether applicants can do the job than by having them do it?* They are harder to develop and administer, but have the **highest validity** (they essentially duplicate the job) **and** higher **face validity**.

| Simulation | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Work sample tests** | Hands-on simulations of part or all of the routine work. Widely used for skilled trades — welders, machinists, carpenters, electricians — and increasingly at all levels. **High-fidelity** samples duplicate the task; **lower-fidelity** ones (e.g. **virtual reality simulations**) give a similar experience where the real task would be unrealistic or dangerous |
| **Assessment centers** | Off-site locations where candidates go through **one to several days of exercises** simulating real problems — e.g. role-playing a manager who must respond to **ten memos in an in-basket within two hours** — evaluated by line executives, supervisors and/or trained psychologists. **Good predictors of performance**, but ratings may be confounded by whether applicants can guess the traits needed, or by role players' dispositions and first impressions |
| **Situational judgment tests** | Ask applicants **how they would perform** in various job situations, comparing answers to those of high performers (e.g. a call center applicant choosing the best response to an angry customer transcript). **Lower cost, moderate validity, relatively low disparate impact.** But coaching improves scores, and applicants can identify the correct answer **even with the situation removed** — raising the question of whether they measure judgment or test preparation |

**Interviews** — always standard practice worldwide, and they carry a **disproportionate amount of influence**. That is a problem: **impression management** such as self-promotion (see [[ch13-power-and-politics|Ch 13]]) strongly affects interviewer preferences **even when the displayed traits are unrelated to the job**, a poor interview cuts a candidate regardless of experience, test scores or letters, and candidates have been rated lower for something as trivial as a **blemish on their face**.

> [!important] Structured beats unstructured
> **Unstructured interviews** — short, casual, improvised questions — are **simply not an effective selection method**: the data is biased and only modestly related to future performance. **Structured interviews** — planned and designed to gather job-related information — **limit subjectivity** and give more reliable responses.
>
> Yet managers are reluctant to give up favourites like *"If you could be any animal, what would you be, and why?"* A good structured item is **open-ended and standardized for all candidates** — e.g. an extroversion probe asking the candidate to describe a specific multi-day workshop where they knew no one, exactly how they perceived it and what they did to interact.

### Stage 3 · Contingent selection

**Contingent selection** — final checks for candidates who passed substantive selection, before the offer is confirmed.

**Drug testing** is controversial. Applicants think testing without reasonable suspicion is invasive and that they should be judged on job performance, not lifestyle. Employers point to cost and safety: the average drug user is **3.6× more likely to be in an accident**, receives **3× the sick pay and benefits**, is **5× more likely to file a workers' compensation claim** and misses **10× more workdays** — with drug use costing **\$81 billion annually** in lost productivity, absenteeism and health care. The Supreme Court has called drug tests **"minimally invasive."**

But do they work? The **positivity rate has stayed below 6% since 1996**, suggesting the tests are insensitive and unreliable, or easily gamed (diluting, altering, substituting samples). A large US Postal Service study found the programme **reduced absenteeism and turnover but did not affect accidents or injuries**. Some HR professionals call them **"obsolete"** outside occupations requiring public trust; some researchers propose **personality tests geared toward a propensity for drug abuse and risky behaviour** instead.

**Medical examinations** — under the **ADA**, US firms may not require a medical exam **before** an offer, but may conduct one **after a contingent offer**, and only to determine whether the applicant is physically or mentally able to do the job (or how to accommodate a disability). Validity is not compelling and job-relatedness is hard to establish, so focus **only on the exact health conditions proven to adversely affect performance** — as trucking companies do by testing **hearing and vision** rather than running broad "physicals."

## Training and development

Competence deteriorates and becomes obsolete. US corporations spend over **\$70 billion annually** on training; retail, wholesale and customer service organizations dedicate over **\$10 million annually** each.

> [!important] The number one goal is transfer
> **Transfer of training** — utilizing the knowledge, skills and abilities learned in training **on the job**. What good is spending billions if no one puts it to use? Done well, training boosts **human capital** and employees' **career competencies**, transferable to future jobs.

**On-the-job methods:** job rotation, apprenticeships, understudy assignments. US companies increasingly use **longer-term job rotations** to cross-train and foster collaboration. Drawback: they **disrupt the flow of work**, and some content is better learned away from the work context.

**Off-the-job methods** absorb most of the spending — **live workshops** are the most popular, plus self-directed training, e-learning, mobile learning, webinars, podcasts and MOOCs. **Corporate universities** have existed since the 1960s (not a recent development): JetBlue University in Orlando trains pilots and flight crews on policies and practices in one place, simulating scenarios that could not be run on the job.

### Content — training vs education

**Education** primarily targets **acquiring knowledge** — facts, figures, the steps in a process. **Training** goes beyond knowledge toward **application**: building skills, developing abilities, acquiring competencies like adaptability, emotional competence and leadership.

Organizational training splits into **technical skills** (new software, programming languages) and **soft skills** (regulating emotions, building relationships, communicating clearly). One important soft-skill area is **ethics training** — recognizing moral issues, regulating moral emotions, considering perspectives, treating others with respect and civility. After one such intervention, **civility, respect, job satisfaction and trust increased, while incivility, cynicism and absences decreased**.

### Methods

| Method | Core idea | Evidence / limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Instructional system design (ISD)** — the **ADDIE** model | Formal, developer- and trainer-centred: **Analyze** (what needs training, what goals, who the trainees are) → **Design** (what success and failure look like, instructional strategies, feedback) → **Develop** (where it occurs, what resources are needed) → **Implement** (engage trainees, create and encourage transfer opportunities) → **Evaluation** (money saved, accidents avoided, people trained — feeding revisions) | Extremely useful and widely used, but criticized for **falling short on the most important person: the trainee** |
| **Active learning** | Trainee-centred. Three tenets: ① **encourage exploration and reflection** ② **normalize errors and mistakes** ③ **control emotions**. E.g. building basic programs, deliberately committing errors to see what happens, managing frustration | Reacted to positively, produces knowledge and skill acquisition, and **facilitates transfer** — largely through **trainee motivation**. But trainees find it frustrating if handed everything and told to "get to it"; it works **best in moderation and for cognitive skills** rather than facts; and it suits people motivated to learn more than those trying to **avoid failure** |
| **Interactive learning** | Learning is a **social construction created from interacting people** — training should be active **and social**. In 18 high schools, positive teacher–student relationships drove engagement; among 10,000+ teachers, satisfaction with training was **more attributable to the trainer than to the course content** | Transfer depends on **culture and climate**: attend ethics training in a workplace that rewards ignoring it and nothing transfers. Safety training transfers when **supervisors, coworkers and safety professionals all take responsibility**. Sustain it with collective reflection and **after-action reviews (AARs)** |
| **E-learning** | The **fastest-growing** method. Emphasizes **learner control over pace and content**, online communities, simulations and group discussions — the best of active and interactive learning in a boundless electronic environment | Can beat traditional classroom instruction when learners **actively participate** in exercises and quizzes. Improve it with **regular prompts to set learning goals, effective study strategies and progress measurement**; delivery is moving to **microlessons, on-the-spot tips and learning games on mobile** |

### Evaluating effectiveness

Effectiveness can mean **trainee satisfaction**, **amount learned**, **transfer to the job**, or **financial ROI** — and **these are not always related**. Some people enjoy an upbeat class and learn very little; some learn a great deal and cannot figure out how to use it; and behaviour changes are sometimes too small to justify the cost. So **rigorous measurement of multiple outcomes** should be part of every training effort.

The aggregate evidence is positive: on-the-job training raises productivity by **significantly more than its cost**; **cross-cultural training worked when delivered after arrival in the new country, not before departure**; the climate for employee development relates to business unit performance; and across 260 companies, **training expenditures were positively related to corporate innovation**.

## Performance management

**Performance management** (also called performance appraisal or evaluation) is **more often than not designed and executed poorly** — an annual appraisal that terrifies employees, gives little actionable feedback, runs on subjective impressions, and leaves everyone tense and worse off than if it had never happened. The chapter's horror stories: a year-old complaint on the permanent record that the employee never heard and the supervisor did not record; sexist comments with nothing to do with performance (*"somebody said they didn't like your facial expression"*; *"you need to be more direct"* followed by *"you need to be less direct"* once the employee changed).

Done correctly it is **a continuous process where managers identify, measure and develop individual and team performance aligned with strategic goals**, focused on the three OB outcomes: **job performance, OCB, and (avoiding) CWB**. A person who does core tasks well but is rude is not a good employee; neither is the most pleasant worker who cannot do the job.

**Purposes:** general HR decisions (promotions, transfers, terminations) · identifying **training and development needs** · providing **feedback** and the basis for **reward allocation** including merit pay.

### What do we evaluate?

| Criterion | Use and risk |
|---|---|
| **Individual task outcomes** | Quantity produced, scrap, cost per unit; sales volume, new accounts. **Incredibly unmotivating** when behaviour and outcomes are disconnected or outcomes are governed by forces beyond the employee's control — recall **expectancy theory** ([[ch07-motivation-concepts|Ch 7]]). The illustration: being evaluated on **air-conditioner sales in Alaska** |
| **Behaviors** | Necessary where work is part of a group effort and individual contributions are hard to isolate — promptness in submitting reports, leadership style, contact calls per day, helpfulness to other reps. Can include **OCBs**: helping others, suggesting improvements, volunteering for extra duties |
| **Traits** | Good attitude, confidence, dependability, staying busy, experience — but these **may not correlate with task outcomes**, and some traits actively distort supervisors' perceptions. **Politically skilled employees** are better at recognizing and capitalizing on chances to "showcase" initiative to evaluators — and may not take initiative when the supervisor is not watching. Still widely used |

### Who should evaluate?

Traditionally the manager, but self-managed teams and telecommuting mean the immediate superior may not be the most reliable judge. **Self-evaluations** suffer from **overinflated assessment and self-serving bias** and seldom agree with supervisors' ratings — better suited to **developmental than evaluative** purposes.

**Use multiple sources**: any individual rating may say as much about the rater as the ratee, and averaging across raters is more reliable and unbiased.

**360-degree evaluations** collect feedback from the employee's full circle of daily contacts — subordinates, customers, bosses, peers, team members, suppliers. Typically **five to ten appraisals** per employee (as few as three or four, as many as 25). The appeal: participation, accountability, more accurate readings, and ratings from people who saw the person **in different contexts**.

> [!warning] 360-degree evidence is mixed
> Many organizations **do not train evaluators** to give constructive criticism. Letting employees **choose** their peer and subordinate raters **artificially inflates** positive feedback. Too much weight can go to people who know little about actual performance, and **disagreements between rater groups are hard to reconcile** — **peers give much more lenient ratings** than supervisors or subordinates and make more appraisal errors. These evaluations should **supplement, not supplant**, objective evaluation.

### Methods of evaluation

| Method | Description |
|---|---|
| **Written comments** | A narrative of strengths, weaknesses, past performance, potential and suggestions. Simplest — no forms or training — but the result may reflect **the evaluator's writing skill** more than the employee's performance, and comparison across employees is hard without a scoring key. **AI and machine learning** now let HR score written comments quickly and accurately |
| **Critical incidents** | Describing what the employee did that was **especially effective or ineffective**, citing only specific behaviours. Provides rich examples and **anchors** defining what excellent and unsatisfactory performance look like |
| **Graphic rating scales** | One of the oldest and most popular: the evaluator rates a set of factors (quantity, quality, depth of knowledge, cooperation, attendance, initiative) on incremental scales — e.g. job knowledge from 1 ("is poorly informed about work duties") to 5 ("has complete mastery of all phases of the job"). Less depth than comments or incidents, but **faster and quantitatively comparable** |
| **Behaviorally anchored rating scales (BARS)** | Combines critical incidents with graphic rating scales: employees are rated along a continuum whose points are **examples of actual job behaviour** rather than general descriptions or traits. Built by first collecting specific illustrations of effective and ineffective behaviour |
| **Electronic performance monitoring (EPM)** | Captures objective indicators — how long employees stay logged into collaboration tools, how many reports they file, whether they **"cyberloaf."** Also used for legal liability, safety and developmental feedback — and sometimes for **purely authoritarian purposes**. Employees react **negatively**, especially when it is **real-time, non-transparent and invasive** |
| **Forced comparisons** | Relative rather than absolute. **Group order ranking** places employees into classifications (top fifth, second fifth) — with 20 employees, four must be in the bottom fifth. **Individual ranking** rank-orders everyone best to worst, with **no ties**, assuming the gap between 1st and 2nd equals the gap between 21st and 22nd |

> [!warning] Relative evaluation feels unfair
> Employees consider performance management much fairer when judged **relative to their own past performance** rather than relative to peers. Amazon has been described as directing managers to use group order ranking across teams and divisions; managers who came forward describe **immense pressure**, especially when everyone is a top performer. One former manager: *"Whomever is the least best has to go… it's like firing someone who got an A− when the rest of the class got A's."*

### Improving evaluations

**Rater errors:** **positive leniency** (unconsciously inflating) · **negative leniency** (understating) · the **halo error** (one characteristic unduly influencing the assessment of others) · the **similarity error** (favouring people with traits like one's own — e.g. introverts rating extroverts lower). Some evaluators treat appraisal as a **political opportunity**, and many managers **deliberately distort ratings** to keep a positive relationship with subordinates or to look good by showing everyone performing well.

**Four fixes:**

1. **Use multiple evaluators** — more evaluators means more accurate information and greater acceptance of feedback. The diving/gymnastics logic: drop the highest and lowest, average the rest. If nine of ten supervisors rate an employee excellent and one rates them poor, discount the outlier.
2. **Evaluate selectively** — appraisers should judge only areas where they have **working knowledge**, and should be **as close as possible in organizational level** to the person evaluated. Evaluators in a **core position in the individual's social network** get more firsthand observation than peripheral ones.
3. **Train evaluators** — most rater training changes the **frame of reference**, teaching what to look for so everyone defines good performance the same way. Also effective: asking raters to **describe behaviour in as much detail as possible**, which forces recall instead of acting on current feelings.
4. **Provide due process** — three features: ① **adequate notice** of what is expected ② **a fair hearing** where all relevant evidence is aired and the individual can respond ③ **a final decision based on the evidence and free of bias**. Employees who feel **safe to contribute** and supported by a leader who welcomes disagreement leave appraisal meetings feeling they succeeded. **Posting appraisals online** before the interview raised perceived rater accountability and employee participation.

> [!important] Bias interrupters
> Only **9.5%** of employees from underrepresented ethnic groups received **references to leadership** in their evaluations — **more than 70% less** than in White women's evaluations — and mentioning leadership skills usually predicts **higher competency ratings the following year**. Decades of research show underrepresented employees are judged **on their mistakes rather than their potential**, and are more likely to have **personality traits** mentioned instead of what they did — with those traits more often negative.
>
> Two interrupters: ① an evaluation form with **specific competencies**, each rating supported by **at least three pieces of evidence**, avoiding open-ended questions (without structure, people fall back on **stereotypes**) ② a **workshop** teaching people to use the form, using **last year's actual comments** to surface patterns of bias. Audit reviews thoroughly **every year**.

### Providing feedback

Few activities are more unpleasant for managers, and unless pressured by policy they tend to ignore it. **Four reasons:**

1. Managers **fear confrontation** when presenting negative feedback.
2. Employees **become defensive** — criticizing the manager or redirecting blame. (Managers do it too: they become **cynical and defensive** about multi-source feedback on themselves and are **more likely to leave** as a result, especially under pressure with few resources.)
3. Managers have **inflated perceptions** of how employees receive their feedback and of their own accuracy, so they deliver it ineffectively.
4. Employees have an **inflated assessment of their own performance** — the average estimate is much higher than reality.

The solution is training, not avoidance. An effective review — where the employee perceives the appraisal as **fair**, the manager as **sincere and empathetic**, and the climate as **constructive** — leaves the employee upbeat, informed and determined. Practical points: employees **in a bad mood are much less likely to take advice**; appraisals should be **as specific as possible** (people most overrate themselves on *overall* performance, and specific areas are easier to improve anyway); and the review should be **a counseling activity more than a judgment process**, best allowed to **evolve from the employee's self-evaluation**.

> [!note] Myth or Science? The 24-hour workplace is harmful — mostly true
> Employees who **used smartphones at night for work were less engaged the next day**, even accounting for other technology use. **Workaholism** — constantly thinking about work off the job and feeling compelled to work excessive hours — is associated with **burnout, stress and family problems**, and is enhanced not only by personality but by **excessive workloads, conflicting priorities and time pressures**. Workaholics often do not notice, because they are committed and enjoy it **until burnout**.
>
> The counterweight is **psychological detachment**: short regular breaks of total rest and avoidance of work responsibilities **recharge energy** and make people more productive over the long haul. See [[ch18-stress-and-health|Ch 18]].

## Accessible workplaces

Policies on disability vary by country — Canada, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Syria and Mali have specific protective laws. In the US, representation of individuals with disabilities rose rapidly after the **ADA (1990)**, which requires employers to make **reasonable accommodations** so workplaces are accessible. Recognized disabilities include **missing limbs, seizure disorder, Down syndrome, deafness, schizophrenia, alcoholism, diabetes, depression and chronic back pain** — conditions sharing almost no common features, so there is **no single definition** of how each relates to employment. The **EEOC** classifies a person as having a disability if they have any physical or mental disability that **substantially limits one or more major life activities**.

**Physical disabilities.** When disability status is randomly manipulated among hypothetical candidates, individuals with a disability are rated as having **superior personal qualities like dependability**, and one review found workers with disabilities **receive higher performance evaluations** — yet they **encounter lower performance expectations and are less likely to be hired**. In Europe, employer-motivation policies have often failed and outright **quota systems in Germany, France and Poland have backfired**. Managers should be attuned to the **true requirements of each job** and match skills to them. Accommodation should not be merely transactional: across nearly 20,000 German federal employees, **supervisors and subordinates worked together** to establish accommodations that worked for everyone — and **reduced turnover**.

**Hidden (invisible) disabilities** — not directly observable, and disclosed only at the employee's discretion. Categories: **sensory** (hard of hearing) · **autoimmune** (rheumatoid arthritis) · **chronic illness or pain** (carpal tunnel) · **cognitive or learning** (ADHD) · **sleep disorders** (insomnia) · **psychological** (PTSD). **Mental disabilities may affect performance more than physical ones** — people with depression and anxiety are significantly more likely to be absent.

> [!warning] The disclosure dilemma
> Under the **ADAAA (2008)** US organizations must accommodate a broad range of disabilities — but **employees must disclose to be eligible** for accommodations and employment protection. Many do not, fearing they will be **stigmatized or ostracized** and that managers will think them **less capable**; a **new diagnosis later in life** compounds those fears.
>
> A hidden disability is not truly invisible — someone with undisclosed ASD still shows the characteristic behaviours — and observers **misattribute**: reading a coworker's slow, slurred speech as alcohol dependency rather than the long-term effects of a **stroke**.
>
> Research suggests **disclosure generally helps everyone**: it raises the individual's job satisfaction and well-being, helps others understand and assist, and lets the organization accommodate so both sides reach top performance. After disclosure, managers and employees should **learn about the condition** rather than make unfounded assumptions.

## HR leadership

HR does more than the four systems: it designs and administers **benefits**, conducts **attitude surveys**, drafts and enforces **employment policies**, and stands on the frontline of **work–life conflicts, mediations, terminations and layoffs** — uniquely representing **both the employees' and the company's perspectives**.

**High-performance work system (HPWS)** — a group of HR practices that **work together and reinforce one another** to accomplish strategy and improve organizational outcomes over time. The relationship runs both ways: HPWS may raise performance, and higher performance provides more resources for the HPWS. HPWS matters **more when leadership is not oriented toward organizational goals**. Variants: **motivation-enhancing** (profit sharing, reward systems) · **skill-enhancing** (training and development) · **creativity-oriented** (innovative solutions for diverse customer needs) · **empowerment-oriented** (speaking up, autonomously taking charge — improving job performance).

> [!note] Toward a Better World — Kawasaki Heavy Industries
> Kawasaki aligned its HR strategy with its **ESG** goals under the value *"support for the next generation"*: the **Gyomu Mokuhyo Kyoyuka** ("Sharing Business Targets") programme of bi-annual supervisor–subordinate meetings; an apprenticeship pairing credentialed **"grand master" specialists** with newer employees; an annual **technical skills contest** gathering young employees worldwide at the Akashi Works; and two learning campuses — **Takumi Juku** (Harima Works) and **Manabiya** (Akashi Works). Outward-facing: the **Kawasaki Good Times World** museum (~200,000 visitors a year) and **Handicraft and Experiment Courses** for children.
>
> Why involve HR in ESG? Research finds ESG has a **major impact on workforce sentiment** and matters increasingly to recruitment, selection and retention; a survey of 170 board directors suggests **performance management systems should help meet ESG goals**. Kawasaki reports **under 2% annual turnover** with over two-thirds of the workforce interested in continued employment.

**Communicating HR practices.** It is **not enough to have a practice in place** — HR must let employees know about it and explain the implications. When the whole system is communicated, employees feel they can **control and manage what they get out of work**, and come to see the HR philosophy as **an expression of the employer's concern**, raising commitment, retention and engagement.

> [!important] Perception is the mechanism
> In a large food-service organization, work-group perceptions of HR practices predicted **OCB, commitment and intention to remain** — **but only if employees were aware the practices existed**. And the *attribution* matters: employees who think practices exist **to improve performance and benefit workers** reciprocate with greater commitment and performance; those who think the same practices exist **to exploit workers** do not. Effects are also stronger when employees feel **motivated**, and employees who understand the **purpose** of a performance management system use it more effectively.

**Drafting and enforcing employment policies.** **Policies differ from benefits**: benefits set working conditions, policies give **guidelines for behaviour**. A company may provide a break room for mothers of young children (benefit), but a **policy** is needed for where feeding may happen, acceptable timing, and where milk is stored. Any policy needs **enforcement** — HR sets consequences for infractions and often enforces them.

Sometimes HR must act **even when the employee's direct manager disagrees**, especially on legal compliance. The **unpaid intern** example: the Labor Department requires that unpaid interns receive a vocational education experience and that their work **not profit the employer**. An intern on the film *Black Swan* sued for minimum wage violations and a US District Court ruled he was improperly classified — sparking similar claims against NBC Universal, Fox, Viacom and others, often settled out of court. The burden still falls on interns to litigate.

## Key takeaways

- **HR and OB are inextricably linked** — HR practices shape behaviour and behaviour shapes HR practices, endlessly.
- **Applicant attraction** is driven most by **perceived fit and impressions of the recruiter** early on; vague job requirements backfire.
- **Referral hiring is ubiquitous (61%) but evidence is mixed** — powerful referrers make everyone else judge the hire harshly.
- Selection runs **initial → substantive → contingent**, and the most-used methods are often the least valid: **unstructured interviews, applications, résumés and cover letters**.
- **Cognitive ability tests are arguably the best single predictor** but produce **the most disparate impact** — hence only 16–18% usage.
- **Performance-simulation tests have the highest validity and face validity** because they duplicate the job.
- **Structured interviews** limit subjectivity; unstructured ones gather biased data.
- **Drug tests** have a positivity rate under 6% and reduced absenteeism and turnover but not accidents or injuries — validity evidence is weak.
- Training's number one goal is **transfer**; **ADDIE** structures it, **active** and **interactive** learning engage the trainee, and **e-learning** is the fastest-growing method.
- Performance management should be **continuous, behaviour-focused, multi-source, developmental not punitive**, with **due process** and **bias interrupters**.
- **Reasonable accommodations** require disclosure, which employees avoid for fear of stigma — though disclosure generally helps everyone.
- **HPWS practices reinforce one another** — but only work when employees **know about them** and attribute them to benefit rather than exploitation.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Applicant attraction** | The degree to which someone is drawn toward an organization, intends to apply, and would accept an offer |
| **Referral hiring** | Hiring based on the manager's own prior experience with the person or a referrer's recommendation |
| **Realistic job previews** | A job tryout demonstrating to job seekers what they would actually be doing |
| **Human capital resources** | A work unit's capacities from the collective knowledge, skills, abilities and other resources of the workforce |
| **Initial / substantive / contingent selection** | Rough cuts on basic qualifications / determining the best qualified / final checks before the offer |
| **Work sample tests** | Hands-on simulations of part or all of the work applicants must perform |
| **Assessment centers** | Off-site sets of performance-simulation tests evaluating managerial potential |
| **Situational judgment tests** | Tests asking how applicants would perform in job situations, compared to high performers' answers |
| **Structured / unstructured interviews** | Planned interviews gathering job-related information / short casual improvised interviews |
| **Transfer of training** | Using the knowledge, skills and abilities learned in training on the job |
| **ISD / ADDIE** | Formal training design: Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluation |
| **Critical incidents** | Evaluating the specific behaviours that separate effective from ineffective job execution |
| **Graphic rating scale** | Rating performance factors on an incremental scale |
| **BARS** | Scales combining critical incidents and graphic rating scales, anchored in actual job behaviour |
| **Forced comparison / group order ranking / individual ranking** | Explicit comparison to others / placing employees into classifications like quartiles / rank-ordering best to worst |
| **High-performance work system (HPWS)** | HR practices that work together and reinforce one another to improve organizational outcomes |
