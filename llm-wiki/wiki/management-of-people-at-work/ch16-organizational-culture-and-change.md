# Chapter 16 — Organizational Culture and Change

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch15-foundations-of-organization-structure|Ch 15]] · Next: [[ch17-human-resource-systems|Ch 17 — Human Resource Systems]]

---

## Core summary

**Organizational culture** is a system of shared meaning held by members that distinguishes the organization from others — its **values, beliefs and assumptions**. Put into practice, those ① **filter what employees pay attention to** ② are **physically manifested** as material symbols and stories ③ form the **foundation for shared meaning**.

> [!important] Culture is descriptive, not evaluative
> Culture research asks how employees **perceive** the organization — does it encourage teamwork? reward innovation? stifle initiative? **Job satisfaction is evaluative** — how employees *feel* about the organization's expectations and reward practices.
>
> And it is powerful: **88% of millennials** told Gallup they would stay more than five years if satisfied with the company's **purpose or mission**. But changing it is hard — as one CEO put it, *"Changing management without changing the culture is tantamount to merely rearranging the deck chairs on a slowly sinking cruise ship."*

The chapter runs: **what culture is** → **how employees learn it** → **how it is created and sustained** → **what cultures do** (including climate) → **how to influence culture** → **change**, its resistance, and four **approaches to managing it**.

## What organizational culture is

### Two questions researchers ask

- **Do organizations *have* cultures?** — trying to identify what values and beliefs **descriptively separate** one organization from another
- **What *are* organizations' cultures?** — trying to describe a specific culture through its **stories, rituals, material symbols and language**, which are notoriously hard to measure and tap deep assumptions often graspable only by an insider

### Three frameworks

**Competing values** — four cultures, distinguished by **internal vs external focus** and **flexibility vs stability**:

| Culture | Based on | Employees value | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Clan** | Human affiliation | Attachment, collaboration, trust, support | Internal, flexible |
| **Adhocracy** | Change | Growth, variety, attention to detail, stimulation, autonomy | External, flexible |
| **Market** | Achievement | Communication, competence, competition | External, stable |
| **Hierarchy** | Stability | Communication, formalization, routine | Internal, stable |

> [!tip] Match culture to objective
> Most cultures positively affect attitudes, performance, innovation, quality and efficiency. But **clan and market cultures align best with customer-oriented outcomes**, while **hierarchy is best for profitability and revenue growth**.

**Organizational Culture Inventory** — three categories: **constructive** (affiliation, encouragement, achievement) · **passive-defensive** (avoid accountability, seek validation and approval, conventional) · **aggressive-defensive** (competitive, perfectionist, power-oriented).

**Organizational Culture Profile (OCP)** — employees sort values by how closely they represent their organization, across eight dimensions: **innovation · attention to detail · decisiveness · team-orientation · outcome-orientation · aggressiveness · supportiveness · rewards-emphasis**.

### Uniform, dominant and strong

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Dominant culture** | Expresses the **core values** most members share, giving the organization its distinct personality |
| **Subcultures** | Minicultures within an organization, typically defined by department or geographical separation — arising from common problems or experiences. Sometimes **strong enough to subtly reject the "official" culture** |
| **Strong culture** | Core values **intensely held and widely shared**. The more members accept the core values and the greater their commitment, the greater the influence on behaviour |

Statements about organizational culture are valid **only if people with different backgrounds and at different levels describe the culture in similar terms**. A strong culture builds cohesiveness, loyalty, meaning and commitment, which **lessen the propensity to leave** — and across **~90,000 employees in 137 organizations**, culture strength related to financial outcomes **where there was a strong sense of mission and high employee involvement**.

> [!note] Culture can be changed — slowly
> Wisconsin's Wellspring nursing home network shifted to caregiver collaboration, education, accountability and empowerment: **fewer standards infractions, higher retention**, and for patients **fewer bedridden residents, less use of restraints and psychoactive medication, less incontinence and fewer tube feedings**.
>
> Dynegy's CEO gave up a \$15,000 marble desk and office suite for a **cubicle shared with all 235 headquarters employees**, visited facilities, trained "culture champions," reinstated performance reviews, and **banned smartphones from meetings** — with turnover falling from 8% to 5.8%.

## How employees learn culture

| Vehicle | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Stories** | Anchor the present in the past and legitimize current practices — founders, rule breaking, rags-to-riches, workforce reductions, relocations, reactions to past mistakes, coping. Henry Ford II reminding executives *"It's my name that's on the building."* Nike executives telling how Bill Bowerman poured rubber into a **waffle iron** to make a better running shoe. Emotionally charged, inspirational stories are **transformational, persuasive and motivational** |
| **Rituals** | Repetitive sequences of activities expressing and reinforcing key values — which goals matter and which people are important versus expendable. Kimpton Hotels' **Housekeeping Olympics** with blindfolded bedmaking and vacuum races. Rituals work because they **inspire a sense of control and reduce anxiety** |
| **Material symbols** *(artifacts)* | Headquarters layout, executive cars, corporate aircraft, office size, furnishings, perks, attire, the social media page. They convey who is important, how egalitarian management wants to be, and what behaviour is appropriate. Threadless holds meetings in an **Airstream camper inside a converted FedEx warehouse**; IDEO's Palo Alto office has a play lab with an **airplane wing through the wall**; Genentech gives **six weeks' paid leave per six years of service** |
| **Language** | Unique terms for equipment, officers, key individuals, suppliers, customers and products. Once assimilated, acronyms and jargon become **a common denominator uniting members** |

> [!warning] Language cuts both ways
> Many now see jargon and acronyms as **impediments** — creating confusion and inefficiency where plain language would not. Worse, language can be a **veil for portraying unethical behaviour positively**: a leaked message from luggage company Away's CEO reframed cutting PTO and flexible work as *"the career skill of accountability… I hope everyone in this group appreciates the thoughtfulness I've put into creating this career development opportunity."*
>
> Language also shapes discrimination: mission statements emphasizing **thoughtful consideration** predicted whether a company faced EEOC discrimination suits, and organizations using **stigmatizing labels for COVID-19** produced more discriminatory experiences for Asian American employees.

## Creating and sustaining culture

### How culture begins — the founders

Culture creation happens in **three ways**: founders **hire and retain employees who think and feel as they do** · they **socialize employees** to their way of thinking · and their **behavior encourages employees to identify with them** and internalize their beliefs. When the organization succeeds, **the founders' personalities become embedded in the culture** — and the influence is strong enough to transmit to **spinoffs** that break off as entirely new organizations.

Examples: Hyundai's fierce, disciplined, authoritarian style mirroring founder Chung Ju-Yung (with the shift to business casual attire signalling later culture change) · Bill Gates at Microsoft · Ingvar Kamprad at IKEA · Herb Kelleher at Southwest · Fred Smith at FedEx · Richard Branson at Virgin.

The formation chain: **founders' philosophy → selection criteria → organizational culture**, with **top management** and **socialization** feeding in. In the most comprehensive study to date — **500,000+ employees across 26,000+ organizations** — culture was strongly related to **current leadership, practices and structures**.

### Keeping it alive — three forces

**Selection.** The final hiring decision is heavily influenced by judgment of **how well candidates fit**, identifying people whose values match. It runs **both ways** — applicants who see a conflict can withdraw, **or fake their way in** by feigning shared values. W. L. Gore puts applicants through extensive team interviews to check they can handle the uncertainty, flexibility and teamwork standard in Gore plants.

**Top management.** Through words and behaviour, senior executives establish norms about whether risk taking is desirable, how much freedom managers give, what to wear, what is rewarded.

> [!important] Leadership should complement culture, not duplicate it
> Research on hundreds of CEOs and TMT members found better outcomes when culture and leadership styles are **complementary in content and not redundant**. Where leadership behaviours and cultural values are **redundant**, leaders have **less** effect; where leaders **provide what the culture lacks**, they substitute for the missing element. Hence a **transformational leader is more effective than a transactional one in a bureaucratic, hierarchical culture**.

**Socialization** — the process enabling new employees to acquire the social knowledge and skills to adapt to the culture. Gallup found that among employees who **strongly agreed their onboarding was exceptional**: 81% felt satisfied with their organization as a place to work · 77% felt their job was as good or better than expected · 70% felt fully prepared and supported to excel · 61% felt they had the best possible job.

**Three stages:**

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Prearrival** | Everyone arrives with values, attitudes and expectations. **How accurately people judge the culture before joining**, and how positive, adaptive and proactive their personalities are, become critical predictors of adjustment |
| **Encounter** | The new member confronts the possibility that expectations about job, coworkers, boss and organization **may differ from reality**. At the extreme, disillusionment leads to resignation. Reduced by realistic previews and by **friendship ties** — newcomers are more committed when friendly coworkers help them learn the ropes. But **undesirable behaviours transmit here too**: in southern Chinese manufacturing sales departments, newcomers modelled heavy drinking with clients, raising work–family conflict and turnover risk |
| **Metamorphosis** | The new member changes and adjusts to job, work group and organization. Complete when they have **internalized and accepted the norms**, are confident in their competence, feel trusted and valued, understand the rules and informal practices, and know how their work will be judged |

**Five socialization practice dimensions:** formal vs informal · individual vs collective · fixed vs variable · serial vs random · investiture vs divestiture.

Two bundles: **institutional practices** (formal, collective, fixed, serial, divestiture) strip away differences and produce standardized predictable behaviour, encouraging **person–organization fit, commitment and role clarity** — common in police and fire departments. **Individual practices** (informal, individual, variable, random, investiture) produce **role innovation, motivation and social integration** — used in R&D, advertising and filmmaking.

> [!warning] Honeymoons and hangovers
> Studies measuring attitudes over the first months document a pattern: the **initial adjustment period is marked by decreases in job satisfaction** as idealized hopes meet organizational reality. Social support from supervisors and coworkers is **gradually withdrawn** over the first weeks as everyone returns to business as usual, and **role conflict and role overload rise** — with the largest increases producing the largest drops in commitment and satisfaction. It is hardest for people energized by interpersonal interaction (extroverts), at least short-term.
>
> The fix: **"too many" socialization practices make things worse**; what helps is **a great deal of social support**.

## What cultures do

**Five functions:** ① a **boundary-defining** role, creating distinctions between organizations ② conveying a **sense of identity** ③ facilitating **commitment to something larger than self-interest** ④ enhancing the **stability of the social system** — the social glue providing standards for what to say and do ⑤ a **sense-making and control mechanism** guiding attitudes and behaviour.

Culture predicts the **strategies** organizations adopt, the **structure** they assume, the **leadership styles** used and the **practices** adopted.

> [!note] Decentralization makes culture more important — and harder
> Reducing formal authority and control systems means **different forces pull culture in non-uniform directions**. Employees organized in teams may show **greater allegiance to their team and its values than to the organization**.

### Culture creates climate

**Organizational climate** — the shared perceptions members hold about their organization and work environment, directed at the **policies, practices and procedures** they experience.

> [!important] The onion
> **If an organization is like an onion, the culture is at the core, and the climates are on the surface layers.** Climate is the readily observable **behavioral evidence** for culture — it links what the organization values and believes with **explicit practices, policies and procedures**, and with **what employees perceive is actually supported, rewarded and practised**.
>
> The COVID illustration: a chain restaurant's commercial saying *"the health and safety of our employees and customers is our number one priority"* tells you it has a **safety culture**. Whether the location you visit has masks, distancing and surface cleaning tells you its **safety climate** — and two locations of the same chain can differ like night and day, because **climates vary just as subcultures do**.

Across dozens of studies, **positive climates were strongly related to job satisfaction, involvement, commitment and motivation** — and to customer satisfaction and financial performance. Dimensions studied include safety, innovation, creativity, communication, warmth and support, involvement, justice, diversity and customer service. **Safety climate** demonstrably decreases documented injuries, because climate shapes the habits people adopt: where there is a climate of safety, everyone wears the gear even if individually they would rarely think about it.

Climates **interact** — worker empowerment produces higher performance where there is also a climate of **personal accountability** — and they can **clash**: simultaneously encouraging caution and risk-taking produces dissonance and **less commitment**.

**Espoused vs enacted.** Employees distinguish what they hear supported in meetings, memos and rule books from what they **actually see enacted**. Climate is a function of what employees perceive is **rewarded** — and when context, culture and climate **align**, climates are enacted and produce positive outcomes.

### Culture as asset and liability

| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Ethical culture** | The shared concept of right and wrong behaviour, developed over time, shaping members' ethical decision making. It espouses **clear standards modelled by leadership**, and people are **open to discussing moral issues** and reinforced for ethical behaviour. It also **reduces burnout and raises engagement** |
| **Ethical climate** | Climates emphasizing **self-interest** are negatively associated with satisfaction and commitment and positively associated with **turnover intentions, workplace bullying and deviance**. Climates emphasizing **caring and rules** bring greater satisfaction and reduce turnover intentions, bullying and dysfunction |
| **Sustainable culture** | Maintaining practices over long periods because the supporting tools and structures are not damaged by the process — Seventh Generation's name from the Iroquois proverb *"In our every deliberation, we must consider the impact of our decisions on the next seven generations"* |

**Toxic cultures.** Cultures that passively **avoid conflict** are less creative. **Results-oriented cultures** are not toxic in themselves, but become so **without an ethical culture** to keep employees from disengaging from the ethical aspects of their work. **Lean management** cultures can become **job insecurity cultures**, where constant fear of losing one's job makes employees skip important behaviours — including **safety** — to work faster.

> [!warning] Culture clashes in mergers
> Cultural compatibility has become the **primary concern** in acquisitions, ahead of financial advantage and product synergy. **Bain found 70% of mergers failed to increase shareholder value**; Hay Group found **over 90% of European mergers failed to reach financial goals**. As one consultant put it: *"One of the biggest failings is people. The people at Company A have a different way of doing things from Company B… you can't find commonality in goals."*
>
> The wider point: there are **many more business success stories due to excellent cultures than success stories despite bad cultures, and almost none because of bad ones**. One mechanism runs through customers — across ~100 dealerships over six years, positive culture improved sales performance **by increasing customer satisfaction**.

## Influencing organizational cultures

### A positive culture

**Positive organizational culture** — emphasizes building on employee strengths, rewards more than it punishes, and emphasizes individual vitality and growth.

- **Build on strengths.** Peter Drucker: *"Most Americans do not know what their strengths are."* Gallup finds employees who use their strengths daily are **six times more likely to be engaged**.
- **Reward more than punish.** Managers withhold praise fearing employees will coast or that praise is unvalued. Charles Schwab: *"I have yet to find the person, however great or exalted in station, who did not do better work and put forth a greater effort under a spirit of approval than they would ever do under a spirit of criticism."* Asked what would cause them to produce great work, the majority of O.C. Tanner's employees said **more personal recognition**.
- **Encourage vitality and growth.** Recognize the difference between a **job and a career** — supporting not only what the employee contributes but **how the organization can make the employee more effective**.

> [!warning] Toxic positivity
> Not all national cultures value positivity as much as the US, and there are limits within it. *"Promoting a social orthodoxy of positiveness focuses on a particular constellation of desirable states and traits but, in so doing, can stigmatize those who fail to fit the template."* Taken to the extreme it becomes **toxic positivity** — imposing unrealistic and psychologically damaging pressure to be perfect while **avoiding perfectly natural negative states**.

### An ethical culture — five principles

1. **Be a visible role model** — employees benchmark against top management's actions, but **everyone** can model
2. **Communicate ethical expectations** — a code of ethics stating primary values and the judgment rules to follow
3. **Provide ethical training** — seminars, workshops and programmes reinforcing standards and addressing dilemmas
4. **Visibly reward ethical acts and punish unethical ones** — evaluate decisions against the code, and **review the means as well as the ends**
5. **Provide protective mechanisms** — formal channels to discuss dilemmas and report unethical behaviour **without fear of reprimand**: ethical counselors, ombudspeople, ethics officers

It must **start at the top**: where top management emphasizes strong ethical values, supervisors practise ethical leadership and line employees show **lower deviance and higher cooperation**. Conversely, auditors who perceived **pressure from leaders to behave unethically** reported increased intentions to do so. And it flows upward too — employees whose ethical values match their department's are **more likely to be promoted**.

### An innovative culture

**Four structural sources:** **organic structures** (lower vertical differentiation, formalization and centralization, so more flexibility, adaptation and cross-fertilization) · **contingent rewards** for creativity, especially with **feedback plus autonomy** · **slack resources**, letting the organization afford innovations, bear the cost of instituting them and **absorb failures** · **high interunit communication** through committees, task forces and cross-functional teams.

**Contextual features:** encourage experimentation and **reward both successes and failures, celebrating mistakes** · share a common vision and underlying goals · be cohesive, mutually supportive and encouraging · leverage HR systems — training and development, **high job security so employees do not fear being fired for mistakes**, and encouraging people to become change agents. Nationally, **strong social norms with low tolerance for deviation stifle innovation**, while close cross-cultural personal relationships spark it.

**Idea champions** — individuals who take an innovation and actively and enthusiastically promote it, build support, overcome resistance and ensure implementation. They share personality characteristics: **extremely high self-confidence, persistence, energy and risk-taking**, plus transformational-leadership traits. Watch the other audiences too: **doubters** will probably never come around, **converts** warm to the idea over time, and **defectors** cool on it — so vary the message accordingly.

## Change

**Change** — when things become different than the way they were. **Planned change** — change activities that are **proactive, intentional and goal-oriented**, with two goals: improving the organization's **ability to adapt** to its environment, and **changing employee behavior**.

**Six forces for change:** the **nature of the workforce** (cultural diversity, aging population, immigration and outsourcing) · **technology** · **economic shocks** · **competition** (global competitors, mergers, regulation) · **social trends** · **world politics**.

**Change agents** — people who act as catalysts and assume responsibility for managing change activities. They **see a future others have not identified** and can motivate, invent and implement that vision. They can be managers or nonmanagers, current or new employees, or outside consultants — and different agents suit different stages: **formal leaders early** for clarity and expectations, **peer change agents later** to motivate long-term adoption.

### Resistance to change

| Individual sources | Organizational sources |
|---|---|
| **Habit** — programmed responses become resistance when change demands new ones | **Structural inertia** — selection processes and formalized regulations exist to produce stability, which counterbalances change |
| **Security** — change threatens feelings of safety | **Limited focus of change** — interdependent subsystems mean a limited change in one gets **nullified by the larger system** |
| **Economic factors** — fear of not performing new tasks to previous standards, especially where pay is tied to productivity | **Group inertia** — group norms constrain even individuals who want to change |
| **Fear of the unknown** — change substitutes ambiguity and uncertainty | **Threat to expertise** — new patterns threaten specialized groups' expertise |
| **Selective information processing** — hearing what we want, ignoring what challenges the world we have created | **Threat to established power relationships** — any redistribution of decision authority threatens long-established power |

> [!warning] The dangerous kind is the quiet kind
> Resistance can be **overt, implicit, immediate or deferred**. Overt and immediate resistance — complaints, work slowdowns, strike threats — is **easiest to manage**. The challenge is **implicit or deferred** resistance: loss of loyalty or motivation, increased errors or absenteeism, surfacing weeks, months or years later. **Deferred actions cloud the link** between change and reaction, so a single change of little inherent impact can be the **straw that breaks the camel's back** because resistance to earlier changes was stockpiled.
>
> And note: **not all change is good**. Rapid transformational change is risky — speed leads to bad decisions, and initiators often fail to see the full magnitude or true costs. In acquisitions, **a slow rather than fast transition** reduces employee and middle-manager resistance. Meanwhile **resistance can be positive** when it produces open discussion and debate — preferable to silence, and a signal that people are engaged.

**Eight tactics for overcoming resistance** — the first four:

1. **Communication** — communicate a rationale **balancing the interests of various stakeholders** (shareholders, employees, community, customers) rather than shareholders only. Formal information sessions reduce anxiety; high-quality information raises commitment. **Framing matters**: change framed as **threatening** produces non-compliance, while change framed as a **challenge or a positive influence** produces championing, compliance and engagement.
2. **Participation** — it is hard to resist a decision you participated in. Reduces resistance, obtains commitment and improves decision quality — **provided participants have the expertise to contribute**. Costs: potential for a poor solution, heavy time consumption, and **heightened resistance where employees already have too many job demands**.
3. **Building support and commitment** — low emotional commitment produces resistance and preference for the status quo, while commitment to the organization as a whole produces acceptance. Provide coaching and training to cope, develop a positive climate, and where anxieties are high, offer **counselling, new-skills training or a short paid leave of absence**.
4. **Develop positive relationships** — people accept change more readily when they **trust the change agents and see them as legitimate**.

> [!note] Who can actually drive radical change
> Change often comes from **outside change agents, employees new to the organization** (less invested in the status quo), or **managers slightly removed from the main power structure**. Long-tenured senior managers are often **major impediments** — and when forced to change, they implement merely **incremental** change, because radical change is too threatening. Which is why boards recognizing the need for rapid, radical change **frequently turn to outside candidates**.

## Four approaches to managing change

### 1 · Lewin's three-step model

**Unfreezing** the status quo → **movement** to a desired end state → **refreezing** the change to make it permanent.

The status quo is an **equilibrium**. Unfreezing happens by **increasing the driving forces** (which direct behaviour away from the status quo), **decreasing the restraining forces** (which hinder movement away from equilibrium), or both. Firms **successful in the past** meet more restraining forces, because people question the need to change. Once movement begins, **keep the momentum** — organizations that build up to change do worse than those that get to and through movement quickly. Without **refreezing**, change is short-lived and people revert.

> [!warning] Three criticisms
> ① Like Maslow, Lewin assumes change happens **sequentially, in order, always progressing**. ② The model implies **perfect agency** for managers — do change agents really know where they are, where people are, and where they are going, in organizations this fluid? ③ The **"freezing" language** is problematic: time keeps moving, freezing may never happen, and new changes dynamically interrupt current ones. Modern approaches instead consider **timing, pacing, rhythm and the nature of the change agents**.

### 2 · Kotter's eight-step plan

Built on Lewin, addressing the common failures — no sense of urgency, no coalition, no vision, poor communication, not anchoring changes to culture, not removing obstacles, not providing short-term goals, and **declaring victory too soon**.

1. Establish a **sense of urgency** with a compelling reason for change
2. Form a **coalition** with enough power to lead the change
3. Create a **new vision** and strategies for achieving it
4. **Communicate the vision** throughout the organization
5. **Empower others to act** by removing barriers and encouraging risk taking
6. Plan for, create and reward **short-term wins**
7. **Consolidate improvements**, reassess and adjust
8. **Reinforce** the changes by demonstrating the link between new behaviours and success

Steps **1–4 are Lewin's unfreezing**, **5–7 are movement**, **8 is refreezing** — Kotter's contribution is the detailed guide.

### 3 · Action research

A change process based on the **systematic collection of data** and selection of a change action based on what the data indicate — a **scientific methodology** for planned change. Five steps paralleling the scientific method:

**Diagnosis** (gathering information about problems, concerns and needed changes from members) → **Analysis** (what problems do people focus on? what patterns?) → **Feedback** (sharing findings with employees) → **Action** (employees and change agent carry out the identified actions **together**) → **Evaluation** (using the initial data as a benchmark).

**Two benefits:** it is **problem-focused** — the type of problem determines the type of change action, rather than the change agent arriving with a solution. And it **lowers resistance**, because employees are so thoroughly engaged; once they have participated in feedback, **the change process takes on momentum of its own**.

### 4 · Organizational development (OD)

A collection of planned change interventions, built on **humanistic–democratic values**, seeking to improve organizational effectiveness **and employee well-being**. OD values human and organizational growth, collaborative and participative processes, and a spirit of inquiry — placing heavy emphasis on the **subjective ways people see and make sense of their work environment**.

| Intervention | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Process consultation** | An outside consultant helps a client (usually a manager) understand process events and identify what needs improvement — workflow, informal relationships, formal communication channels. **The consultant does not solve the problem** but coaches the client to solve their own after jointly diagnosing it, so **the client develops the skill and keeps it after the consultant leaves** — and, having participated in both diagnosis and alternatives, is **less resistant to the plan chosen** |
| **Team building** | High interaction among team members to increase trust and openness, improve coordination and raise performance — typically goal setting, developing interpersonal relations, **role analysis** clarifying each member's role and responsibilities, and team process analysis |
| **Intergroup development** | Seeks to change groups' **attitudes, stereotypes and perceptions about each other**, addressing dysfunctional conflict between groups — resembling diversity training but focused on group rather than demographic differences |

## Key takeaways

- **Culture is descriptive** (how members perceive the organization), **satisfaction is evaluative** (how they feel about it).
- Four **competing values** cultures — clan, adhocracy, market, hierarchy — and the right one depends on the objective: **clan/market for customers, hierarchy for profitability**.
- Culture is learned through **stories, rituals, material symbols and language** — and language can be **a veil for unethical behaviour**.
- **Founders create culture**; **selection, top management and socialization** sustain it. Leadership works best where it **complements rather than duplicates** the culture.
- Socialization runs **prearrival → encounter → metamorphosis**, with a documented **honeymoon-and-hangover** pattern — the fix is **social support**, not more socialization practices.
- **Culture creates climate**: culture is the onion's core, climates the surface layers — and climate is what people perceive is actually **rewarded**.
- Culture becomes a **liability** through toxicity (results without ethics; lean management becoming job insecurity) and through **culture clashes in mergers**, where 70–90% fail.
- A **positive culture** builds strengths, rewards more than punishes, and encourages growth — but **toxic positivity** is a real limit.
- Change meets **individual** (habit, security, economics, fear, selective processing) and **organizational** (structural inertia, limited focus, group inertia, threats to expertise and power) resistance — and **deferred, implicit resistance is the dangerous kind**.
- Four approaches: **Lewin** (unfreeze–move–refreeze, now criticized), **Kotter** (eight steps), **action research** (data-driven and resistance-lowering), **OD** (process consultation, team building, intergroup development).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Organizational culture** | A system of shared meaning distinguishing an organization from others — values, beliefs, underlying assumptions |
| **Dominant culture / core values / subcultures** | The core values most members share / the primary values accepted throughout / minicultures by department or geography |
| **Strong culture** | Core values intensely held and widely shared |
| **Rituals / material symbols** | Repetitive activities expressing and reinforcing key values / physical artifacts symbolizing values and beliefs |
| **Socialization** | The process enabling new employees to acquire the knowledge and skills to adapt to the culture |
| **Prearrival / encounter / metamorphosis stage** | Arriving with expectations / confronting the gap between expectation and reality / changing and adjusting |
| **Organizational climate** | Shared perceptions of the organization's policies, practices and procedures |
| **Ethical culture** | The shared concept of right and wrong behaviour in the workplace |
| **Positive organizational culture** | Builds on employee strengths, rewards more than punishes, emphasizes vitality and growth |
| **Idea champions** | Individuals who actively promote an innovation, build support, overcome resistance and ensure implementation |
| **Change / planned change** | When things become different / proactive, intentional, goal-oriented change activities |
| **Change agents** | People who act as catalysts and take responsibility for managing change activities |
| **Driving forces / restraining forces** | Forces directing behaviour away from the status quo / forces hindering movement from equilibrium |
| **Action research** | Change based on systematic data collection and a change action chosen from the analysis |
| **Organizational development (OD)** | Planned change interventions on humanistic–democratic values, improving effectiveness and well-being |
| **Process consultation / team building** | A consultant helping a client understand and improve process events / high interaction to increase trust and openness |
