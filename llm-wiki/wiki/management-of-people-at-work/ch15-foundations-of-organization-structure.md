# Chapter 15 — Foundations of Organization Structure

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch14-conflict-and-negotiation|Ch 14]] · Next: [[ch16-organizational-culture-and-change|Ch 16 — Culture and Change]]

## Core summary

**Organizational structure** defines how job tasks are formally divided, grouped and coordinated. As one HR director frames it: *"Structure follows strategy… structure controls behavior… structure supports execution."*

Structure also has an equity dimension: **systematic biases and structural barriers can prevent members of underrepresented groups from advancing**, so organizations have a responsibility to identify what formally *and informally* limits advancement.

The chapter runs: **seven structural elements** → **three classic frameworks** (simple, bureaucracy, matrix) → **three newer designs** (virtual, team, circular) → **downsizing** → **why structures differ** (strategy, size, technology, environment, institutions) → **effects on employee behavior**.

## The seven elements

### 1 · Work specialization

The degree to which tasks are **subdivided into separate jobs**, each step completed by a separate individual. The Ben & Jerry's illustration is a chain of specialists — shipping and receiving coordinators → mix master → pasteurization and homogenization specialist → flavor vat technicians → chunk feeder and variegator → freezer workers → quality assurance specialists — producing **a million pints a day**.

**Benefits:** efficient use of skills, improvement through repetition, less time lost switching tasks and tools, cheaper and easier to find and train workers, and it encourages custom inventions and machinery. **Microspecialization** (Amazon Mechanical Turk, TopCoder) delegates tiny programming, data-processing or evaluation tasks to a global network assembled by a program manager.

> [!warning] The curve turns
> Specialization can be carried too far, at which point **human diseconomies** surface — boredom, fatigue, stress, low productivity, inferior quality, increased absenteeism and high turnover — **more than offsetting the economic advantages**. Productivity can then be increased by **enlarging rather than narrowing** the scope: variety of activities, a whole and complete job, and teams with interchangeable skills, producing higher output **and** higher satisfaction.
>
> Its success also depends on **task stability** — where duties change constantly, specialization is less desirable.

### 2 · Departmentalization

The basis on which jobs are grouped.

| Basis | How it works | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **Functional** | Group by function performed — engineering, accounting, manufacturing, HR, supply chain | Efficiency from putting specialists together, and easier development of expertise — but **coordination across units is a problem**, and infighting within and between units reduces motivation |
| **Product or service** | Each major product sector under an executive with complete global responsibility (P&G: baby care, beauty, grooming, fabric care…) | Increased **accountability**, since all activities for a product sit under one manager — but the unit still needs a **broader understanding of the organization** outside its own specialization |
| **Geographical** | Each function allocated to regions or markets (Toyota restructured this way) | Valuable where customers are geographically scattered with similar needs within locations — but regional teams form their own identities and practices, so **inter-region communication and knowledge sharing need special care** |
| **Process** | Structure around employee, product or customer processes (an infosec firm: policy management → network security → identity and access → operations) | Helps organizations better offer products and services, even when the process must occasionally change |
| **Customer** | Group by the customer served — government, industrial, private | Focuses on distinct customer bases |

**Microsoft's restructuring history** shows how unstable this choice is: customer departmentalization for years → **functional** in June 2013 to foster innovation → more changes within a year (PowerPoint, Excel and Access teams reorganized into content-creation and data-visualization teams) → now primarily **product**, with smaller groups by function and geography.

Divisional structures (product, geographical, process, customer) **facilitate coordination** and give clear responsibility for everything related to a product, **at the cost of duplicated functions**. Marketing experts in Spain and China can each bring cultural perspective to a campaign — but the organization is paying twice for the same task.

### 3 · Chain of command

An **unbroken line of authority** from the top to the lowest echelon, clarifying who reports to whom. Two supporting concepts:

- **Authority** — the rights inherent in a managerial position to give orders and expect them obeyed. Where most authority is vested in one person, **LMX becomes more important** because the leader's buy-in is what gets things done.
- **Unity of command** — a person should have **one and only one superior**. If broken, employees face conflicting demands and priorities.

> [!note] The chain of command is fading — but not gone
> Low-level employees now access in seconds information once available only to top managers, and many are empowered to make decisions once reserved for management. Self-managed and cross-functional teams make authority and unity of command look less relevant.
>
> Yet **59% of surveyed managers** agreed: *"There is an imaginary line in my company's organizational chart. Strategy is created by people above this line, while strategy is executed by people below the line."* The same survey found lower-level employees' **buy-in to the overall strategy was inhibited** by relying on hierarchy for decisions. And the biggest drawback: authority and unity of command **negatively affect idea generation and strain innovation implementation**.
>
> If you *do* report to multiple people: watch your workload, conflicting messages, who has the **final say**, and loyalty expectations — and be proactive, foster communication between your supervisors, and set boundaries and norms.

### 4 · Span of control

The number of subordinates a manager can direct efficiently and effectively — which determines the **number of levels** in the organization. **The wider the span, the fewer the levels, and the more efficient the organization.**

> [!important] The arithmetic that decides it
> Two organizations, ~4,100 operative-level employees each. A **span of 4** produces **six levels and 1,365 managers**. A **span of 8** produces **four levels and 585 managers** — two fewer levels and about **800 fewer managers**. At an average managerial salary of \$60,000, the wider span saves **\$48 million a year**.

Narrow spans allow close control, but have **three drawbacks**: they are **expensive** (more management levels), they make **vertical communication more complex** (slowing decisions and isolating upper management), and they encourage **overly tight supervision and discourage autonomy** — supervisors lose the time to provide leadership and support, so performance suffers.

The trend is toward wider spans — supported by **heavy investment in employee training**, since managers handle a wide span best when employees know their jobs well or can turn to coworkers.

### 5 · Centralization and decentralization

The degree to which **decision making is concentrated at a single point**. Centralized: top managers decide, lower managers execute. Decentralized: decisions pushed down to managers closest to the action or to workgroups. The concept covers **only formal authority**.

| | Advantage |
|---|---|
| **Centralized** | Better at avoiding **commission errors** — bad choices |
| **Decentralized** | Better at avoiding **omission errors** — lost opportunities. Acts faster, gathers more input, casts leaders as champions rather than deciders, and reduces employees' sense of alienation from decisions affecting their work |

Evidence: Finnish firms with **decentralized R&D offices in multiple locations** produced more innovation than those centralizing R&D in one office. **COVID made decentralization the default**, as decisions were delegated to local teams following local health regulations. And centralization is not limited to insiders — allocating decision authority to **external managers or highly skilled employees at contracting firms** raises the likelihood of change and innovation.

> [!warning] Both are double-edged
> Offshore sites need **localized decision making** to respond to regional opportunities, client bases and laws — plus **centralized oversight** to hold regional managers accountable; failing to balance the two harms the organization *and* its relationships with foreign governments.
>
> A study of ~3,000 US Air Force officers found negative effects of decentralization in **multiteam systems**: excessive risk seeking and coordination failures. And when **voice is centralized** — only a few people or leaders speaking up — employees' knowledge goes unused and performance falls.

### 6 · Formalization

The degree to which jobs are **standardized**. Highly formalized: minimal discretion over what to do and when and how, explicit job descriptions, many rules and clearly defined procedures, consistent uniform output. Low formalization: unprogrammed behaviours and great freedom of discretion.

Mixed evidence: it **hurts team flexibility in decentralized structures** — it does not work where duties are inherently interactive or where flexibility and innovation are needed. But **formalizing pay determinations reduces gender inequality** in most circumstances; where formalization is **fair and legitimate** (roles, duties and titles commensurate with worth) it **prevents turnover**, since people spend less effort making sense of their positions and are less burdened by coordination and clarification demands. And in **complexity and turbulence** it helps, when members are driven to collaborate to pull through a crisis.

### 7 · Boundary spanning

Individuals forming relationships **outside their formally assigned groups** — an HR executive who frequently engages with IT; an R&D member who implements ideas from production.

It prevents formal structures from becoming too rigid, and enhances **organizational and team creativity, decision making, knowledge sharing and performance**. It also works **between** organizations: transferring knowledge across boundaries, translating information, making connections, even transforming organizations through influence.

> [!tip] Two rules for boundary spanning
> **External boundary spanning is most effective when followed up with internal boundary spanning** — the effects are strongest in organizations that encourage extensive internal communication. And **leaders are not always the best spanners**: employee-to-employee relationships across companies (salesperson–client) are sometimes stronger than those between organizational leaders.
>
> **The risk:** an employee who leaves for a competitor becomes a "connection" between networks — and is **more likely to leak sensitive information**.
>
> **How to create it:** formal liaison roles or cross-area committees · development activities teaching **the jargon, methods and practices of other groups** · hiring people with experience in multiple functions · **job rotation programmes** · and drawing attention to overall organizational goals and shared identity so people feel comfortable reaching out.

## Three classic frameworks

| Framework | Definition | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple structure** | Low departmentalization, **wide spans** (flat), authority centralized in a single person, little formalization — usually two or three vertical levels | **Fast, flexible, inexpensive**, with clear accountability. Common in startups; large firms often simplify in a crisis to focus resources | Becomes inadequate as the organization grows — low formalization plus high centralization creates **information overload at the top** and slows decisions, which undoes many small businesses. And it is **risky**: everything depends on one person, whose illness can halt the organization's information and decision-making capability |
| **Bureaucracy** | Highly routine tasks through **specialization**, formalized rules and regulations, functional departmentalization, centralized authority, **narrow spans**, decisions following the chain of command | Performs standardized activities **very efficiently**: economies of scale, minimum duplication of people and equipment, a shared common language, and it can run on **less talented and less costly middle and lower managers** because rules substitute for discretion | **Specialization creates conflict** where unit perspectives override organizational goals (the production/R&D/marketing/accounting argument about "nothing happens until *we*…"). And **obsessive concern with standard procedure** — where a case does not fit "the way things are done," there is no room for modification. Bureaucracy is efficient **only** where employees confront familiar problems with programmed practices |
| **Matrix structure** | **Dual lines of authority**, combining functional and product departmentalization — employees have two supervisors. **84% of US employees** are in matrixed organizations | Facilitates coordination across complex interdependent activities; direct frequent contact between specialties **moves information to whoever needs it faster**; reduces "bureaupathologies" by limiting territorial protection; achieves economies of scale and efficient allocation of specialists | **Confusion, power struggles and stress.** Reporting to more than one boss introduces **role conflict**; unclear expectations introduce **role ambiguity**; and without unity of command, conflict between functional and product managers is common |

> [!note] Bureaucracy is not automatically the enemy of innovation
> Walmart's CEO called it "a villain"; JPMorgan Chase's called it "a disease." The data are unflattering — **28% of employees' time** goes to bureaucratic tasks, and **two-thirds of respondents** say it significantly slows decision making.
>
> But research finds employees can fulfil bureaucratic expectations with minimal frustration when doing so **lets them control the tasks that matter to them** and when the bureaucracy is a **shared burden rather than an individual one**. Rather than eliminating it, **connecting bureaucratic tasks to benefits employees value** keeps it from damaging innovation. (Where it becomes excessive, liberation is warranted — reduced government funding requirements protected Pfizer's scientists from needless delays, contributing to a COVID vaccine in **six months**.)

**Spotify's agile matrix** shows how culture offsets the matrix's costs. Values of agility, flexibility, fluidity, collaboration and clear direction support an organization of **squads** and **tribes** (focused on products/services), **chapters** (people doing similar jobs across products — database administrators, front-office developers), and **guilds** — amorphous collections of members sharing an interest they want to improve. This lets Spotify **assemble squads for temporary objectives without worrying about who reports to whom**.

## Three newer designs

### Virtual (network) structure

A **small core organization that outsources major business functions** — highly centralized, with little or no departmentalization, opening boundaries between organizations. *Why own when you can rent?*

The prototype is filmmaking. In Hollywood's golden era, vertically integrated studios employed thousands of full-time specialists. Today many films are made by individuals and small companies coming together **project by project** — staffing each project with the best-suited talent, minimizing bureaucratic overhead, and **lessening long-term risk because there is no long term**. Netflix's *Yasuke* assembled a creator, a lead voice actor, the MAPPA studio in Tokyo and a Grammy-nominated composer for one series.

**Four network forms:**

| Form | What is outsourced | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Franchise** | Sales and service to franchise units, with managers, systems and experts at the centre | 7-Eleven, McDonald's, Dunkin' Donuts. Franchisees **do not collaborate**, are hard for the centre to influence, and may **compete for central resources**. Persistent tension between the organization's desire for **standardization** and franchisees' desire for **autonomy** |
| **Hollow** | **Noncritical functions** only | Outsourcing web and mobile app building while retaining HR in-house |
| **Modular** | A **noncritical part of a product** | Dell computers using AMD processors, AU Optronics displays, IBM disk storage |
| **Starburst** | A function **spun off** into a separate firm | Netflix splitting off DVD.com; IAC planning to spin off Vimeo |

**Advantage:** flexibility letting people with an innovative idea and little money compete against larger firms, and large savings from eliminating permanent offices and hierarchical roles. **Drawback:** virtual organizations are in **perpetual flux and reorganization**, so roles, goals and responsibilities are unclear — setting the stage for **political behavior**. (JFK's more virtual structure limited its communication and agility during the 2018 snow emergency, while Logan's matrix handled it.)

### Team structure

Eliminates the chain of command and **replaces departments with empowered teams**, removing most vertical and horizontal boundaries plus external barriers to customers and suppliers. It goes by many names — skunk works, holacracies, amoeba organizations, ambidextrous organizations, boundaryless organizations, meta-teams, market-oriented ecosystems — sharing three properties:

1. A **platform of resources** (people, technology, money) is set aside for market opportunities
2. Leaders assign **independent teams** to each opportunity
3. Teams are **empowered to anticipate customer needs and respond with agility and speed**

Management flattens the hierarchy and minimizes status and rank — a review of nearly **14,000 teams** found hierarchy engenders conflict that limits team effectiveness. Supports: cross-hierarchical teams, participative decision making, 360-degree appraisals. At Oticon, **all traces of hierarchy have disappeared** — uniform mobile workstations, and project teams rather than departments coordinating work.

Amazon frames the choice culturally: a **"Day 1 culture"** obsesses over the customer, keeps a long-term focus and innovates boldly; **"Day 2 cultures"** are slow, bureaucratic and authoritative.

### Circular structure

Concentric rings: the **CEO at the centre**, then the top management team, then directors, then specialists and workers, grouped by function.

> [!note] The circular structure is a rebranding, not a redesign
> It is **virtually identical to the simple, vertical, hierarchical structure**. The difference is in **how information is conveyed** — instead of a top-down chain of command, it presents the organization as a team with top management at the heart and its **vision spreading outward**, with less sharply defined segments.
>
> Its appeal is to creative entrepreneurs and to executives focused on CSR who want to spread a vision. The cost: employees are apt to be **unclear about whom they report to and who is running the show**, without clear linkages between people in each band.

## Downsizing

**Downsizing** — a systematic effort to make an organization leaner by closing locations, reducing staff, or selling off business units that do not add value. It may include **de-layering** — narrowing the span of control by reducing hierarchy levels.

Motives vary: **outside forces** (natural disaster, pandemic, recession), **pressure to meet earnings estimates**, focusing on **core competencies** as a **lean management** technique — and sometimes nefarious ones. One study found CEOs paid less than their peers were **more likely to conduct layoffs** and, if the firm performed better afterward, **more likely to receive pay increases**.

> [!warning] The evidence is contested
> Most evidence suggests downsizing has a **negative impact on stock returns in the year it happens** — contingent on the organization's goals and context.
>
> The cautionary case: Bo Andersson turned a profit at Russia's GAZ after firing **50,000 workers**, then as CEO of AvtoVAZ cut tens of thousands more at Togliatti. A Putin ally warned he was **"playing with fire,"** and he was removed in 2016 — **what is standard practice in the West was frowned on in Russia**, where the auto industry is a matter of national pride and jobs are preserved rather than cut.
>
> **The attitude problem:** survivors worry about future layoffs, become less committed and experience more stress and strain — and downsizing produces **voluntary turnover**, so vital human capital is lost. The result is a company **"more anemic than lean."** Paradoxically, **the victims may fare better than the survivors**, experiencing higher perceived control and less stress.

**Four strategies to reduce the damage:**

- **Invest** — companies that downsize to focus on core competencies do better when they invest in **high-involvement work practices** afterward
- **Communicate** — discussing it early leaves employees less worried and feeling their perspective was considered
- **Participate** — voluntary early-retirement programmes or severance packages achieve leanness without layoffs
- **Assist** — severance, extended health benefits and job search assistance show the company honours employees' contributions

## Why structures differ

| | **Mechanistic model** | **Organic model** |
|---|---|---|
| | High specialization · rigid departmentalization · clear chain of command · **narrow spans** · centralization · high formalization | Cross-functional teams · cross-hierarchical teams · free flow of information · **wide spans** · decentralization · low formalization |

The model shapes which behaviours work: **political behaviors** are more effective in **mechanistic** organizations; **transformational leadership and empowerment** are more effective in **organic** ones.

### Strategy

| Strategy | Definition | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| **Innovation** | Achieving meaningful and unique innovations; competitive pay and benefits to attract top candidates and motivate risk-taking | **Organic** — loose, low specialization, low formalization, decentralized. Though **some mechanistic elements help**: well-developed communication channels, policies for long-term commitment and clear authority make rapid change smoother |
| **Cost minimization** | Tight cost control, avoiding unnecessary expense, cutting prices | **Mechanistic** — tight control, extensive specialization, high formalization, high centralization |
| **Imitation** | Minimizing risk and maximizing profit opportunity by entering new products or markets **only after innovators have proven viability** | **Both** — mechanistic control over current activities, **organic subunits** for new undertakings |

> [!note] Two cautionary tales
> **Failing to innovate:** Steve Sasson invented the first digital camera at Kodak in 1975. Management's response: *"That's cute — but don't tell anyone about it."* Leadership was too focused on film's success and too afraid of what digital would do to the film market.
> **Imitation's limits:** Instagram's copy of Snapchat's "Stories" added **250 million users**. But imitation can make firms copy **the effects of success rather than its causes**, ignores the **luck and serendipity** behind what is being imitated, and produces long-term decline where the imitator lacks the capacity to scale. It also carries an ethical cost — a reputation for partnering with firms, learning from them, and **"stabbing them in the back"** once you have your own version.

### Size, technology, environment, institutions

**Size.** Organizations with **2,000 or more** employees tend to have more specialization, departmentalization, vertical levels and rules. But size **matters less as the organization expands** — at 2,000 an organization is already mechanistic, so 500 more changes little; adding 500 to an organization of **300** shifts it significantly toward mechanistic.

**Technology** — the way an organization transfers inputs into outputs. Structure adapts to technology **and vice versa**; structure and culture become **inscribed in the data structures, software and hardware** an organization uses.

**Environment** — outside institutions or forces affecting structure: suppliers, customers, competitors, public pressure groups. Three dimensions:

| Dimension | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Capacity** | The degree to which the environment can **support growth**. Rich, growing environments generate excess resources that buffer scarcity |
| **Volatility** | The degree of **instability**. Dynamic environments with unpredictable change make accurate prediction hard — and IT's pace is making most environments volatile |
| **Complexity** | The degree of **heterogeneity and concentration** among elements. Complex environments have numerous, widely dispersed, dissimilar competitors |

> [!important] The environment rule
> **The more scarce, dynamic and complex the environment, the more organic the structure should be. The more abundant, stable and simple, the more mechanistic.**
>
> Illustration: airlines choosing **strategic flexibility** succeed more in unpredictable turbulent environments than those prioritizing mechanistic efficiency. And among Chinese top executives, belief in **"negotiable fate"** — that personal action shapes outcomes — predicted more entrepreneurial approaches to volatility, which in turn predicted innovation and financial performance.

**Institutions** — cultural factors acting as guidelines for appropriate behaviour, which **institutional theory** studies as forces producing similar structures for reasons that are **not necessarily adaptive**. Sources: **regulatory pressure** (government contractors needing clear reporting relationships and information controls) · **inertia** (structured that way because that is how it has always been done, or how others do it) · **cultural norms** (high-power-distance countries producing strict authority relationships that look legitimate there — with adaptability problems in Japanese organizations attributed to institutional pressure to maintain authority relationships) · and **fads**, where organizations copy successful companies **to look good to investors** rather than to perform better.

## Key takeaways

- **Seven elements**, each answering one design question: specialization, departmentalization, chain of command, span of control, centralization, formalization, boundary spanning.
- **Specialization has a curve** — economies give way to human diseconomies (boredom, stress, absenteeism, turnover) if pushed too far.
- **Span of control is where the money is**: a span of 8 rather than 4 removes two levels and ~800 managers in a 4,100-person organization.
- **Centralization avoids bad choices; decentralization avoids lost opportunities.**
- **Boundary spanning** prevents rigidity — and works best when external spanning is paired with internal spanning.
- The classics: **simple** (fast but fragile and dependent on one person), **bureaucracy** (efficient only for familiar problems), **matrix** (coordination at the cost of role conflict and ambiguity).
- The newer designs: **virtual** (flexible but in perpetual flux and politically charged), **team** (empowered, boundaryless), **circular** (a rebranding of hierarchy, good for vision, weak on reporting clarity).
- **Downsizing's damage is to survivors** — communicate, invite participation and assist, or you get an anemic rather than lean company.
- **Mechanistic vs organic is decided by strategy, size, technology, environment and institutions** — and the environment rule is: **scarce, dynamic, complex → organic**.
