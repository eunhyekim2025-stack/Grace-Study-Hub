# Chapter 12 — Leadership

> [[management-of-people-at-work/index|← Management of People at Work]] · Previous: [[ch11-communication|Ch 11]] · Next: [[ch13-power-and-politics|Ch 13 — Power and Politics]]

---

## Core summary

**Leadership** — the ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals. Organizations need **both** strong leadership and strong management: leaders to **challenge the status quo, create visions and inspire**; managers to **formulate detailed plans, build efficient structures and oversee operations**.

The chapter walks through five families of theory, each answering the previous one's limitation, then closes with **trust** and the **challenges** to the whole enterprise:

1. **Trait theories** — who emerges (better than who is effective)
2. **Behavioral theories** — initiating structure and consideration; implies leadership can be **trained**
3. **Contingency theories** — Fiedler, SLT, and the follower-centred set: leader–participation, **shared leadership**, **followership**
4. **Positive styles** — LMX, charismatic, and the **full range model** ending in transformational
5. **(Un)ethical leadership** — authentic, ethical, servant, and **abusive supervision**

Then: **trust** as the common substrate, and the challenges — **leadership as attribution** and **neutralizers and substitutes**.

## Trait theories

**Trait theories of leadership** consider personal qualities and characteristics differentiating leaders from nonleaders, predicting two distinct outcomes: **leadership emergence** and **leadership effectiveness**. Exhibiting the right traits and being *considered* a leader does not mean being **effective**.

| Trait | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Extroversion** | The **strongest predictor of motivation to lead and leader emergence**, and predicts transformational and consideration behaviours. But it is the **agentic, bold, assertive** facets that do the work, not the warm, sociable, affiliative ones — which explains part of women's **under-emergence**, since women are stereotypically associated with the affiliative facets. Being "too bold" *or* "too warm" both hurt emergence |
| **Conscientiousness** | Predicts **initiating structure and consideration** behaviours, moderately predicts effectiveness and follower satisfaction, and is the **strongest leader trait predictor of group performance** |
| **Agreeableness / emotional stability** | Important to some aspects — they predict **follower satisfaction with the leader** |
| **Openness** | **Less important** — a modest predictor of effectiveness and follower voice behaviour |
| **Proactive personality** | Matters at specific life-cycle points — decisive in **leadership transitions**, especially when the incoming leader is far more proactive than the predecessor. Proactive leaders also suit groups whose members have a **high need for relational approval** |
| **Dark Triad** | **Midrange scores were optimal** — too much *or* too little produces ineffective leadership. But when followers work **closely** with the leader they observe exploitative behaviour, which destroys trust. Trait activation: when these leaders feel unfairly treated or entitled, they act destructively |
| **Emotional intelligence** | **Empathy** is the core component — sensing needs, hearing what followers do and do not say. High-EI people are **more likely to emerge as leaders**, even controlling for cognitive ability and personality. But the EI–transformational link is moderate on self-report and **much weaker when followers rate the leader** |

> [!important] Two conclusions and a twist
> ① **Traits can predict leadership.** ② Traits predict **emergence better than they distinguish effective from ineffective** leaders.
> The twist: newer work **flips the arrow** — engaging in leadership behaviours and taking leadership roles can **change your personality**, with leaders becoming more conscientious over time.
>
> Mitigations for Dark Triad leaders: building **self-awareness and self-regulation** (hard, since narcissists self-enhance and filter feedback) and **consulting followers** — employees at a large Chinese IT company were less fazed by narcissistic leaders who **sought and considered their feedback**.

## Behavioral theories

Trait theories tell you whom to **select**; behavioral theories imply you can **train** people to lead. The Ohio State Studies narrowed a thousand-plus dimensions to two:

| Dimension | Definition | Effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating structure** | The extent to which a leader **defines and structures** their role and followers' roles to facilitate goal attainment — assigning tasks, setting performance standards, emphasizing deadlines | More strongly related to **group and organizational productivity** and to more positive performance evaluations |
| **Consideration** | The extent to which a leader has job relationships characterized by **mutual trust, respect for ideas and regard for feelings** — helping with personal problems, being friendly and approachable, treating everyone as equals, expressing appreciation | Followers are **more satisfied, more motivated, less stressed** and have more respect for the leader |

> [!note] What a considerate boss is worth
> Across millions of responses over several years, the National Bureau of Economic Research found working for a considerate boss **"equivalent to the increased satisfaction that comes from more than doubling your household income."**

Both behaviours also predict whether employees are **selected into leadership succession pipelines** — people who act like leaders are more likely to be considered for leadership.

**When each is used.** Proud and self-confident (but not hubristic) leaders do more of both. Under **widespread understaffing**, leaders lean on **initiating structure** to keep processes efficient; under **expertise understaffing** (limited talent in key roles where turnover would be devastating) they lean on **consideration**, showing appreciation for key talent. And across thousands of managers in dozens of countries, **culture matters much less than the organization or the manager** in determining which is practised.

## Contingency theories

### The Fiedler model

Group performance depends on the **match between a leader's style and how much control the situation gives them**. Style is **task-oriented or relationship-oriented** and assumed **stable**. Three situational dimensions:

1. **Leader–member relations** — confidence, trust and respect members have in the leader
2. **Task structure** — how regimented the job assignments are
3. **Position power** — influence over hiring, firing, discipline, promotion, salary

**Prediction:** in **extreme** situations — very favourable or very unfavourable — **task-oriented** leaders perform better; in **moderately favourable** situations, **relationship-oriented** leaders do.

Initially supportive studies, little recent research, and some predictions unsupported — **relationship-oriented leaders have been found to perform very well in favourable conditions** too.

### Situational leadership theory (SLT)

Hersey and Blanchard move the focus to **followers' readiness** — their commitment to and competence in a specific task. Four styles, combining directive and supportive behaviour:

| Follower state | Style | What the leader does |
|---|---|---|
| Not quite competent, **but committed** | **Telling / directing** | Clear specific direction on what to do and how |
| Some competence, **not committed** (discouraged at how far there is to go) | **Selling / coaching** | Keep directing **and** add support, socio-emotional feedback, encouragement, conviction that the task is worthwhile |
| Competent, **variable commitment** (worried about responsibility, insecure) | **Participating / supporting** | Step back on direction; **stay supportive and encouraging** |
| Competent **and** committed | **Delegating** | Hand over the task and leave them to it |

Intuitively appealing, but **research efforts to test it have generally been disappointing** — internal ambiguities and inconsistencies in the model plus methodological problems. Endorse cautiously.

### The follower-centred trio

> [!important] "Leaders do not exist in a vacuum"
> The theories above assume a leader uses a **homogeneous style** with everyone. These three do not.

**Leader–participation model** — leaders should determine how far a problem calls for **participation and shared responsibility with followers**, adjusting accordingly. It does not cover all leadership behaviours; it recommends **which decisions are best made with subordinate participation**, and lays the groundwork for shared leadership.

**Shared leadership theory** — leadership can become an **emergent state** in which leadership roles are **distributed across followers**, all capable of influencing one another. It shifts the assumption from leadership as something one person enacts to something a **collective** enacts.

- **Conditions:** a strongly held shared purpose, flexible roles, proactive and functionally diverse members, and an environment of social support, autonomy and **psychological safety**
- **Timing:** less likely right after a team forms (members still navigating structure) and near the end of tenure (processes wrapping up)
- **Effects:** improves team performance, attitudes and behaviours, especially with **transformational or charismatic practices** and **complex tasks** — by building trust, empathy and cohesion, and by raising creativity, since followers support each other's ideas
- **Limit:** it produces **task and relationship conflict** when the members sharing leadership operate from **different levels of power**

Emergency trauma teams are the vivid case — leadership responsibilities delegated almost improvisationally to whoever is best suited to the problem that just appeared.

**Followership theory** — the **capability of followers to put a leader's vision into practice**. It "flips the leadership script": a leader's success depends heavily on the quality and makeup of followers, and influence runs both ways — **engaged followers produce engaged leaders; exhausted followers produce exhausted leaders**.

| The ideal follower | The poor follower |
|---|---|
| Industrious, enthusiastic, a "good citizen" and "team player" — hardworking, productive, excited, outgoing, happy, loyal, reliable, and more likely to perform **OCBs** | Prone to **insubordination, incompetence and conformity**; easily influenced, follows trends, does not speak up when needed (producing stagnation); arrogant, rude or ill-tempered (producing relationship conflict); or simply **not equipped** with the knowledge, skills or experience to make the vision real |

**"Managing up"** applies the same influence skills **upward** — appreciating and adapting to leaders with different perspectives, navigating and influencing them for the team's good, and forging a productive working relationship.

### Leading in crisis

The proverb says *"Cometh the hour, cometh the man"* — but the reality is messier. Angela Merkel was praised for decisive, evidence-based leadership during COVID, and **Germany had one of the world's lowest fatality rates**. Yet during stability women **under-emerge** through the **glass cliff** and the **think crisis–think female** effect: women are not seen as leaders in status-quo periods because of stereotypes about agency, **despite little evidence they are more or less effective than men**.

> [!tip] What actually works in crisis
> Crisis leaders are imagined in heroic terms, but a review of historical leadership incidents found much of the successful episodes involved **transactional behaviours** — **strategizing** (analyzing the situation, delegating, planning) and **coordinating** (orchestrating action, managing information flow). Analyzing the situation matters most, and effective crisis leaders **move quickly to a shared mental model** of the crisis with followers.
>
> **Emotion matters:** a leader responsible for a costly product recall was rated **more favourably when accepting responsibility and expressing sadness** than when denying and expressing anger.
>
> **Charismatic leadership** in crisis comes in two forms — **visionary** (establish a vision, then plead for action) and **crisis-responsive** (plead for action, then justify it by communicating results). Both work; the crisis-responsive form fades once the crisis ends. Transformational leaders see **less unit turnover after the crisis**, with soldiers more embedded through the shared experience.

## Positive leadership styles

### Leader–member exchange (LMX)

Leaders and followers have **unique one-on-one relationships that vary in quality**, and leaders form a special relationship with a small **ingroup** — trusted, given disproportionate attention and special privileges — while others fall into the **outgroup**.

**Who ends up in the ingroup:** competent, conscientious, proactive and positive employees — **and those who ingratiate and self-promote**. Leaders induce high LMX through emotional labor, transformational behaviours, ingratiating followers, and **rewarding those they want closer while punishing those they do not**. High-quality relationships develop where the supervisor holds **high expectations**, both see themselves as **similar**, they **like** each other, and they **trust** each other. Same-gender pairs tend to have higher LMX.

**Outcomes for ingroup members:** higher performance ratings **and objectively better performance**, more OCB, fewer CWBs, more speaking up, greater satisfaction with the leader — operating through **trust (the largest effect)**, motivation, empowerment and satisfaction. LMX also predicts **psychological and physical well-being**, lower stress and burnout, less felt ostracism — and even **higher salaries, bonuses and promotions**.

> [!warning] But playing favourites damages the group
> LMX has only a weak effect on group performance, and where leaders **play favourites** it harms team attitudes, collective efficacy, justice climate and coordination, and breeds conflict — **unless the differentiation is based on actual performance**. What matters more for group outcomes is the **overall level** of LMX quality: if the leader treats everyone well to some degree, followers are happy.
>
> Two boundary conditions: LMX matters **more in individualistic than collectivistic cultures** (across ~70,000 employees in 23 countries); and **leader–follower agreement** on relationship quality is weak — when they disagree, engagement suffers **regardless of which side rates it higher**.

### Charismatic leadership

Weber's *charisma* (Greek for "gift"): a quality by which an individual is **set apart from ordinary people** and treated as endowed with exceptional powers.

**Charismatic leadership theory** — followers **attribute** heroic or extraordinary abilities when they observe certain values-driven, symbolic or emotion-laden behaviours, and then **give those leaders power**. Four key characteristics:

1. **Vision and articulation** — an idealized goal proposing a future better than the status quo, clarified in terms others understand
2. **Personal risk** — willing to take high personal risk, incur costs and self-sacrifice for the vision
3. **Sensitivity to follower needs** — perceptive of others' abilities, responsive to needs and feelings
4. **Unconventional behaviour** — acting in ways perceived as novel and counter to norms

**How they influence:** by articulating an appealing **vision** — a long-term strategy for attaining a goal, linking the present to a better future — expressed in a **vision statement**. Desirable visions fit the times and reflect the organization's uniqueness. They set a tone of cooperation, build **self-esteem and confidence** through high expectations plus belief that followers can meet them, convey new values by example, and use emotion-inducing unconventional behaviour to demonstrate conviction. Followers **"catch" the emotions** the leader conveys.

**Situational modifiers:** **stress and crisis** heighten receptivity — people psychologically aroused respond more to charismatic leaders, especially those using **constructive, promotion-oriented** language rather than negative language without solutions. (Stress cuts both ways: **leaders under stress may lack the resources** to be charismatic.) **Ambiguity** also amplifies it — where we cannot observe how someone is doing, we rely on charismatic cues, and charisma **predicts selection and appointment** especially in ambiguous situations.

> [!warning] The dark side
> Larger-than-life charismatic leaders **do not necessarily act in their organizations' best interests**. Narcissists score higher on some charismatic behaviours. Charisma **distorts follower fairness perceptions** — seeing the leader positively, followers become more willing to perceive them as fair. Theranos and Nikola lied about their technology's capabilities while convincing investors of its ingenuity. Charisma is a positive tool that **can be used for nefarious ends**; success depends on the situation, the vision, and the **checks and balances** monitoring outcomes.

### The full range leadership model

Styles on a continuum from **passive and ineffective** to **active and effective**:

| Family | Style | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Laissez-faire** | Laissez-faire | Abdicates responsibilities, avoids making decisions |
| **Transactional** | Management by exception (**passive**) | Intervenes only if standards are not met |
| | Management by exception (**active**) | Watches and searches for deviations from rules and standards, takes corrective action |
| | **Contingent reward** | Contracts an exchange of rewards for effort, promises rewards for good performance, recognizes accomplishments |
| **Transformational** | **Individualized consideration** | Gives personal attention, treats each employee individually, coaches, advises |
| | **Intellectual stimulation** | Promotes intelligence, rationality and careful problem solving |
| | **Inspirational motivation** | Communicates high expectations, uses symbols to focus effort, expresses important purposes simply |
| | **Idealized influence** | Provides vision and a sense of mission, instils pride, gains respect and trust |

> [!warning] Laissez-faire is passive but its effects are not
> Only **17%** of employees report supervisors actively engaged in developing them; **68%** report no such engagement at all. Contrary to intuition, teams under laissez-faire leaders **do not step up** to fill the void — in line with social learning theory, members **mirror the passive leader** and neglect leadership behaviours themselves, harming performance. It also does emotional harm: followers who define themselves through their supervisor relationship read it as **low-quality LMX** and lose organizational commitment. And for people being bullied or mistreated, a supervisor who **"does nothing"** encourages role conflict and ambiguity, relational conflict, mistreatment and a stressful environment.

The **four I's** of transformational leadership produce extra effort, higher productivity, creativity, well-being, morale and satisfaction, organizational effectiveness, **psychological safety** and follower freedom to speak up, better psychological and physical health, lower turnover and absenteeism, and greater adaptability. Transformational leadership is used more in health and security industries and in cultures valuing **assertiveness and egalitarianism** and **lower in power distance**.

### Comparing them

| Comparison | The distinction |
|---|---|
| **Transformational vs charismatic** | Charismatic emphasizes **how** leaders communicate (passionate? dynamic?); transformational emphasizes **what** they communicate (individualized? intellectually stimulating? inspirational? influential?). Both centre on inspiring followers |
| **Transformational vs transactional** | **Complementary, not opposing.** Transformational **builds on** transactional, producing effort and performance beyond what transactional alone achieves. Transformational matters more for **group performance, OCB and satisfaction with the leader**; transactional (mainly **contingent reward**) matters more for **leader effectiveness, follower performance and follower job satisfaction** |

> [!note] Management by exception is a double-edged sword
> Contingent reward is nearly as effective as many transformational approaches; management by exception lags. But newer research finds its pros and cons **offset each other**: monitoring and stepping in only when needed makes followers feel **empowered and not micromanaged** (raising task performance and OCB), while simultaneously signalling **low-quality LMX** — a leader who only appears when there is a problem is not building the relationship.

**Why transformational leadership works — five mechanisms:** ① **affective/attitudinal** (positive moods, satisfaction, commitment, well-being) ② **motivational** (more confident, engaged, willing) ③ **identification** (with the leader's values and identity, and with the team) ④ **social exchange** (better LMX and perceived support) ⑤ **justice enhancement** (better fairness perceptions, more contribution and trust).

Research suggests the **social exchange mechanism is primary**: transformational behaviours improve the leader–follower relationship, and **that improved relationship** then produces the affective, attitudinal, motivational, identification and justice changes.

> [!warning] Downsides and criticisms
> Critics call transformational behaviours potentially **manipulative**, unduly empowering inspirational people. It is **costly to the leader** — emotionally exhausting, sometimes to the point of leaving the role, especially when followers are **low in conscientiousness and competence**. And there is a construct problem: **are the four I's behaviours, or desirable follower outcomes?** Do leaders intellectually stimulate, or do followers *perceive* they are stimulated?

## The (un)ethical aspects of leadership

| Style | Definition | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Authentic leadership** | Leaders who are **self-aware**, anchored by mission and principles, consider others' opinions and all relevant information before acting, and **display their true selves** | Strongly predicts group performance, OCB, LMX, satisfaction and trust in the leader, rated effectiveness, follower attitudes and empowerment; less strongly creativity, engagement, deviance, turnover intentions and burnout. Leaders who "practise what they preach" build trust and commitment — and **inspire followers to be authentic themselves** |
| **(Un)ethical leadership** | Leaders serving as **ethical role models**, demonstrating normatively appropriate behaviour by using power ethically and treating others fairly | Shapes how employees think about moral issues and builds an organizational culture with clear values; sets the example for how employees treat each other (reducing CWB, intimidation, excuses); **empowers people to speak out against injustice**; improves job attitudes through **trust**; and improves task performance and OCB through LMX, ethical culture and organizational identification. One study of 111 US retail stores found ethical leadership **reduced "retail shrink"** by reducing incivility |
| **Servant leadership** | Going beyond self-interest to focus on opportunities that **help followers grow and develop** — listening, empathizing, persuading, acceptance, stewardship, actively developing potential | Strongly related to follower job attitudes, trust and LMX; predicts performance and OCB somewhat less strongly, apparently through follower **gratitude** and a need to reciprocate. It **trickles down** — high-level servant leaders produce low-level servant leaders. It aids **onboarding**, compensating for low new-employee proactivity. In a Chinese IT firm during COVID it **converted anxiety and fear into engagement and socially responsible behaviour**. Of the ethical styles, it holds the most promise of **standing on its own** as distinct |

> [!warning] The authenticity double standard
> Women are generally perceived as **more authentic**, especially in traditionally "feminine" hobbies and "warm" leadership styles. But when they use **assertive, transactional approaches** — initiating structure, directive behaviour — it violates the stereotype and gets them labelled **inauthentic**. Hillary Clinton was mocked as "Saint Hillary" for her "politics of meaning" in the 1990s and branded **inauthentic** when she adopted more traditionally masculine approaches decades later.
>
> Ironically, **more women in corporate leadership predicts better financial performance** on both accounting and market measures — and women governors' leadership was associated with **fewer COVID-19 deaths** in a US analysis.

**Two shared limitations.** Authentic and ethical leadership are both hard to disentangle from **transformational leadership** in practice. And **consistency is hard**: leaders ethical on one day were **more likely to be unethical the next**, from stress, mental depletion, and a sense of having built up "credits." Ethical leadership is less effective when there are **too many followers**, when followers **do not share the leader's moral perspective**, and when followers are not in a state of **mindfulness**.

### Abusive supervision

The perception that a supervisor is **hostile in verbal and nonverbal behaviour** — uncontrolled outbursts, public ridicule, inappropriate blaming. In a 2018 Monster poll, **76%** of US job seekers reported currently having a toxic or abusive supervisor.

**Antecedents:** nearly all forms of **justice are negatively related** to it — a sense of injustice is at its core, especially **interpersonal** justice. Agreeableness and conscientiousness are weakly negative; **negative affect, narcissism and poor self-control** are strongly linked. Supervisors' own overload, exhaustion, stress and frustration feed it — and disturbingly, **abusing actually helps them cope** with those emotions and become more engaged. Follower side: people with **negative worldviews** report more abuse, and **both poor and high performers** become victims — the former berated for incompetence, the latter out of **jealousy or threat to the hierarchy**.

**Consequences:** increased depression, emotional exhaustion, lower self-esteem, dehumanization, ostracism and job tension · decreased commitment, satisfaction and perceived support, plus more work–family conflict · more CWB and deviance (**especially retaliation aimed at the supervisor**) and less OCB. Observing coworkers fear it will happen to them and **work unduly harder** — though in ethical, collaborative climates they instead **help the victim and call out the abuser**.

> [!warning] It runs in cycles, and it spreads
> Abused employees lash out at both supervisor and organization — because they **blame the organization**, seeing the supervisor as its representative — and the supervisor retaliates with more abuse. Abuse can also **activate followers' dark traits** (Machiavellian employees turn to unethical behaviour).
>
> It also **propagates between leaders**: new leaders who see abusive supervisors as competent and their behaviour as "what it takes" **adopt it**, and merely *talking about* abusive supervision normalizes it. New leaders with a **strong moral identity who distance themselves** from such supervisors avoid it and lead more ethically.
>
> Conscientious, resilient, self-regulating employees cope better, and followers can manage the power balance by demonstrating they are instrumental to the leader's goals — **but the primary responsibility lies with the leader**. Leaders **can be trained** not to abuse, and abusive leaders often show guilt and shame that produce constructive behaviour afterward — which helps followers **only if it is authentic and not "two-faced."**

## Leadership and trust

**Trust** — a psychological state of **mutual positive expectations** between people who depend on each other and are genuinely concerned for each other's welfare. It splits into **competence-based** (faith in technical skill and experience) and **integrity-based** (faith in motives, honesty and character) — and integrity-based trust is much more effective at reducing the costs of building new relationships.

**Four outcomes of trust:**

- **Encourages risk taking** — deviating from the usual way, or taking a supervisor's word on a new direction, is a risk that trust makes possible
- **Facilitates information sharing** — people withhold concerns when they do not feel **psychologically safe**; managers who give ideas a fair hearing and act on them get people speaking out
- **Makes groups more effective** — trusting groups help each other and exert extra effort; mistrusting groups guard against exploitation and restrict communication, eventually destroying themselves
- **Enhances productivity** — employees who trust supervisors receive higher performance ratings; people respond to mistrust by **concealing information and pursuing their own interests**

**Three bases of trustworthiness:**

| Base | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Integrity** | Honesty and truthfulness, plus **consistency between what you do and say**. Of 28 leadership attributes rated by 570 white-collar employees, honesty was **the most important by far** |
| **Benevolence** | The trusted person has **your** interests at heart, even where they diverge from theirs |
| **Ability** | Technical and interpersonal knowledge and skill — you will not depend on someone whose ability you doubt, however principled |

**Trust propensity** — how likely someone is to trust a leader — is closely linked to **agreeableness**, and people with lower self-esteem trust less. Teams whose members have differing propensities **spiral downward** when conflict arises.

**Culture:** Taiwanese employees responded to **benevolent and ethical paternalistic leadership** with increased trust — plausibly a collectivistic pattern grounded in Confucian values of hierarchy and relationship. In individualistic societies paternalism may rankle, and trust is built instead along **support and consistency**.

> [!important] Repairing trust
> Trust is built by **observing behaviour over time** — leaders must show integrity, benevolence and ability precisely **where they could behave opportunistically**. Maintain it by **owning up to and apologizing for legitimate mistakes rather than denying them**. Build it by shifting from **top-down commands to ongoing organizational dialogue**, and by regular conversations that are **intimate, interactive and inclusive**.
>
> Repairing it depends on the violation. **Ability failure** — apologize and acknowledge you should have done better. **Integrity failure** — apologies do **not** do much good. **Saying nothing, or refusing to confirm or deny guilt, is never effective.** Apologize **quickly**; late apologies work worse. And after **deception**, trust **never fully returns** — not after apologies, promises, or a consistent pattern of trustworthy action.

## Challenges and opportunities

### Leadership as an attribution

The **attribution theory of leadership** holds that leadership is **merely an attribution people make about other individuals** — we attribute intelligence, outgoing personality, verbal skill, aggressiveness, understanding and industriousness to leaders, and hold them responsible for both extreme success and extreme failure.

Consequences: employees' **perceptions** of leader behaviour predict whether they blame leaders for failure regardless of leaders' self-assessment; across 3,000+ employees worldwide, people who **"romanticize" leadership** were more likely to believe their own leaders were transformational **whether or not they were**.

> [!warning] Attributions carry demographic bias
> US respondents assumed a leader described with **no racial information was White** at a rate beyond the company's base rate — regardless of the respondent's own race. When identical leadership situations were described with race manipulated, leaders believed to be **White were rated more effective**. And many people hold stereotypes of **men as having more leader characteristics than women** (a tendency that has decreased over time). These attribution differences are one mechanism behind **leadership under-emergence** for women and people from ethnically diverse backgrounds.
>
> The uncomfortable implication: attribution theory suggests what matters is **projecting the appearance of being a leader** rather than actual accomplishment.

### Neutralizers and substitutes

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Neutralizers** | Attributes that make it **impossible** for leader behaviour to make any difference to follower outcomes — e.g. **indifference to rewards** |
| **Substitutes** | Attributes that **replace** the need for a leader's support or structure — e.g. **experience and training**, professionalism |

Sources: **individual** (experience/training, professionalism, indifference to rewards) · **job** (highly structured task, task that provides its own feedback, intrinsically satisfying work) · **organization** (explicit formalized goals, rigid rules and procedures, cohesive work groups). Valve, W. L. Gore and GitHub have experimented with eliminating leaders, governing through **accountability to coworkers** who determine team composition and sometimes pay.

Two caveats: the substitute/neutralizer line is **fuzzy** (does an intrinsically enjoyable task neutralize leadership, substitute for it, or both?), and the fact that substitutes matter to performance **does not make leadership inconsequential** — leadership is simply one component of the wider OB model.

### Identifying, selecting and developing leaders

**Selection.** JCPenney is the cautionary case: a CEO with no department-store experience changed the whole strategy and the stock fell **69%** in about a year. Start from the **knowledge, skills and abilities** the job needs — and note the competencies differ by level:

- **Executive** — conceptual, problem-solving, social and strategic skills
- **Middle manager** — interpersonal, social judgment and people skills
- **Supervisory** — the same, plus technical skills and knowledge

Personality tests can identify **extroversion, conscientiousness and openness**. **Broad experience is a poor predictor** of effectiveness (though boards over-rely on track record); **situation-specific experience** is relevant.

**Training.** US companies spent **\$82.5 billion** on workplace training in 2020, with the majority of leadership budgets going to development. Despite the folk claim that "managers are created, leaders are born," **the research is optimistic**: people learn from leadership training, apply it at work and perform better as a result — but **not all programmes are equal**.

The sequence: **needs analysis** ("what are our staffing needs? what is our leadership talent pool like?") → explicitly identify **what to develop** → design and deliver → check **transfer** (did the leader actually use it? did they have the opportunity and motivation?) → feed results into promotion, retention or reassignment decisions. **Coaching** is the alternative: 25–40% of Fortune 500 companies hire coaches for top executives, reporting an average return of about **\$100,000 per investment**.

> [!note] The "superhero syndrome"
> During COVID many leaders put on a brave face, projecting strength and expertise, then met changes too fast to handle and compensated by working **unreasonably long hours**. Leaders who avoided it had the confidence to **admit that conditions were changing rapidly and they did not have all the answers**.

## Key takeaways

- **Leadership = influencing a group toward a vision or goals.** Organizations need leaders *and* managers; **nonsanctioned leadership** can outweigh formal authority.
- **Traits predict emergence better than effectiveness.** Extroversion drives emergence through its **agentic** facets; **conscientiousness** best predicts group performance; **midrange Dark Triad** is optimal.
- **Initiating structure** raises productivity; **consideration** raises satisfaction — worth more than doubling household income.
- **Contingency** moved from Fiedler and SLT (both weakly supported) to **shared leadership** and **followership**, which put followers at the centre.
- **LMX** differentiates followers into in- and outgroups with real consequences — but **overt favouritism damages the team**, while a high average level helps it.
- **Charisma** works and is dangerous; the **full range model** runs laissez-faire (never) → transactional (necessary) → transformational (best), and transformational works mainly through **improved leader–follower exchange**.
- **Authentic, ethical and servant** leadership are the ethical family; **abusive supervision** is the negative case, running in cycles and propagating between leaders.
- **Trust rests on integrity, benevolence and ability.** Apologize fast for ability failures; apologies do little for integrity failures; **deception is unrecoverable**.
- **Leadership is partly attribution** — with demographic bias baked in — and **neutralizers and substitutes** limit how much any leader can matter.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Leadership** | The ability to influence a group toward the achievement of a vision or set of goals |
| **Trait theories of leadership** | Theories considering the personal qualities differentiating leaders from nonleaders |
| **Behavioral theories of leadership** | Theories proposing that specific behaviours differentiate leaders from nonleaders |
| **Initiating structure / consideration** | Defining and structuring roles for goal attainment / relationships marked by mutual trust and regard for feelings |
| **Fiedler contingency model** | Effective groups depend on matching leader style to the control the situation gives |
| **Leader–member relations / task structure / position power** | Fiedler's three situational dimensions |
| **Situational leadership theory (SLT)** | The appropriate style depends on followers' readiness — telling, selling, participating, delegating |
| **Leader–participation model** | Leaders should determine how far a problem calls for participation and shared responsibility |
| **Shared leadership theory** | Leadership as an emergent state distributed across followers |
| **Followership** | The capability of followers to put a leader's vision into practice |
| **Leader–member exchange (LMX) theory** | Leaders form unique relationships of varying quality, producing ingroups and outgroups |
| **Charismatic leadership theory** | Followers attribute heroic abilities on observing values-driven, symbolic or emotional behaviours |
| **Vision / vision statement** | A long-term strategy for attaining a goal / its formal articulation |
| **Full range leadership model** | Styles on a continuum from passive and ineffective to active and effective |
| **Laissez-faire leadership** | Passive abdication and avoidance of leadership responsibilities |
| **Transactional / transformational leaders** | Guide toward established goals via role clarification, rewards and intervention / inspire followers to transcend self-interest |
| **Authentic leadership** | Self-aware, mission-anchored leaders who consider all relevant information and show their true selves |
| **(Un)ethical leadership** | Leaders as ethical role models using power ethically and treating others fairly |
| **Servant leadership** | Going beyond self-interest to help followers grow and develop |
| **Abusive supervision** | Supervision that is hostile both verbally and nonverbally |
| **Trust / trust propensity** | Mutual positive expectations between mutually dependent people / how likely someone is to trust a leader |
| **Attribution theory of leadership** | Leadership as merely an attribution people make about other individuals |
| **Neutralizers / substitutes** | Attributes making leader behaviour irrelevant / attributes replacing the need for it |
