> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- AI projects fail at >70 % because human psychology is ignored.  
- Core barriers: algorithm aversion, fairness worries, black‑box opacity, and pro‑human bias.  
- “Anti‑AI bias” penalises AI users in pay, leadership perception, and job security.  
- Human‑centered, iterative design is essential to overcome these biases.  
- Prepare by strengthening soft skills, ethical awareness, and foresight on AI‑driven second‑order effects.

## 1. AI adoption reality
- Billions are spent, yet failure rates exceed those of traditional digital transformation (>70 %).  
- Organizations often view AI as a pure engineering problem, ignoring the behavioral dimension.  
- AI research and deployment are “moving goalposts”; continuous iteration and oversight are required.  

## 2. Core psychological barriers
> [!info] **Algorithm aversion** – Users lose trust after a few errors, even if the algorithm outperforms humans overall.  

> [!info] **Fairness concerns (uniqueness neglect)** – AI treats all users uniformly, sparking resistance in high‑stakes domains (hiring, medicine).  

> [!info] **Black‑box problem** – Few truly understand model internals; opacity lowers confidence despite human cognition also being opaque.  

> [!info] **Pro‑human bias** – Default preference for human decisions masks the fact that humans are equally (or more) biased via training data.  

> [!info] **Processing fluency** – Concrete human credentials (years of experience) are trusted more than abstract algorithmic attributes (data source).  

> [!info] **Dual perception** – AI is cast simultaneously as a magical savior and a dangerous monster, echoing historic tech cycles (e.g., electricity).  

> [!warning] **Over‑reliance & awe** – “Magical” framing can cause users to over‑trust AI, assuming supernatural capabilities.

## 3. Anti‑AI bias in the workplace
- Experiments show AI‑assisted workers are valued less (average “win‑win” score = 46 %).  
- Participants pay more for a purely human partner than for an AI‑augmented one.  
- AI users are judged **lazier, more replaceable, less competent, less diligent, and less warm**.  
- Leadership potential ratings drop for AI‑assisted individuals (implicit leadership theory: leaders must work hard).  
- Freelancers/gig workers face the steepest penalties; formal contracts mitigate the bias.  
- Companies are more likely to cut headcount when AI is involved in fixed‑pay scenarios.  
- Many hide AI use (“secret cyborg”) to preserve a competent image, stifling knowledge sharing.  

| Bias effect | Typical outcome |
|------------|-----------------|
| Pay & compensation | Lower wage offers for AI‑assisted workers |
| Leadership perception | Reduced rating of leadership potential |
| Job security | Higher likelihood of headcount cuts |
| Reputation | Perceived as lazy or replaceable |
| Transparency | Concealment of AI use (“secret cyborg”) |

> [!example] **Pay experiment** – Participants valued “Steve + AI” less than “Steve alone,” yielding a 46 % win‑win score, illustrating anti‑AI bias in compensation decisions.

## 4. Human‑centered AI design
- **Behavioral human‑centered AI**: design with creator and user psychology in mind.  
- Address **inventor’s bias** – creators over‑estimate benefits and under‑estimate risks of their systems.  
- Treat AI adoption as an ongoing socio‑technical program: continuous monitoring, iterative tweaks, and alignment with evolving organizational contexts.  

> [!warning] Ignoring behavioral factors leads to project failure despite technically superior models.

## 5. Preparing for an AI‑augmented future
1. **Foresight exercise** – In groups, pick a profession and predict a plausible AI‑driven transformation by 2035 (first‑principles reasoning).  
2. **Second‑order consequences** – Identify resources that become abundant/scarce and the resulting value shifts.  
3. **Reverse‑engineer preparation** – Decide today which skills will make you an early adopter of the predicted shift.  

*Sample student prediction*: AI‑augmented design tools enable rapid, innovative creation.  
*Second‑order consequence*: Face‑to‑face collaboration declines → communication & empathy become premium skills.  

**Recommended skill focus**
- Strengthen soft skills: communication, empathy, relationship building.  
- Stay current on emerging tech (autonomous/flying vehicles, generative AI).  
- Study AI security, ethics, and auditability.  

> [!info] **“Stay where the puck is going”** – Prioritize future‑oriented skill development over defending current roles.

## 6. Broader societal trends
- The middle class is projected to capture the largest share of AI‑driven benefits (finance, law, etc.).  
- Rapid AI rollout can cause “languishing” – a mild, persistent demotivation distinct from clinical depression.  
- Demand for meaningful human experiences (bespoke travel, curated events) will rise as automation handles routine tasks.  
- Transparency & auditability become critical: open‑weight, value‑aligned models allow stakeholders to contest decisions; closed‑source frontier models lack explainability.  

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Algorithm aversion | Loss of trust in algorithms after few errors, despite overall superiority |
| Fairness concerns (uniqueness neglect) | Tendency of AI to treat everyone the same, prompting resistance in high‑stakes contexts |
| Black‑box problem | Opacity of model internals that reduces user confidence |
| Pro‑human bias | Preference for human decisions that masks human bias |
| Anti‑AI bias | Systematic penalisation of AI users in pay, leadership perception, and job security |
| Human‑centered AI | Design approach that incorporates creator/user psychology and mitigates inventor’s bias |
| Secret cyborg | Employee who uses AI covertly to avoid stigma |
| Second‑order consequence | Indirect effect of an AI‑driven change on the value of other resources or skills |
