> [!summary] Key takeaways
> - Inventory ordering uses empirical demand tables; choose the smallest demand whose cumulative probability meets the target service level.  
> - The optimal quantity balances underage (stock‑out) and overage (excess) costs via the critical‑ratio formula.  
> - Revenue management treats fixed capacity like a newsvendor: reserve premium slots using the same critical‑ratio rule.  
> - Overbooking is an application of the newsvendor model—sell extra tickets until the cost of bumping equals the revenue of an extra seat.  
> - Both domains aim to **minimise expected cost** or **maximise expected profit**, not to eliminate cost entirely.

## Inventory ordering (discrete‑parameter model)

- **Four-step procedure**  
  1. Gather input data (historical demand).  
  2. Estimate demand distribution directly from data (no normal assumption).  
  3. Compute expected cost for each candidate order quantity.  
  4. Pick the quantity that minimises total expected cost.  

> [!info] **Frequency (latency) table** – list each observed demand value, its count, and probability = count / N (N = number of periods).  

> [!info] **Cumulative (in‑step) probability** – running sum of probabilities up to each demand level.

| Demand (units) | Frequency | Probability | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | 6 | 0.06 | 0.06 |
| … | … | … | … |
| 96 | 8 | 0.08 | 0.55 |
| … | … | … | … |

- **Choosing Q\***: for a target service level (e.g., 0.545), locate the first cumulative probability ≥ service level; the associated demand value is the optimal order (here ≈ 96 units).  

> [!warning] The optimal order rarely drives total cost to zero because actual demand will differ from the order quantity.

- **Cost insight** – the average‑demand order is optimal only when underage cost (C_U) equals overage cost (C_O). If C_U > C_O, order less than the mean; if C_U < C_O, order more.

## Revenue management fundamentals

### Core characteristics
1. **Fixed capacity** – seats, rooms, or cars cannot be increased.  
2. **Finite selling horizon** – each service date must be sold before the departure/checkout date.  
3. **Irrevocable product** – unsold inventory after the deadline cannot be shifted.

### Two strategic levers
- **Price discrimination** – charge different rates based on customer segment (e.g., contract length, volume, funding source).  
- **Capacity allocation** – decide how much inventory to reserve for high‑yield (premium) vs. low‑yield (discount) customers.

### Trade‑off of reservation decisions
- Too few premium slots → lost high‑margin sales.  
- Too many premium slots → empty seats if premium demand falls short, incurring opportunity cost.

> [!info] **Unit‑cost perspective** – the “cost” of a reserved premium unit equals the revenue you would have earned selling it at the discount price. Profit margin = premium price – discount price.

### Newsvendor equivalence

> [!info] **Critical‑ratio rule**  
> \[
> P(D \le Q^*) = \frac{C_U}{C_U + C_O}
> \]  
> where  
> • \(C_U\) = underage cost (lost profit per unsold premium seat)  
> • \(C_O\) = overage cost (compensation/penalty per bumped passenger)

- The formula is identical to the inventory‑ordering critical ratio; it determines the optimal number of premium slots (or overbooked tickets).

### Overbooking example (NY‑LA flight)

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Aircraft seats | 100 |
| Historical demand mean | 25 |
| Demand SD | 15 |
| Premium ticket price | \$125 |
| Bump‑cost (compensation) | \$230 |

1. **Compute costs**  
   - \(C_U =\) lost profit if a seat stays empty = premium price = \$125.  
   - \(C_O =\) bump‑cost = \$230.  

2. **Critical ratio**  
   \[
   \frac{C_U}{C_U + C_O} = \frac{125}{125 + 230} \approx 0.352
   \]

3. **Select overbooking quantity** – find the smallest ticket count Q such that \(P(D \le Q) \ge 0.352\) using the demand distribution (normal approx or empirical).  

> [!example] If the normal approximation gives \(P(D \le 115) = 0.36\), then sell 115 tickets (15 over the 100 seats). Expected bump‑cost balances extra revenue from the 15 extra tickets.

### Bottom line
- Revenue management **maximises expected profit** by allocating limited capacity between price classes and by calibrating overbooking levels using the same critical‑ratio logic as the newsvendor problem.

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Critical ratio | \(C_U/(C_U+C_O)\); probability threshold for optimal quantity |
| Underage cost (C_U) | Cost of ordering too little (lost profit or empty seat) |
| Overage cost (C_O) | Cost of ordering too much (excess inventory or bump‑cost) |
| Discrete‑parameter model | Uses empirical demand frequencies instead of a parametric distribution |
| Price discrimination | Charging different prices to distinct customer segments |
| Capacity allocation | Reserving inventory for high‑yield vs. low‑yield customers |
| Overbooking | Selling more tickets than seats, balancing bump‑cost against extra revenue |
