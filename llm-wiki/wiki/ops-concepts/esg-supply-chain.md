# ESG Issues in Supply Chains

> [[operations-management|← Operations Management]] · The **Environmental, Social, and Governance** dimension of operations — how a manufacturer governs conduct (here, mainly **child labour**) across suppliers it does not directly control. Connects to the supplier-relationship side of [[ops-concepts/mrp-erp|ERP/SRM]].

> [!note] Source classification
> Built from the Session 13 deck (marked *SMU Classification: Restricted*). This note summarises the **general ESG/supply-chain concepts and frameworks** only.

---

## The problem — unobservable conduct deep in the chain

**Child labour** (ILO): work that is "physically, mentally, socially or morally dangerous and harmful to underage children, and interferes with their schooling." It is widespread — the deck cites 200M+ children (~10%) engaged worldwide.

The economic reason it persists in supply chains is **moral hazard** — a form of **post-contractual opportunism**: the supplier takes **actions with efficiency consequences that are not freely observable**, and may pursue private interests at the buyer's (and workers') expense. (Everyday analogues: an employee studying when work waits; brokers encouraging over-trading.) The management question is how to **prevent moral hazard** when you cannot directly watch the supplier.

---

## Lever 1 — Supplier audits

Auditing is the foundation of ESG control: **know who you buy from**, evaluate and select suppliers on their ability to meet requirements, and keep records of acceptable suppliers.

- **Internal audit** — the manufacturer audits its own supplier (cheaper, but lacks independence/accountability).
- **External audit** — an NGO, the media, or a third party audits (more credible, higher accountability).

The right mix depends on factors like the **cost of goodwill damage** and inspection cost.

---

## Lever 2 — Information disclosure

Firms are encouraged (and increasingly required) to **publish** their anti-child-labour policies and efforts — Apple, Sony and Nike issue annual social-responsibility reports.

- **California Transparency in Supply Chains Act** (effective 2012): large firms (>$100M revenue) must publicly disclose efforts to eliminate child labour, slavery and human trafficking.
- Platforms such as **KnowTheChain** benchmark disclosure.

> Disclosure does **not always** help — its effect depends on whether it raises the firm's **goodwill cost** of being caught (see the comparison below).

---

## Lever 3 — Penalty schemes

| Scheme | What happens when child labour is found |
|---|---|
| **Zero tolerance** | Supplier **loses the contract** |
| **Corrective actions** | Supplier is **required to fix** the problem |
| **Hybrid** | Lose the contract if found by **external** audit; carry out corrective actions if found **internally** |

The schemes interact with the other levers differently:

| Lever | Zero tolerance | Corrective actions | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Combat strategy | price premium + inspection | inspection | price premium + inspection |
| Improving disclosure | **effective** | no impact | ineffective when goodwill costs are **low** |
| Raising goodwill cost | **effective** | **effective** | **effective** |
| Reducing inspection cost | ineffective when goodwill costs are **high** | **effective** | ineffective when goodwill costs are **high** |

---

## Lever 4 — Cooperation in auditing

When several manufacturers share a **common supplier**, they can cooperate:

| | **Joint auditing** (one shared audit) | **Audit sharing** (each audits, shares results) |
|---|---|---|
| Example | Accord & Alliance on building/worker safety in Bangladesh | International Pharmaceutical Supply Chain Consortium |
| **Pros** | Lower cost · no free-riding | Independent audits from several firms (more reliable) · clear ownership of results |
| **Cons** | Lacks independence · accountability unclear | Costly · free-riding risk |

The trade-off is the classic one between **cost/coordination** (joint) and **independence/accountability** (shared).
