> [!summary] Key takeaways
- CV (coefficient of variation) beats SD for cross‑process variability comparison.  
- Queue waiting time is driven by arrival rate (A), service time (B), server count (M), utilization, and availability.  
- Reduce wait by: add servers, cut utilization (arrival rate), or speed service (lower P).  
- Women’s restroom queues are longer because of longer P, higher utilization, and fewer stalls.  
- Match staffing to demand‑time slots; iterate N until target wait and profit are met.

## 1. Statistical foundations
- **Standard deviation** measures spread but can mislead for process variability.  
> [!info] **Coefficient of Variation (CV)** = σ / μ; dimension‑less, ideal for comparing variability across different scales (e.g., inventory turnover vs days of supply).  

## 2. Real‑time queueing model
- **Arrival process**  
  - Inter‑arrival time = **A** (random).  
  - Arrival rate = **1 / A**.  
- **Service process**  
  - Service time = **B**.  
  - Service rate = **1 / B**.  
> [!warning] Do **not** confuse A with its reciprocal; they are distinct variables.  

- **Key factors linking Q, M, P**  
  1. **Effective processing time (capacity factor)** – average service time per customer (P).  
  2. **Utilization factor (U)** – proportion of time servers are busy (λ · P / M).  
  3. **Availability factor** – proportion of time servers are operational.  

## 3. Managing queue length
- **Primary levers**  

| Lever | Action | Effect on wait |
|------|--------|----------------|
| Add servers (**M**) | Increase capacity | Lowers utilization → shorter queues |
| Reduce utilization | Lower arrival rate (traffic redirection, appointments) | Directly cuts queue length |
| Speed service (**P**) | Faster equipment, process redesign | Decreases processing time per customer |

- **Kimmler’s formula** (queueing‑theory expression) – ensure the exponent covers the whole term and the “‑1” sits inside the square‑root.  
> [!warning] Common algebraic slip: mis‑placing the exponent or “‑1” yields erroneous staffing numbers.  

- **Physical vs Psychological queue**  
  - Physical: actual line length.  
  - Psychological: perceived wait (e.g., ticket‑number systems). Improves satisfaction without changing real wait.  

- **Cooling / load‑balancing** – pool resources (e.g., unisex facilities) to spread demand, preventing a single bottleneck.  

## 4. Restroom case study
- Women’s queues > men’s because:
  - **Longer service time (P)** – handling babies, multiple purposes.  
  - **Higher utilization (U)** – near‑100 % when arrivals ≈ 6 / hour × 10 min ≈ 60 min.  
  - **Smaller capacity (M)** – fewer stalls/urinals.  

| Metric | Women | Men |
|--------|-------|-----|
| Avg. service time (P) | ~10 min | ~2 min |
| Arrival rate (λ) | ~6 / hr | ~6 / hr |
| Utilization (U) | ~100 % | ~20 % |
| Capacity (M) | Fewer stalls | More urinals |

> [!example] With 6 women/hr and 10 min service, utilization ≈ 1.0 → backlog; men with 2 min service stay at U≈0.2, queue stays short.  

- **Policy insight**: increase women’s stalls, add dedicated baby‑care rooms, or segment demand (e.g., separate “family” restroom) to lower P and raise M.  

## 5. Staffing & profit optimization
- **Goal**: meet target average wait (e.g., ≤ 1 min) while maximizing profit.  
- **Trial‑and‑error algorithm**  

1. Set N = 1 server.  
2. Compute utilization ρ = λ / (N·μ) where μ = 1 / service‑time.  
3. Use M/M/N formula to get average wait.  
4. If wait > target, increment N and repeat.  

> [!example] λ = 5 / hr, service time = 2 min → μ = 30 / hr.  
- N = 1 → ρ = 5/30 ≈ 0.17 → wait ≈ 0.12 min (meets 1 min).  
- Adjust N per time‑slot to align with demand bursts (e.g., 8‑9 am, 12 pm).  

- **Profit calculation**  
  - Revenue = (rate per minute) × (total minutes served).  
  - Cost = staff wage × N.  
  - Iterate N to balance profit vs service‑level target.  

- **Utilization reality check**  
  - Target wait of 1 min often forces utilization ≈ 3‑5 % → excessive labor cost.  
  - Aim for realistic utilization (10‑20 %) by smoothing demand (staggered schedules, external facilities).  

## 6. Operational considerations
- **Automation** reduces cost (C), delay (D), and processing effort (P) simultaneously.  
- **Pricing** can curb demand (lower CVP) but capacity upgrades (more lanes, unisex restrooms) usually yield larger queue reductions.  
- **Express lanes** isolate short‑transaction customers, cutting their processing time without hurting long‑transaction flow.  
- **Training cadence** (monthly, quarterly) improves service quality; core centers benefit most due to reusable “apartment” units.  
- **Agency problem** – misaligned incentives between organization (principal) and operational unit (agent) can hinder performance improvements.  

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| CV (Coefficient of Variation) | σ / μ; dimension‑less variability measure |
| Arrival rate (λ) | Number of customers per time unit; λ = 1 / A |
| Service rate (μ) | Customers served per time unit; μ = 1 / B |
| Utilization (U or ρ) | Fraction of server capacity used; λ · P / M |
| Kimmler’s formula | Queueing expression linking Q, M, P, utilization, availability |
| Physical queue | Actual line of customers |
| Psychological queue | Perceived waiting experience (e.g., ticket numbers) |
| Cooling / load‑balancing | Pooling resources to spread demand and avoid bottlenecks |
