> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- B’s capacity exceeds market demand, leaving 4,000 units spare for internal transfers.  
- A must purchase 3,000 units; its external purchase price caps the transfer price at $190.  
- B’s relevant cost (including opportunity cost) sets a floor of $140 for the transfer price.  
- Viable transfer price range: **$140 – $190** per unit.  
- In later scenarios (3B/3C) limited spare capacity raises the floor to $210 for part of the volume, yielding an overall TP ≈ $186.7.

## Scenario 3A – Basic transfer‑price set‑up

- **Division B**  
  - Manufacturing capacity: 8,000 units  
  - Market demand: 4,000 units → 4,000 units spare (available for internal sales)  
  - External selling price: **$200** per unit  

- **Division A**  
  - Manufacturing capacity: 3,000 units (full‑capacity)  
  - Market demand for finished goods: 5,000 units → needs **3,000 units** from B  

- **Internal demand**: A requires exactly the 3,000 units B can supply from its spare capacity.

> [!info] **Transfer price (TP)** – The price at which one division sells to another within the same firm.

> [!info] **Relevant cost (RC)** – The incremental cost to the selling division, including any opportunity cost of forgoing an external sale.

### Determining the price bounds

| Division | External price | Relevant cost (RC) | Transfer‑price condition |
|----------|----------------|--------------------|---------------------------|
| B (seller) | $200 | ≥ $140 (no opportunity cost) | **TP ≥ $140** |
| A (buyer)  | $190 | ≤ $190 (external purchase ceiling) | **TP ≤ $190** |

> [!warning] The transfer price must lie **between** the seller’s minimum RC and the buyer’s maximum external price; otherwise one division will prefer external trade.

## Exercise 3B – Limited spare capacity

- New spare capacity in B: **1,000 units** (instead of 4,000).  
- B must still meet internal demand of 3,000 units for A.  
- Because partial procurement is not allowed, B must transfer **all 3,000 units** to A.

### Cost breakdown

1. **First 1,000 units** (spare capacity) → RC = $140 per unit.  
2. **Remaining 2,000 units** (capacity that would be used for external sales) → RC = $210 per unit (opportunity cost of foregone external profit).  

- Total relevant cost for 3,000 units:  
  \[
  (1{,}000 \times 140) + (2{,}000 \times 210) = 140{,}000 + 420{,}000 = 560{,}000
  \]  
- **Average RC (and minimum TP)**:  
  \[
  560{,}000 \div 3{,}000 \approx \mathbf{186.7}
  \]

> [!example] **Resulting transfer price** ≈ **$186.7** per unit satisfies both divisions under the tighter capacity constraint.

## Exercise 3C – Same constraints, different wording

- Same numbers as 3B; the calculation method can be expressed as:
  - Separate the total relevant cost into “spare‑capacity” and “opportunity‑cost” portions, then average over total units.  
  - The outcome is identical: **TP ≈ $186.7**.

> [!warning] If the transfer price falls below $140, B would lose money on the 1,000 spare units; if it exceeds $190, A would prefer buying externally.

## General steps for transfer‑price analysis

1. **List capacities & market demand** for each division.  
2. **Identify internal demand** (how many units one division needs from another).  
3. **Gather external prices** for both selling and buying divisions.  
4. **Compute relevant costs**:  
   - For the seller: variable cost + opportunity cost of foregone external sales.  
   - For the buyer: external purchase price sets the ceiling.  
5. **Set the transfer‑price range**:  
   \[
   \text{RC}_{\text{seller}} \le \text{TP} \le \text{External price}_{\text{buyer}}
   \]  
6. **Choose a TP** within the range that aligns with corporate objectives (e.g., profit‑center evaluation, tax considerations).

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Transfer price (TP) | Internal price charged between divisions for goods/services. |
| Relevant cost (RC) | Incremental cost to the seller, including opportunity cost of external sales. |
| Opportunity cost | Revenue forgone by not selling externally. |
| Internal demand | Quantity one division requires from another within the firm. |
| External price | Market price at which a division could sell to or buy from outside the firm. |
