# Module 6 — Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) Analysis

> [[management-accounting/index|← Management Accounting]] · Previous: [[m05-cost-behaviour-estimation|Module 5]] · Next: [[m07-variable-vs-absorption-costing|Module 7 — Variable vs Absorption]]

**CVP = Cost · Volume · Profit.** The most practical tool in the whole course.

---

## Two income statement formats

The same month, the same profit, two different layouts — and the difference is *what they group by*.

| Traditional format | \$ | | Contribution format | \$ |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|
| Sales | 100,000 | | Sales | 100,000 |
| − Cost of goods sold | (70,000) | | − **Variable** expenses | (60,000) |
| **= Gross margin** | **30,000** | | **= Contribution margin** | **40,000** |
| − Selling & admin expenses | (20,000) | | − **Fixed** expenses | (30,000) |
| **= Net operating income** | **10,000** | | **= Net operating income** | **10,000** |

| | Traditional | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Groups costs by** | Function — production vs selling/admin ([[m02-cost-concepts-job-order-costing|the factory wall]]) | **Behaviour** — variable vs fixed ([[m05-cost-behaviour-estimation|Module 5]]) |
| **Used for** | **External reporting** | **Internal planning and decision making** |
| **Key subtotal** | Gross margin | **Contribution margin** |

> [!important] Why CVP needs the contribution format
> You cannot predict profit at a different volume from a traditional statement, because cost of goods sold
> mixes fixed and variable costs together. The contribution format separates them, which is exactly what
> makes the prediction possible. This is also why [[m07-variable-vs-absorption-costing|variable costing]]
> and CVP go together.

---

## The assumptions CVP quietly makes

Every answer below is only as good as these three:

1. **Selling price is constant** — the price does not change as volume changes (no bulk discounts, no price cuts to shift stock)
2. **Costs are linear and can be accurately split into variable and fixed** — variable cost constant *per unit*, fixed cost constant *in total*, across the entire [[m05-cost-behaviour-estimation|relevant range]]
3. **In multiproduct companies, the sales mix stays constant**

> [!warning] Where exam questions attack
> Almost every "why might this analysis be wrong?" question is aimed at one of these three. A volume large
> enough to need a discount breaks ①; a volume outside the relevant range breaks ②; selling more of the
> cheap product breaks ③.

## Contribution margin format

Running example throughout this note — **Racing Bicycle Company (RBC)**, June, 500 bicycles:

| Item | Total | Per unit | Percent |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales | \$250,000 | \$500 | 100% |
| Less: Variable Expenses | (\$150,000) | (\$300) | 60% |
| **Contribution Margin** | **\$100,000** | **\$200** | **40%** |
| Less: Fixed Expenses | (\$80,000) | – | – |
| **Net Operating Income** | **\$20,000** | – | – |

**Reading the per-unit column:** each additional bicycle sold generates another **\$200** of contribution
margin, so net operating income rises by \$200 — fixed expenses do not move. Sell one fewer and income
falls by \$200. That is the whole engine.

**What a 40% CM ratio means:** every extra \$100 of sales adds \$40 of contribution margin (and, once
fixed costs are covered, \$40 of profit).

---

## The CVP equation

Everything in this module is one equation, rearranged:

$$\text{Profit} = (\text{Sales} - \text{Variable expenses}) - \text{Fixed expenses}$$

Written per unit, with $P$ = price, $V$ = variable cost per unit, $Q$ = quantity:

$$\text{Profit} = (P \times Q - V \times Q) - \text{Fixed} = (P - V) \times Q - \text{Fixed}$$

$$\boxed{\text{Profit} = \text{Unit CM} \times Q - \text{Fixed expenses}}$$

**Check it at 401 bicycles:** Profit = \$200 × 401 − \$80,000 = **\$200**. One unit past break-even earns
exactly one unit's contribution margin.

There is a dollar-based twin of the same equation, used when you are given sales revenue rather than units:

$$\text{Contribution margin} = \text{Sales} \times \text{CM ratio} \qquad \text{Profit} = (\text{Sales} \times \text{CM ratio}) - \text{Fixed}$$

**Check it at 500 bicycles:** (500 × \$500 × 40%) − \$80,000 = \$100,000 − \$80,000 = **\$20,000** ✓

---

## The two ratios

| Ratio | Formula | RBC |
|---|---|---:|
| **CM ratio** | Contribution margin ÷ Sales (or unit CM ÷ price) | 100,000 ÷ 250,000 = **40%** |
| **Variable expense ratio** | Variable expenses ÷ Sales (or unit VC ÷ price) | 150,000 ÷ 250,000 = **60%** |

$$\text{CM ratio} = \frac{\text{Sales} - \text{Variable expenses}}{\text{Sales}} = 1 - \text{Variable expense ratio}$$

> [!tip] Free marks
> If a question gives you the variable expense ratio, you already have the CM ratio — subtract from 1.
> The two always sum to 100%.

**Using the CM ratio for a volume change.** If RBC lifts sales from 400 to 500 bikes, the extra
100 × \$500 = \$50,000 of sales brings 40% of that through to contribution margin:
100 × \$500 × 40% = **\$20,000** more CM, and — since fixed costs do not move — **\$20,000** more profit.

## Graphing it

### The CVP graph

**How to build it, in four steps:**

1. **Define and label the axes** — horizontal (X) = unit volume, vertical (Y) = dollars
2. **Draw fixed expenses** — a horizontal line at \$80,000, parallel to the volume axis
3. **Draw total expenses** — pick a volume (say 400 units), plot total cost there
   (\$80,000 + 400 × \$300 = \$200,000), and draw a line through it back to where the fixed expense line
   meets the dollar axis
4. **Draw sales** — pick the same volume, plot total sales (400 × \$500 = \$200,000), and draw a line
   through it back to **the origin** (at zero units there is zero revenue, but there is still \$80,000 of cost)

The two lines cross at the **break-even point**. Left of the crossing is the loss wedge; right of it is
the profit wedge, and the wedge widens at the rate of the unit CM.

| Units sold | 0 | 200 | 400 | 600 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales | – | \$100,000 | \$200,000 | \$300,000 |
| Total variable expenses | – | 60,000 | 120,000 | 180,000 |
| **Contribution margin** | – | **40,000** | **80,000** | **120,000** |
| Fixed expenses | 80,000 | 80,000 | 80,000 | 80,000 |
| **Net operating income (loss)** | **(80,000)** | **(40,000)** | **–** | **40,000** |

### The profit graph

A simpler form: plot **profit** itself against volume, so there is only one line instead of two.

$$\text{Profit} = 200Q - 80{,}000$$

It starts at **−\$80,000** at zero units (you owe the fixed costs before selling anything), climbs at
\$200 per unit, and **crosses zero at 400 units** — the break-even point read straight off the axis.

> [!tip] Which graph answers which question
> The **CVP graph** shows *where the money goes* (revenue vs cost, and the size of each gap).
> The **profit graph** shows *the bottom line only* — faster to read for break-even, useless for showing
> how much of the revenue is being eaten by variable cost.

## Break-even and target profit — three methods each

The same two questions, solved three ways. Pick whichever matches the data you were given.

### Break-even (Profit = 0)

**Formula method — units**

$$Q = \frac{\text{Fixed expenses}}{\text{Unit CM}} = \frac{80{,}000}{200} = 400 \text{ units}$$

**Formula method — dollars**

$$\text{Sales} = \frac{\text{Fixed expenses}}{\text{CM ratio}} = \frac{80{,}000}{0.40} = 200{,}000$$

**Percentage method**

$$\text{BE\%} = \frac{\text{Fixed expenses}}{\text{Total CM}} = \frac{80{,}000}{100{,}000} = 80\%$$

RBC needs **80% of its current sales** to break even — so 80% × \$250,000 = **\$200,000**, and
80% × 500 units = **400 units**. One percentage, both answers.

### Target profit

**Formula method — units and dollars.** Target profit of \$40,000:

$$Q = \frac{\text{Target profit} + \text{Fixed}}{\text{Unit CM}} = \frac{40{,}000 + 80{,}000}{200} = 600 \text{ units}$$

$$\text{Sales} = \frac{\text{Target profit} + \text{Fixed}}{\text{CM ratio}} = \frac{120{,}000}{0.40} = 300{,}000$$

**Percentage method**

$$\text{Target profit \%} = \frac{\text{Target profit} + \text{Fixed}}{\text{Total CM}} = \frac{120{,}000}{100{,}000} = 120\%$$

RBC needs **120% of current sales**: 120% × \$250,000 = **\$300,000**, and 120% × 500 = **600 units**.

> [!note] Why break-even is just target profit with the target set to zero
> Set Target profit = 0 in any of the three target-profit formulas and you get the matching break-even
> formula. They are not six things to memorise — they are two things, and one of them is a special case
> of the other.

### When there are taxes

Tax is charged on operating income, so a **net income** target must be grossed up *before* it enters any
formula above:

$$\text{Target Operating Income} = \frac{\text{Net Income}}{1 - \text{Tax Rate}}$$

> [!warning] A favourite exam trap
> For a **target net income of \$300,000 with a 25% tax rate**,
> Target Operating Income = \$300,000 ÷ 0.75 = **\$400,000**.
> That \$400,000 is what goes into the units formula. (Check: 400,000 × 0.75 = 300,000 ✓)

## Risk measures

### Margin of Safety

**The excess of budgeted or actual sales over break-even sales** — how much sales can drop before losses
are incurred. The higher it is, the lower the risk of falling into a loss.

| Form | Formula | RBC |
|---|---|---:|
| In dollars | Total sales − Break-even sales | 250,000 − 200,000 = **\$50,000** |
| In units | Total units − Break-even units | 500 − 400 = **100 units** |
| | *(or MoS dollars ÷ price)* | 50,000 ÷ 500 = 100 ✓ |
| As a percentage | MoS in dollars ÷ Total sales | 50,000 ÷ 250,000 = **20%** |

> [!important] MoS% and BE% are the same statement twice
> $$\text{MoS\%} = \frac{\text{Total sales} - \text{BE sales}}{\text{Total sales}} = 1 - \frac{\text{BE sales}}{\text{Total sales}} = 1 - \text{BE\%}$$
> So **BE% = 1 − MoS%**. RBC: 80% = 1 − 20% ✓. Compute either one and you have the other for free.

### Cost structure and profit stability

**Cost structure** is the relative proportion of fixed and variable costs — and managers often have real
latitude over it (lease vs buy, salaried staff vs contractors, in-house vs outsourced).

Two firms, identical price of \$100 per unit and identical profit at 10 units:

| | **High FC / Low VC** | | | **Low FC / High VC** | | |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Units | 5 | 10 | 20 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| Sales | 500 | 1,000 | 2,000 | 500 | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| Variable expenses | 100 | 200 | 400 | 300 | 600 | 1,200 |
| **Contribution margin** | **400** | **800** | **1,600** | **200** | **400** | **800** |
| Fixed expenses | 500 | 500 | 500 | 100 | 100 | 100 |
| **Net income** | **(100)** | **300** | **1,100** | **100** | **300** | **600** |

- Unit CM: **\$80** on the left (80% CM ratio) versus **\$40** on the right (40%)
- In a **good** year (20 units) the high-fixed-cost firm earns \$1,100 against \$600 — **higher**
- In a **bad** year (5 units) it loses \$100 while the other still earns \$100 — **lower**
- The low-fixed-cost firm enjoys **greater stability** of income across good and bad years

Neither structure is "better". High fixed cost buys upside and sells away safety; low fixed cost does the
reverse. The choice is a bet on how volatile your demand is.

### Operating Leverage

**A measure, at any given level of sales, of how a percentage change in sales volume will affect profit.**

$$\text{Degree of Operating Leverage (DOL)} = \frac{\text{Contribution margin}}{\text{Net operating income}} = \frac{100{,}000}{20{,}000} = 5$$

$$\% \Delta \text{Profit} = \% \Delta \text{Sales} \times \text{DOL}$$

**Proof at 500 units.** A 10% sales increase should give 10% × 5 = **50%** more profit:

| | Actual (500 units) | +10% (550 units) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales | \$250,000 | \$275,000 |
| Less: variable expenses | 150,000 | 165,000 |
| **Contribution margin** | **100,000** | **110,000** |
| Less: fixed expenses | 80,000 | 80,000 |
| **Net operating income** | **\$20,000** | **\$30,000** ← **+50%** ✓ |

Sales and variable expenses both rose 10%; fixed expenses did not move at all. That gap is the whole
source of the leverage.

> [!important] Two things people get wrong
> **① High leverage does not mean high profitability.** It means **high amplitude — more risk.**
> **② DOL is not a constant.** It is measured *at a given sales level*, and it falls as sales rise above
> break-even (the denominator grows faster than the numerator). At break-even, profit is zero and DOL is
> infinite; far above it, DOL approaches 1. Above, the high-fixed-cost firm has DOL = 800 ÷ 300 ≈ 2.7 at
> 10 units while the low-fixed-cost firm has 400 ÷ 300 ≈ 1.3 — the same fact as the table, in one number.

| | High fixed cost (e.g. a chip fab) | High variable cost (e.g. distribution, outsourced) |
|---|---|---|
| Leverage | High | Low |
| In a boom | Profit explodes | Profit rises gently |
| In a downturn | Risk of heavy losses | Rides it out |

## Multi-product CVP — the sales mix

**Sales mix is the relative proportion in which a company's products are sold.** Different products have
different prices, cost structures and CMs, so "the break-even quantity" becomes ambiguous — 400 of *what*?
CVP handles it by assuming the **mix stays constant** and treating the whole business as one blended product.

Suppose RBC sells bicycles **and** carts:

| | Bicycle | | Carts | | **Total** | |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Sales | \$250,000 | 100% | \$300,000 | 100% | **\$550,000** | 100.0% |
| Variable expenses | 150,000 | 60% | 135,000 | 45% | 285,000 | 51.8% |
| **Contribution margin** | **100,000** | **40%** | **165,000** | **55%** | **265,000** | **48.2%** |
| Fixed expenses | | | | | 170,000 | |
| **Net operating income** | | | | | **\$95,000** | |
| **Sales mix** | \$250,000 | **45%** | \$300,000 | **55%** | \$550,000 | 100% |

Note the overall CM ratio of **48.2%** is not the average of 40% and 55% — it is weighted by how much
revenue each product contributes.

**Method 1 — the break-even percentage.**

$$\text{BE\%} = \frac{170{,}000}{265{,}000} = 64.15\%$$

Break-even sales = 64.15% × \$550,000 = **\$352,830**. Because the mix is assumed constant, apply the same
64.15% to each product: bicycles 64.15% × \$250,000 = **\$160,375**, carts 64.15% × \$300,000 = **\$192,450**.

**Method 2 — the overall CM ratio.**

$$\text{Break-even sales} = \frac{\text{Fixed expenses}}{\text{Overall CM ratio}} = \frac{170{,}000}{0.482} = 352{,}697$$

Then split by the sales mix: 45% to bicycles, 55% to carts.

> [!note] Why the two answers differ by a few hundred dollars
> \$352,830 versus \$352,697 is **rounding**, not a mistake — 48.2% is a rounded CM ratio, and dividing by
> a rounded denominator amplifies the error. Carry more decimals and they converge. Exam answers will show
> a small "rounding error" line in the net operating income row for exactly this reason.

### Mix in dollars ≠ mix in units

To disaggregate a total back into products, you must use the **matching** mix:

| To split... | Use the sales mix in... |
|---|---|
| Total sales **dollars** | **dollars** — bicycles 45%, carts 55% |
| Total sales **quantity** | **units** — e.g. bicycles 2,000 of 4,000 = 50%, carts 50% |

A product can be 45% of revenue and 50% of volume at the same time; the cheaper product always takes a
larger share of the unit mix than of the dollar mix. Using the wrong one is a silent error — the arithmetic
works, the answer is wrong.

*(The unit figures above are the lecture deck's illustration of the principle and do not tie back to the
\$500 bicycle price; read them as showing that the two mixes differ, not as RBC's actual volumes.)*

> [!tip] The hidden trap
> **If the sales mix shifts, the break-even point moves even when total volume does not.**
> Sell more of the low-margin product and the overall CM ratio falls, so you need more sales just to stand
> still. This is why *what* you sell matters as much as *how much* — and it is assumption ③ above, the one
> CVP simply assumes away.

## Self-check

Coffee Klatch is an espresso stand. Average selling price **\$1.50** per cup, average variable expense
**\$0.30** per cup, average fixed expense **\$1,200** per month, average **2,000 cups** sold per month.

1. What is the CM ratio?
2. How many cups must be sold to attain a target profit of \$1,800 per month?
3. What are the break-even sales dollars?
4. What is the margin of safety in dollars?
5. What is the degree of operating leverage?

> [!success]- Answers
> Unit CM = 1.50 − 0.30 = **\$1.20**.
> 1. **0.8** — CM ratio = 1.20 ÷ 1.50 = 80% (or 1 − variable expense ratio of 20%).
> 2. **2,500 cups** — (1,800 + 1,200) ÷ 1.20 = 3,000 ÷ 1.20.
> 3. **\$1,500** — 1,200 ÷ 0.80. (In cups: 1,200 ÷ 1.20 = 1,000 cups.)
> 4. **\$1,500** — actual sales 2,000 × 1.50 = \$3,000, less break-even \$1,500. MoS% = 50%, so BE% = 50% ✓
> 5. **2.00** — CM = 2,000 × 1.20 = \$2,400; net income = 2,400 − 1,200 = \$1,200; DOL = 2,400 ÷ 1,200.
