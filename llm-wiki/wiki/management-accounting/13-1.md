> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- Rank products by **contribution per constrained resource unit**; allocate the scarce resource to the highest rank.  
- Transfer‑price negotiation hinges on **seller’s minimum (opportunity cost)** and **buyer’s maximum (external cost)**; internal transfer occurs only if the intervals overlap.  
- Relevant costs are **avoidable** costs; ignore sunk or allocated fixed costs.  
- Relieve bottlenecks by subcontracting, adding capacity, or redesigning the process.  
- Mis‑aligned transfer prices can turn a firm‑wide optimal decision into a sub‑optimal one.

## 1. Capacity‑constrained product selection
- **Step‑by‑step method**  
  1. Identify the constrained resource (machine minutes, labor hours, inventory parts).  
  2. Compute each product’s contribution margin (CM) per unit.  
  3. Determine resource units required per product unit.  
  4. Calculate **CM per resource unit** = CM ÷ resource use.  
  5. Rank products; allocate capacity from highest to lowest until exhausted or demand met.  

> [!example] Tutoring example  
- 6 h available.  
- Company A: $20 for 2 h → $10 / h.  
- Company B: $24 for 3 h → $8 / h.  
- Prioritize Company A (higher $/h) to maximize earnings.

> [!example] Production‑capacity example  
| Product | CM/unit | Minutes/unit | CM/min |
|---------|--------|--------------|--------|
| 1       | $24    | 1            | $24    |
| 2       | $15    | 0.5          | $30    |

- Produce Product 2 first (higher $/min), then use leftovers for Product 1.

- **Practical steps** (summary)  
  1. Identify bottleneck.  
  2. Compute CM per bottleneck unit.  
  3. Satisfy demand of the top‑ranked product.  
  4. Allocate remaining capacity to the next product.

- **Inventory‑parts illustration** – parts whose demand exceeds on‑hand inventory become functional constraints; order more or adjust production.

- **Bottleneck‑relief options**  
  • Sub‑contract the constrained operation.  
  • Add machines or labor.  
  • Redesign the process to move/eliminate the bottleneck.

## 2. Transfer pricing fundamentals
> [!info] Transfer price – the internal price at which one division sells a part or service to another division within the same firm.

### Common methods
| Method | Basis |
|--------|-------|
| Market‑based | External market price |
| Negotiated | Mutual agreement between divisions |
| Cost‑based | Production cost ± markup (full or variable) |

> [!warning] Cost‑based pitfalls – using full cost adds irrelevant fixed costs; cost‑plus markup removes the seller’s incentive to control costs.

### Decision‑making principles
- Compare **relevant (avoidable) costs** only.  
- High internal prices favor the selling division; low prices favor the buying division.  
- No single method fits all; choose based on strategy, cost structure, and market conditions.

> [!example] Company Z internal vs. external sourcing  
- Division X unit cost = $6 (relevant).  
- External market price = $50.  
- Division Y can:  
  1. Buy externally for $12 + $2 shipping = $14 total.  
  2. Buy internally at TP = $X, add $10 processing, sell for $30.  
- Y chooses the lower total cost; X accepts only if TP ≥ its opportunity cost.

### Capacity extremes for the selling division (X)
- **No excess capacity** – X must forgo internal sales if TP < external price.  
- **Excess capacity** – X can sell internally as long as TP > incremental cost.

> [!info] Seller’s minimum price = opportunity cost (often the external market price).  
> [!info] Buyer’s maximum price = cheaper of external purchase price or total cost of internal purchase (including downstream processing).

### Threshold tables
| Division | Situation | Minimum TP (seller) | Maximum TP (buyer) |
|----------|-----------|---------------------|--------------------|
| X (no excess) | Must sell externally | $15 (external price) | – |
| X (excess) | Only internal demand | $6 (incremental cost) | – |
| Y (can buy externally) | Choose cheapest source | – | $12 (external price) |
| Y (only internal) | Must cover processing | – | $20 (30‑10) |

### Negotiation interval procedure
1. Determine each division’s threshold (seller min, buyer max).  
2. Check interval overlap.  
3. If overlap → internal transfer feasible; negotiate any price within the common range.  
4. If no overlap → each division trades externally.

> [!example] Overlap cases  
- **Case 1:** X excess (≥ $6) & Y can buy externally (≤ $12) → feasible range **$6–$12**.  
- **Case 2:** X no excess (≥ $15) & Y only internal (≤ $20) → feasible range **$15–$20**.  

### Firm‑wide perspective
- Even if internal transfer lowers total cost (e.g., $6 vs. $12), a corporate‑set TP of $15 blocks the transfer, leading to a sub‑optimal outcome.

## 3. Relevant cost considerations
> [!info] Relevant cost – any cost that changes with the decision; excludes sunk or unavoidable fixed costs.

- **Variable production cost** (e.g., $6) is relevant.  
- **Allocated fixed cost** (e.g., $1 per unit) is irrelevant; the relevant cost becomes $5.  
- **Additional variable selling expenses** affect CM and should be added to external cost but are avoided for internal transfers.  

| Cost element | External purchase | Internal purchase |
|--------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Unit price   | $12               | $6                |
| Shipping     | $2                | $0                |
| **Total**    | **$14**           | **$6**            |

> [!warning] Using full cost (including fixed) in cost‑based transfer pricing can inflate the internal price and discourage beneficial internal trade.

## 4. Practical tips & power dynamics
- Sketch a simple two‑division flow diagram (costs, prices, profits) before analysis.  
- Divisions with bargaining power may push for higher TP to boost their profit; corporate leadership may cap TP to protect overall cost efficiency.  
- Recent regulatory trends (e.g., global minimum tax) can influence transfer‑price strategies, especially for multinational firms.

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Constraint | Any limited resource that caps production (machine time, labor, inventory). |
| Contribution margin per resource unit | CM ÷ amount of constrained resource used; basis for ranking products. |
| Transfer price | Internal price for inter‑division sales of goods/services. |
| Relevant cost | Cost that varies with the decision; excludes sunk or unavoidable fixed costs. |
| Opportunity cost | The benefit foregone by not using a resource in its next best alternative. |
| Negotiation interval | Overlap between seller’s minimum and buyer’s maximum acceptable transfer price. |
