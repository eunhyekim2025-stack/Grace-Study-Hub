# Module 1 — Introduction to Management Accounting

> [[management-accounting/index|← Management Accounting]] · Next: [[m02-cost-concepts-job-order-costing|Module 2 — Cost Concepts & Job Order Costing]]

There are two kinds of accounting. The names sound similar, but they split cleanly on one question:
**who is the number for?**

---

## Comparison table (the most examinable form)

| Feature | Financial Accounting | Managerial Accounting |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary user** | Outsiders — investors, lenders, government | Insiders — managers |
| **Type of decision** | Decisions *about* the company (buy/sell shares) | Decisions *for* the company (staffing, product placement) |
| **Scope** | Whole company (the big picture) | Segmented (a specific store, iPad Pro sales) |
| **Data example** | Apple's $98B profit, $3.55 EPS | Weekly iPad Pro sales in the Vancouver store |
| **Rules / regulation** | Strict — GAAP, IFRS, SEC | Loose / optional — whatever management needs |
| **Timing** | Historical, past focus | Future, present **and** past |
| **Reporting speed** | Delayed, slow (audited results) | Immediate, fast |

---

## Why "no rules" is a feature, not a flaw

Managerial accounting is not governed by external regulation. That sounds sloppy — it is actually the point.

- External rules exist to make **all companies comparable**, at the cost of ignoring any one firm's situation.
- A manager does not need comparability. A manager needs to **decide**. So the report is built in whatever
  shape and at whatever speed the decision requires.
- The result: managerial reports are produced **frequently, quickly, and at a fine level of detail**.

> [!tip] How to hold this course in your head
> Modules 1–12 are a toolkit for three questions:
> **① What did this cost to make? (1–4) → ② How many must we sell to break even? (5–7) → ③ Did it go to plan? (8–12)**
