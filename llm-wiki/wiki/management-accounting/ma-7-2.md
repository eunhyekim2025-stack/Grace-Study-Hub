> [!summary] Key takeaways
- ABC allocates overhead by activities → more accurate product costs than plant‑wide rates.  
- Traditional costing over‑costs short‑run products, under‑costs long‑run ones.  
- ABC supports strategic decisions: value‑add identification, product‑line choices, KPI alignment.  
- Implementation follows five steps: define activities, select drivers, calculate cost pools, compute rates, assign to products.  
- Success hinges on cross‑functional teams, quality data, and senior‑level sponsorship.  

## Comparison of ABC and Traditional Costing
- **Traditional**: single plant‑wide overhead rate (e.g., $/machine hour).  
- **ABC**: multiple activity cost pools, each with its own driver.  
- Result: Traditional **over‑costs** short‑run products, **under‑costs** long‑run; ABC reveals true margins.  

> [!example]  
**Baxter example** – Overhead $14 M, 800,000 MH → $17.5 per MH. Applying the plant rate misstates product profitability; ABC reallocates costs to activities for correct margins.  

## Strategic Implications
- Identifies value‑adding vs non‑value‑adding activities → targets cost‑reduction.  
- Informs go/no‑go decisions on product lines based on true activity costs.  
- Requires aligning KPIs, compensation, and performance metrics with ABC‑derived margins.  

> [!warning]  
Management may resist ABC because numbers differ from legacy reports; they often prefer a “one‑stop‑shop” system.  

## Implementation Considerations
- **Data intensity**: extensive data collection, ongoing maintenance of cost pools.  
- **Cross‑functional team**: production, distribution, purchasing, finance, IT must define activities and drivers.  
- **Leadership**: senior sponsorship drives adoption across units.  

## Typical ABC Implementation Steps
| Step | Action | Key Point |
|------|--------|-----------|
| 1 | Define activities (order processing, setup, service, sustaining) | Capture all work that consumes resources |
| 2 | Select cost drivers (machine hours, orders, customers) | Choose measurable, causal drivers |
| 3 | Calculate activity cost pools (e.g., 60 % manufacturing, 10 % non‑manufacturing of total overhead) | Allocate total overhead proportionally |
| 4 | Compute activity rates (cost pool ÷ total driver quantity) | Rate = $ per driver unit |
| 5 | Assign costs to products using driver quantities; sum for product cost and margin | Derive accurate profitability |

> [!example]  
**Numeric illustration** – Total overhead 13,300; manufacturing 60 % = 7,980; non‑manufacturing 10 % = 1,330; driver base 50 units → $186.2 per unit. Multiply by units sold, subtract fixed deductions (e.g., $10,000) → product margin.  

## Exercise Guidance
- List all relevant activities and their drivers.  
- Allocate total overhead to each activity using percentage splits.  
- Compute activity rates and apply to product driver quantities for accurate cost.  

> [!info]  
**Key takeaway** – ABC provides precise product profitability, guides strategy, but demands accurate data, cross‑functional collaboration, and strong leadership support.  

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| ABC (Activity‑Based Costing) | Costing method that assigns overhead to activities and then to products via cost drivers |
| Traditional costing | Single plant‑wide overhead rate, often based on a volume measure |
| Cost driver | Measure that causes a cost to be incurred (e.g., machine hours, orders) |
| Activity cost pool | Group of overhead costs associated with a specific activity |
| Top‑level sponsorship | Senior leadership endorsement critical for ABC rollout |
| Value‑adding activity | Activity that increases product worth from the customer’s perspective |
