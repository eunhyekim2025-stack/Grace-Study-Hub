> [!summary] Key takeaways  
- Deductive arguments cannot add new factual knowledge; inductive reasoning is needed for that.  
- A **statistical syllogism** infers a property for an individual from its prevalence in a reference class.  
- An **inductive generalization** projects a sample’s proportion onto the entire population, but only when the sample is large, random, and representative.  
- The **reference‑class problem** and **sampling objections** (size, bias) are the main ways to weaken inductive arguments.  
- Larger samples reduce standard error; for huge populations the absolute sample size matters more than the proportion sampled.

## 1. Deductive reasoning – why it stops short
- **Deductive validity**: truth of premises guarantees truth of conclusion, but only reproduces what is already encoded in the premises.  
- **Limitation**: cannot generate new factual knowledge about the world.  
- **Warm‑up example**: “Dolphins have moral rights” → reconstruct premises, assign letters (e.g., A, B, …, M), test if **M** follows via propositional rules (Modus Ponens, etc.).  
- **Decluttering method**  
  1. Replace each atomic statement with a single letter.  
  2. Rewrite “unless” / “only‑if” as standard conditionals.  
  3. Build a proof tree to see if the conclusion letter follows.  

> [!info] Logical equivalences  
| Form | Equivalent |
|------|------------|
| If P → Q | If ¬Q → ¬P (Transposition) |
| A only if T | If A → T (or ¬T → ¬A) |
| P ↔ ¬¬P | Double‑negation |

## 2. Statistical syllogism – from population to individual
> [!info] Definition  
A **statistical syllogism** infers that a particular member likely has a property that is common in its reference class.

- **Structure**  

| Step | Content |
|------|---------|
| 1 | Identify property **G** with prevalence **n %** in population **P**. |
| 2 | Identify a specific member **x** of **P**. |
| 3 | Conclude **x** probably has **G**. |

- **Strength factor**: larger **n** → stronger inference.  
- **Cogency**: if premises are true and inference strong, the argument is *cogent* (highly likely conclusion).  

> [!example]  
Population: Singaporeans; Property: homeownership (≈95%); Member: speaker → Speaker likely owns a home.

- **Reference‑class problem**: an individual belongs to multiple classes with different **n** values (e.g., “students” vs “engineers”). Choose the class that best captures the relevant property; otherwise the inference is unreliable.  

> [!warning] Ignoring alternative reference classes can produce contradictory or weak conclusions.

## 3. Inductive generalization – from sample to population
> [!info] Definition  
An **inductive generalization** projects a property observed in a sample onto the whole population.

- **Structure**  

| Step | Content |
|------|---------|
| 1 | Identify **population** (e.g., all Singaporeans). |
| 2 | Identify **property** (e.g., support for PAP). |
| 3 | State **sample frequency** (e.g., 70 %). |
| 4 | Infer the same frequency for the entire population **iff** the sample is unbiased & sufficiently large. |

- **Key objections**  
  - *Sample‑size*: too few observations → high uncertainty.  
  - *Sampling‑bias*: non‑random selection skews results.  
  - *Non‑sampling*: external factors unrelated to sampling (e.g., upcoming policy change).  

- **Sample‑size guideline**  

> [!info] Standard error (SE) ≈ σ / √N  
> To halve SE, quadruple N.

- **Law of Large Numbers**: as N → ∞, sample mean → population mean (IID assumption).  

- **Finite Population Correction (FPC)**  

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| N | Population size |
| n | Sample size |
| FPC factor | √[(N − n)/(N − 1)] (multiplies SE) |

- **Population size effect**  
  - *Large N*: proportion sampled is negligible; a sample of 100 can estimate a city of 5 k or a country of 5 M.  
  - *Small N*: need a sizable fraction (≈10 % of 200) for comparable accuracy.  

> [!example]  
PPP survey: 91.8 % of respondents satisfied with MRT. Valid generalization → “91.8 % of all MRT users in Singapore are satisfied,” provided the sample is representative.

## 4. Comparing syllogism vs generalization
| Feature | Statistical syllogism | Inductive generalization |
|---------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Direction | Population → Individual | Sample → Whole population |
| Basis | Known prevalence **n %** in whole class | Observed proportion **p %** in sample |
| Main threat | Wrong reference class | Small or biased sample |

## 5. Evaluating any statistical argument
1. Identify **population**, **sample** (if any), **property**, **member**.  
2. Verify truth of premises (prevalence, sample representativeness).  
3. Check for **reference‑class attacks** or **sampling objections**.  

## Key terms
| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| Deductive validity | Logical guarantee that conclusion follows from premises |
| Statistical syllogism | Inference from population frequency to a specific individual |
| Inductive generalization | Inference from sample frequency to the entire population |
| Cogent argument | Strong inductive argument with true premises |
| Reference‑class problem | Ambiguity in choosing the appropriate class for a statistical syllogism |
| Standard error (SE) | Measure of sampling variability, decreases with √N |
| Law of Large Numbers | Sample statistics converge to population parameters as N grows |
| Finite Population Correction (FPC) | Adjustment to SE when sampling a sizable fraction of a finite population |
